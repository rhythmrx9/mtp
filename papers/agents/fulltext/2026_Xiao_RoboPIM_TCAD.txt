Abstract—Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated
significant potential in improving robotics applications through
natural language processing capabilities. Matrix multiplication
dominates the computational workload in these LLM-based
robotics systems, and resistive random access memory (ReRAM)
provides notable performance benefits due to its inherent
parallelism and energy efficiency. However, matrix multiplications in these applications pose dual heterogeneity challenges,
encompassing both structural and scale heterogeneity. Structurally, these applications involve both standard dense matrix
operations and block-structured sparse multiplications, where
sparsity patterns vary significantly with robot topology. In terms
of scale, matrix sizes range from just a few to thousands
of dimensions, making large ReRAM crossbars designed for
LLM inefficient for small-scale robotics computations. Existing
acceleration approaches fail to address this dual heterogeneity,
limiting their effectiveness for LLM-based robotics applications.
In this work, we propose robotics applications-processing in
memory (RoboPIM), the first low-energy ReRAM-based accelerator tailored for LLM-based robotics applications. robotics
applications-processing in memory (RoboPIM) adapts to the
diverse matrix multiplication requirements across robot platforms and LLM configurations. Our design features a ReRAM
architecture with various crossbar sizes and implements a
dynamic task slicing scheme that allocates matrix slices to
appropriately sized crossbars. To maximize ReRAM’s parallel processing capabilities, we develop a two-stage scheduling

Despite the promising results of these works, they only focus
on accelerating either LLMs or robotics applications, which
does not consider to accelerate LLM-based robotics applications in a uniform manner.
In LLM-based robotics applications, the dominant operations are standard or block-diagonal matrix multiplications,
which account for the majority of computational time and
energy consumption [10], [11], [13]. Thus, a natural insight
is: can we design an integrated architecture adapting to various matrix multiplications to accelerate LLM-based robotics
applications? Recent works [16], [17], [18] have demonstrated
that resistive random access memory (ReRAM) is highly
efficient on accelerating matrix–vector multiplication (MVM).
Besides, it offers low energy consumption, high storage density, and powerful parallel capabilities [19]. These features
make ReRAM particularly well-suited for LLM-based robotics
applications. However, since robotics applications and LLMs
involve diverse matrix multiplication operations, it is difficult
to design a ReRAM-based accelerator for LLM-based robotics
applications, due to the following dual heterogeneity.
1) Structural Heterogeneity: In addition to normal matrix
multiplication, sparsity in robotics applications exhibits
a block-diagonal pattern and varies significantly with
robot topology. This sparsity stems from the inherent
correlations and independencies within robot structure.
Failing to leverage this sparsity leads to unnecessary
calculations and inefficiencies.
2) Scale Heterogeneity: Matrix sizes vary dramatically,
from the small matrices in robot dynamics (tens of
dimensions) to the large-scale matrices in LLMs (thou-

sands of dimensions). When the small-scale robotic tasks
directly execute on the large crossbars built for LLMs,
it leads to severe hardware underutilization and energy
inefficiency. Hence, considering this dual heterogeneity
is crucial for enhancing processing efficiency in an
integrated ReRAM-based architecture.
To this end, we propose the first ReRAM-based accelerator
for LLM-based robotics applications, robotics applicationsprocessing in memory (RoboPIM). It is a novel architecture
that considers dual heterogeneity of matrix multiplications
in LLM-based robotics applications. First, we develop a
ReRAM-based integrated architecture with different sized
crossbars to support various scales of matrix multiplication. Second, we design a dynamic task slicing scheme to
enable uniform matrix processing on ReRAM in a hardwareadapted manner. Specifically, to fully exploit the matrix
sparsity that varies with robot specifications, we transform
block-diagonal sparse matrix multiplication into multiple
dense submatrix multiplications by a sparsity to density
task slicing (S2D-Slicing). To effectively coordinate such
dense matrix multiplication of varying sizes with hardware
specifications, a density to crossbar-adapted task slicing
(D2C-Slicing) is further proposed, which partitions the standard matrix multiplication task into several subtasks with
crossbar-adapted size, improving the efficiency of matrix processing. Third, based on the dynamic task slicing regime, we
introduce a two-stage task scheduling strategy to fully exploit
the high parallelism capabilities of the integrated architecture
and enhance hardware utilization. Our main contributions of
this article are as follows.
1) We develop the first integrated ReRAM-based architecture, including crossbars of different sizes for LLMbased robotics applications, enabling efficient matrix
multiplication processing.
2) We devise a dynamic task slicing scheme to handle
diverse sparsity patterns and wide-ranging matrix scales,
benefiting both LLM-integrated and traditional robotics.
3) We propose a two-stage scheduling strategy to fully
exploit the high parallelism capabilities of the integrated
ReRAM-based architecture, enhancing the hardware
utilization.
4) We evaluate our accelerator with a wide range of robots
and LLMs. Experimental results show that RoboPIM
achieves 2.85× and 6.85× speedup across diverse
robotic platforms and LLMs, respectively, outperforming
state-of-the-art domain-specific solutions.

B. ReRAM-Based PIM
PIM [23], [24], [25], [26], [27] represents an innovative
computing paradigm that integrates computational capabilities
directly into memory cells, effectively addressing the data
movement bottleneck in conventional architectures. Among
various PIM implementations, ReRAM stands out as a particularly promising memory technology due to its high storage
density and excellent energy efficiency. The fundamental
structure of a ReRAM cell comprises a metal oxide layer
sandwiched between electrodes. This structure allows binary
data representation through distinct resistance states, where
high and low resistance levels correspond to logic “0” and
“1,” respectively.
The main advantage of ReRAM appears in its crossbar
configuration, which enables efficient MVM. In this implementation, matrix elements are mapped as conductance values
(G) within the crossbar array, while input vectors are transformed into analog voltages (V) through digital-to-analog
conversion. Following Kirchhoff’s

This in situ, parallel, and analog computation significantly
enhances both computational throughput and energy efficiency
for matrix operations that are fundamental to many modern
computing workloads.
C. Motivation
Before addressing specific design challenges, we justify the
choice of ReRAM over other hardware candidates. FPGAs and
ASICs suffer from high data movement [28] during memoryintensive LLM inference, and static/dynamic random access
memory (SRAM) PIMs [29], [30] are limited by low density
and significant leakage/refresh power, which are prohibitive for
energy-constrained robotics. Consequently, ReRAM offers a
superior alternative. It uniquely combines high storage density,
nonvolatility, and in situ computing efficiency, making it the
optimal substrate to satisfy the dual demands of memoryintensive LLM inference and energy-constrained robotics.
To fully leverage the potential of ReRAM in this context, it
is crucial to align the hardware architecture with the specific
computational patterns. As described in Section II-A, matrix
multiplications are the dominant operations in LLM-based
robotics applications. We investigate the unique characteristics
of these operations, reveal the inefficiencies in handling them,
and highlight the need for a new design to address these
challenges.

III. ROBO PIM A RCHITECTURE
In this section, we provide a detailed description of the
RoboPIM architecture. Specifically, we present key components of the architecture, including heterogeneous crossbars
of different sizes. We then describe the workflow of RoboPIM
from a high-level perspective.
A. Architecture
Fig. 5(a) presents the hardware architecture of RoboPIM,
an integrated ReRAM-based design that can be embedded in
CPUs, GPUs, or FPGA-based accelerators. Matrix multiplications arising from LLM inference and robotics applications,
ranging from a few to thousands of dimensions, are efficiently
offloaded to the RoboPIM chip and processed in a uniform
manner. Meanwhile, an external processor is employed to
handle nonmatrix multiplication operations such as power
and cross product calculations. A RoboPIM chip consists of
external I/O interface that is capable of interacting with offchip memory through a memory controller. We introduce each
component of RoboPIM as follows.
1) Off-Chip Memory: Off-chip memory includes memory
systems such as DRAM that provides a memory access
interface. It provides the instructions and original data
of matrix multiplication to the RoboPIM chip. Similarly,
the results of matrix multiplication tasks generated by
the RoboPIM chip can be batch written back to the offchip memory.
2) Process Element (PE): The detail of PE is illustrated
in Fig. 5(b). The PE comprises task slicing units, the
scheduler, and the compute units (CUs). It receives
instructions and matrix data from the I/O interface.

Instructions for SpMM are dispatched to the sparse task
slicing unit (STSU), while instructions for dense–dense
matrix multiplication (DeMM) are routed to the dense
task slicing unit (DTSU). Following task slicing, the
scheduler assigns sliced tasks to available CUs. A CU
consists of a dispatcher, several data buffers, and a
hybrid-sized crossbar array, which is responsible for the
efficient execution of matrix multiplication. Matrix data
are transferred directly from off-chip memory to the CU
via the I/O interface, and the computed matrix blocks are
asynchronously written back to the off-chip memory.
3) STSU: The STSU transforms an SpMM task into several DeMM subtasks through S2D-Slicing described in
Section IV-A. As shown in Fig. 5(f), the STSU contains
three key components: an SpMM instruction queue and
a decoder that receive and parse SpMM instructions, a
sparse task queue that caches decoded SpMM tasks,
and an S2D-Slicing unit that partitions SpMM tasks
into DeMM subtasks. These subtasks are stored in a
dense task buffer, encoded into DeMM instructions, and
dispatched to DTSU for further slicing.
4) DTSU: The DTSU converts DeMM tasks into crossbaradapted subtasks through D2C-Slicing detailed in Section IV-B. As depicted in Fig. 5(d), the DTSU parses
DeMM instructions through its dedicated instruction
queue and decoder. The decoded tasks, along with
their associated metadata (e.g., matrix base addresses
and matrix dimensions), are stored in a dense task
buffer. Subsequently, a D2C-Slicing unit slices them
into smaller subtasks to align with the specifications of
the ReRAM crossbar. These generated crossbar-adapted
subtasks are ultimately dispatched to the scheduler for
execution.
5) Scheduler: As depicted in Fig. 5(e), the scheduler
orchestrates task scheduling through crossbar calculator,
dynamic scheduling engine, and CU instruction generator. The crossbar calculator determines crossbar demand
for each task according to matrix dimensions and crossbar sizes. The dynamic scheduling engine maintains
a max heap for each size of crossbar to track the
number of idle crossbars in each CU while executing the
scheduling algorithm described in Section IV-C to map
tasks to the optimal CU. The CU instruction generator
converts scheduling decisions into hardware-executable
commands. This architecture enables efficient crossbar
management and real-time task allocation.
6) CU: A CU is responsible for performing assigned matrix
multiplication tasks as shown in Fig. 5(c), it includes
peripheral circuits, multiple crossbars of different sizes,
on-chip buffers, and a dispatcher. Peripheral circuits
such as DAC support the crossbars to perform MVM
operations. Multiple crossbars of different sizes are
responsible for scale-adaptive task execution, where
tasks with significant scale disparities are dynamically
mapped to appropriate-sized crossbar. This inclusion of
a smaller crossbar size is a direct architectural feature
to efficiently support the small-scale matrix operations
unique to robot dynamics, mitigating the energy over-

IV. DYNAMIC TASK S LICING AND S CHEDULING
In this section, we introduce the dynamic task slicing
scheme, a key strategy designed to enhance the efficiency
of matrix multiplications in RoboPIM. This scheme aims
to address the challenges posed by block-structured sparsity
with slicing sparse matrices into smaller dense submatrices.
Furthermore, we describe how the slicing process is further
optimized by dynamically adjusting the slicing granularity
based on the crossbar sizes. Subsequently, we present the
dynamic scheduling method that assigns these sliced tasks
to crossbars to maximize hardware utilization. Collectively,
these techniques enable RoboPIM to handle both dense and
block-structured sparse matrix multiplications with flexibility
and efficiency, achieving improved performance and energy
efficiency.

slicing stage and dynamic scheduling stage.

exploitation, while dense matrix multiplication tasks are allocated to DTSU for hardware-adapted partitioning. Moreover,

An oversized matrix cannot be directly mapped to the
ReRAM crossbar. Thus, it is essential to find a general
method for slicing matrix multiplication ranging from a few
to thousands of dimension into several subtasks with crossbaradapted size, thereby enabling uniform processing standard
matrix multiplication on RoboPIM.
In particular, there are two ways to slice the oversized tasks:
horizontal slicing by rows and vertical slicing by columns. The
horizontal and vertical slicing methods we define are for RHS
matrices, since RHS matrices are mapped to crossbars, while
LHS matrices are split into corresponding vectors as inputs.
Take matrix multiplication between inverse of the mass matrix
M1 and torque gradient matrix M2 for an example. As shown
in Fig. 7(a), there is a matrix multiplication task between LHS
matrix M1 and RHS matrix M2 . If M2 is oversized, it can
be sliced into two blocks. Fig. 7(b) shows the computational
flow of horizontal slicing by rows. When we slice a matrix
multiplication by rows, two vertical blocks of M1 multiply two
horizontal blocks of M2 . Due to the inherent rules of matrix
multiplication, the results of each subtask need to add together
by element to obtain the final result. Thus, additional adders
are needed, and the dependencies among subtasks introduce
extra synchronization overhead. In contrast, Fig. 7(c) illustrates
the computational flow of vertical slicing by columns. The
whole matrix M1 needs to multiply two vertical blocks of
M2 . Finally, the results of the two subtasks are concatenated
to produce the overall result. Since the two subtasks are
completely independent, this approach preserves the potential
for parallelism. Therefore, we select column-wise slicing as
our basic partition method.

C. Dynamic Task Scheduling
After the processing of S2D-Slicing and D2C-Slicing, the
RHS matrix width of each matrix multiplication task does not
exceed the column size of the crossbar. Thus, the required
number of crossbars for each task can be simply determined by
the row size of the RHS matrix. These tasks will be scheduled
to crossbars of corresponding size. However, an unbalanced
workload may still lead to underutilization of a specific size
of crossbar. To address this issue, we design a dynamic
scheduling strategy to further improve the hardware utilization. Moreover, different tasks may occupy varying numbers
of crossbars with differing durations, necessitating real-time
crossbar management. Therefore, we introduce multiple max
heaps, each dedicated to a different crossbar size, to track the
number of idle crossbars in each CU, facilitating uniform management of ReRAM crossbars and enabling efficient dynamic
scheduling.
The scheduling algorithm is divided into two stages, as
summarized in Algorithm 2. First, in the initialization stage,
sliced matrix multiplication tasks are assigned to two task
queues: T 1 is for larger task and T 2 is for smaller. The numbers
of idle crossbars with two different sizes in each CU compose
two max heaps H1 and H2 . Besides, two overall idle crossbar
numbers N1 and N2 are also maintained to determine whether a
task can be scheduled. If the crossbars required by a task in T 1
do not exceed N1 , this task is offloaded to the CU at the root of
the heap, and H1 is adjusted accordingly. Tasks requiring more
crossbars may be iteratively scheduled until the necessary
resources are allocated. The initialization stage will continue
until there are not enough idle crossbars to accommodate the
task. Subsequently, during the dynamic scheduling stage, upon
completion of a task in T 1 , the scheduler checks if new tasks
in T 1 can be scheduled based on the needed crossbar number
and overall idle crossbar number. All scheduling logic of T 1
is applied to T 2 . Moreover, if T 2 is empty and T 1 is not, the
idle smaller crossbars will work in a collaborative mode. For
instance, if the large crossbar size is 128 × 128 and the small

matrices like spatial inertia (Ii ∈ R6×6 ) and motion subspace

for simultaneous execution. This approach fully leverages
the ReRAM’s inherent parallelism, leading to a significant
reduction in the latency of each recursive step.

VII. C ONCLUSION
In this article, we present RoboPIM, the first integrated
ReRAM-based architecture designed for LLM-based robotics
applications. RoboPIM addresses the challenges of structural and scale heterogeneity in matrix multiplication on
ReRAM, achieving significant improvements in both performance and energy efficiency. To fully adapt to these two
features for LLM-based robotics applications, at the hardware
level, RoboPIM incorporates heterogeneous crossbars of different sizes and a dynamic scheduling strategy to support
uniform processing of matrix multiplication, enhancing the
hardware utilization and parallelism. At the software level,
RoboPIM develops two types of task slicing, S2D-Slicing and
D2C-Slicing. The S2D-Slicing transforms block-structured
sparse matrix multiplication into multiple dense submatrix
multiplication, allowing for adaptive exploitation of varying sparsity. Meanwhile, the D2C-Slicing partitions dense
matrix multiplication across varying scales (from a few to
thousands of dimensions) into crossbar-adapted sizes for
hardware-efficient execution on the ReRAM-based architecture. Extensive evaluation demonstrates that RoboPIM
outperforms CPU, GPU, and state-of-the-art domain-specific
accelerators in both performance and energy efficiency.

A. Robotics Application Accelerator
Robotics applications demand real-time, energy-efficient
processing. General-purpose approaches like [47] leverage
GPU parallelism for motion planning, while Tartan [12]
provides CPU architectural support, but these fail to meet
energy efficiency requirements for resource-constrained systems. Domain-specific accelerators [15], [48] leverage spatial
locality and sparsity for motion planning, demonstrating
improved efficiency but are limited by fixed block sizes for
sparsity and narrow support for varying matrix scales. Prior
dynamic partitioning techniques for ReRAM [49], [50] focus
almost exclusively on single-scale, dense, or unstructuredsparse workloads typical of deep learning, failing to address
the dual heterogeneity of integrated LLM-robotics systems.
RoboPIM addresses this gap with a two-stage dynamic
task slicing (S2D-Slicing and D2C-Slicing) and heterogeneous crossbar architecture, enabling adaptive exploitation of
topology-dependent sparsity and efficient matrix processing
across extreme dimensional range.
