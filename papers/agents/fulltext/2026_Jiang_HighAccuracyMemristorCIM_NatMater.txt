Memristor-based analogue computing in memory (CIM) offers
revolutionary gains in energy efficiency and computing power for
data-intensive applications such as artificial intelligence. However, it
typically struggles with achieving high accuracy at the same time, owing
to the noise-sensitive nature of analogue computing and the non-ideal
characteristics at the device and circuit levels that inevitably result in
computing errors. Although progress has been made in device engineering
and hardware–algorithm co-optimization to mitigate the error and
parasitic effects, many of these advances inadvertently incur a hardware or
energy consumption overhead, undermining the core benefits of analogue
CIM. This Review dissects the computing error sources across the CIM
hierarchy from the memristor device and array to the system architecture
and algorithm, and evaluates the strategies to minimize those errors. We
highlight the material and device innovations, array-level techniques and
algorithm–architecture co-design frameworks towards high-accuracy
analogue CIM. By dissecting the trade-off between computing accuracy
and implementation cost, this Review draws a roadmap for translating
memristor-based analogue CIM technology from proof-of-concept
prototypes to large-scale deployment for accelerating next-generation
artificial intelligence.

Artificial intelligence (AI) has progressed rapidly in recent years, and
transformative technologies such as large language models (LLMs) now
profoundly impact our lives1. However, rising computational demands
for AI substantially outpace the improvement in chip performance
that is driven by Moore’s law scaling, creating a critical hardware bottleneck2. A primary reason for this challenge lies in the von Neumann
architecture that underpins modern computing systems. This architecture necessitates frequent data shuttling between physically separated

memory and processing units (Fig. 1a), causing a fundamental von
Neumann bottleneck alongside memory and power walls that critically
constrain computing performance and energy efficiency3.
To overcome this fundamental constraint, memristor-based analogue computing in memory (CIM) offers a highly promising solution
inspired by the biological brain4. It computes the matrix-vector multiplication (MVM)—the core operation of both artificial neural networks
and signal processing—within the memory array following fundamental

Performing computation directly where data resides delivers unparalleled advantages in energy efficiency and speed, effectively overcoming the constraints of the von Neumann paradigm. Substantial progress
has been made in developing analogue CIM chips for AI, evolving from
initial proof-of-concept arrays5 to board-level integrated systems6–9
and recently achieving full system-on-chip integration10,11. Successful demonstrations across diverse domains including AI12,13, signal
processing14,15 and scientific computing16 underscore the potential of
this technology to fundamentally revolutionize hardware for those
data-intensive applications.
However, the inherent computing errors of analogue CIM,
primarily from device-level fluctuations and array-level parasitics, limit
its accuracy. Often overlooked in idealized theoretical analyses, these
errors pose two challenges for practical hardware implementations.
First, strategies to mitigate those errors usually introduce substantial
overheads in hardware cost and energy consumption, which undermine the core advantages of analogue CIM. Second, these errors could
accumulate from devices to arrays8 and hence fundamentally limit the
scalability and accuracy of analogue CIM, restricting its practical applications for deep neural networks (DNNs) and scientific computing.
Therefore, addressing the error effect and improving the accuracy with acceptable overhead is a pivotal challenge for translating
memristor-based analogue CIM from laboratory prototypes to

large-scale adoption. Whereas previous research studies and
reviews4,17,18 have investigated the device innovations, crossbar architectures or hardware–algorithm co-optimization strategies, they
rarely scrutinize the accuracy–overhead trade-off that is inherent to
these solutions. To fill this gap, this Review systematically analyses the
error sources across the CIM hierarchy, from memristor device and
array to system architecture and algorithm, and further evaluates the
strategies for mitigating these computing errors, as shown in Fig. 1c.
Through a comprehensive review, we intend to identify the useful
approaches that improve the accuracy with acceptable overhead,
providing a roadmap to bridge the gap between theoretical promise
and real-world deployment.

Material engineering
Central to device optimization, material engineering focuses substantially on optimizing the RSL. Selecting suitable materials determines

Material selection. The physical realization and experimental demonstration of the earliest memristors were reported using TiOx-based
devices21,22. Subsequent extensive research has been conducted on a
variety of oxides as the RSL, and most of them have focused on simple

binary oxides for convenient processing. Reliable resistive switching requires thermal stability of both the conductive and insulating
phases, which is more readily achieved in materials with fewer
thermodynamically stable solid-state phases23. In addition, the
thermo-chemical model predicts that a material’s breakdown strength
is inversely proportional to the square root of its dielectric constant
(κ), thus favouring high-κ oxides to achieve low forming voltages24.
Compatibility with CMOS technology is also a critical consideration
for advanced integration. Consequently, HfOx and TaOx have emerged
as highly promising RSL materials that meet these criteria23 (Fig. 2c).
Memristors with high-entropy oxides25 (for example, comprising Zr, Hf,
Nb, Ta, Mo and W) exhibit a ‘cocktail effect’ that combines the benefits
of the good retention of HfOx/TaOx-based memristors and the gradual
conductance modulation of WOx-based memristors, enhancing the
overall performance through the synergy of composite oxides.
Beyond amorphous oxides, other materials have been gaining
interest as the RSL. For example, epiRAM (epitaxial random access
memory) devices use epitaxial single-crystal SiGe films with predefined
dislocations to guide Ag filaments, improving uniformity and enabling
analogue switching26. Brownmillerite oxides such as SrCoO2.5 leverage
ordered oxygen vacancy channels for controllable phase transitions27.
Molecular memristors have also demonstrated 14-bit programmable
conductance states28, although their practical maturity requires further validation given the limited independent replication studies so
far29. Overall, memristors based on those materials remain at an early
stage of development, necessitating extensive further research for
CMOS integration30.

Structural innovations

Doping engineering. Doping engineering, using metal cations (for
example, Al, Ti, Ge, Mg), non-metallic ions (for example, Si, N, F) or
ionic groups (for example, NO2−), provides a versatile strategy for
modifying memristor characteristics. A primary goal is to reduce
the forming voltage by lowering the formation energy of the Frenkel
defect pair31 (Fig. 2d). In addition, dopants can introduce new conductive pathways or additional electrochemical processes to achieve
voltage reduction32. Another key objective involves stabilizing oxygen
vacancies at fixed positions, creating a ‘pinning effect’. This promotes
the formation of multiple, spatially confined weak filaments, substantially enhancing switching uniformity and device reliability during
operation33.

Large-scale integration with advanced Si CMOS technology

Interface engineering. Interface capping layers enhance the quality
of the top interface and the device performance. For instance, Ti-based
metallic interface capping layers act as effective oxygen reservoirs to
substantially reduce the operation voltages34. In addition, thermal
enhanced layers (TELs) with a low thermal conductivity (for example, TaOx), concentrate Joule heating in the HfOx RSL, promoting the
formation of multiple weak CFs and analogue switching35 (Fig. 2e).
Recent work has further proposed ternary oxide TELs with elements
of divergent oxygen affinities: high-affinity elements (for example,
Ti) form metal oxides to tune the conductance, whereas low-affinity
elements (for example, Ta) remain metallic to bond oxygen ions, suppressing relaxation36.
Electrode engineering. The material of the bottom electrode critically influences the memristor characteristics through interactions
at the RSL/bottom electrode interface (Fig. 2f). Active electrodes,
such as Ti-rich TiN, readily scavenge oxygen ions from the RSL, generating vacancies that lower forming voltage but accelerate endurance degradation via depletion of the oxygen reservoir37. Conversely,
inert electrodes (for example, Ru) mitigate such reactions due to high
oxygen diffusion and reaction energy barriers, greatly enhancing the
endurance38. Alternatively, incorporating a weaker oxygen reservoir
layer above the bottom electrode could also improve the device retention by anchoring the CFs39.

Beyond material engineering, structural innovations, such as multilayer RSL and sidewall passivation, offer complementary routes to
synergistically enhance the memristor performance.
Multilayer RSL. Multilayer RSL configurations aim to improve the performance through structural design. For instance, inserting an oxygen
diffusion barrier such as SnO2 in the HfOx-based RSL confines filament
rupture during reset, enhancing high-resistance state uniformity40.
Superlattice structures (for example, HfOx/AlOx stacks) segment the
filament into domains (Fig. 2g), forcing stepwise growth/rupture that
enables gradual analogue switching and reduces variability41. Such a
multilayer RSL can also elevate the crystallization temperature, mitigating thermal diffusion and grain-boundary-assisted ion migration
to enhance retention42.
Sidewall passivation. Nanoscale memristors are vulnerable to
etching-induced damage and free-oxygen diffusion near the cell
peripheries, which destabilize the CFs while impairing reliability and
uniformity34 (Fig. 2h). Sidewall passivation addresses these scaling
challenges by mitigating environmental interference at the cell edges.
These strategies typically involve low-damage etching to reduce edge
defects, cell-edge oxidation to form inert zones that confine switching,
and encapsulation with oxygen barriers such as Si3N4 to seal the cell
and stabilize the filament environment43. This integrated passivation
approach is essential to achieving reliable nanoscale memristors44 and
high yields45 in large-scale integration.

Whereas the above optimizations improve the intrinsic device properties, the ultimate benchmark lies in the performance of large-scale
arrays. Integration with Si CMOS technology enhances the yield and
reduces variability, wheres scaling promises a greater density and
efficiency for analogue computing. However, at advanced nodes,
this integration introduces system-level challenges that require the
co-optimization of device precision and low-conductance states.
Device precision. Achieving high-precision conductance modulation
is a fundamental requirement for accurate analogue computing. However, a trade-off emerges between the technology node advancement
and the achievable bit precision of memristors7,8,39,44–59, as illustrated in
Fig. 2i,j. This challenge stems partly from increased forming voltages
for scaled memristor devices, which impose a critical challenge for the
short-channel Si access transistor in advanced CMOS technology32,34.
Reducing the RSL thickness effectively lowers the forming voltage, yet
paradoxically degrades critical device properties. A thinner RSL hinders complete CF rupture during reset operations, reducing the on/off
ratio. Concurrently, shortened CFs exhibit increased sensitivity to ionic
and thermal perturbations, compromising the device reliability60,61.
These factors collectively constrain the achievable device bit precision, making the development of high-precision memristors that are
compatible with advanced nodes a critical challenge.
Low-conductance engineering. Beyond the intrinsic device precision,
engineering the memristor to achieve a lower operational conductance
state represents a crucial yet often overlooked strategy for mitigating array-level non-idealities. Although a high conductance usually
benefits the read speed in memory applications, a lower conductance
is paramount for CIM systems. It directly mitigates the voltage drop
(IR drop, where I is the current and R is the resistance) along metal
lines, reduces the total power consumption and prevents saturation
in peripheral circuits, thereby enhancing the scalability and accuracy
of large-scale CIM arrays. Strategies to lower conductance include
reducing the cell area and increasing the RSL thickness, although these
may elevate the forming voltage62. Alternatively, adjusting the oxygen

In addition, achieving sufficient computational accuracy in
practice requires the inherent precision limitations of unit cells to
be addressed74,75. For example, multiple unit cells can be grouped to
represent a single weight value with higher precision. In this case,
peripheral circuits, including digital-to-analogue converters (DACs)
and analogue-to-digital converters (ADCs), are also essential components and need to be taken into consideration70. However, these
array-level integrated systems are subject to numerous non-ideal
sources8 that contribute to computational errors hierarchically, as
shown in Fig. 3. At the unit cell level, programming of the memristor inevitably introduces errors due to the limited precision of the
programming circuitry, where a certain margin within the target
conductance value is predefined in the write–verify process. More
importantly, the post-programming relaxation induces a conductance
drift that exacerbates errors over time. Furthermore, the mapping of
high-precision neural network weights (for example, single-precision
floating-point format) onto groups of memristor devices also inherently introduces quantization errors. Meanwhile, array-scale parasitic
resistances, including the inter-cell and access resistance, generate an
IR drop during the MVM computation, and this effect is worsened as
the array dimension is increased (for example, >128 × 128)15. Peripheral
circuitry introduces further non-idealities. For example, DACs and
ADCs exhibit intrinsic offset and gain errors, and ADCs also suffer
from quantization errors and input swing limitations. Furthermore,
the nonlinear current–voltage (I–V) characteristics of memristors,
although a device-level non-ideality, manifest their adverse effects
specifically during array-based computation. Even under precise programming, such nonlinearity causes the output current to deviate from
the ideal value predicted by Ohm’s law, thereby introducing errors
into the weighted-sum operations. By contrast, the device programming nonlinearity and D2D variability, which are critical for on-chip
learning, usually do not introduce analogous errors for inference, as
the conductance can be iteratively programmed to the target value.

Array-level strategies for error suppression
Beyond individual device optimization, the assembly of memristors
into crossbar arrays for parallel MVM computations introduces additional error sources. Ideally, passive memristor crossbars, utilizing a
single memristor (1R)5,64 as a unit cell, offer the highest cell density and
three-dimensional (3D) stacking potential. However, the scalability of
large-scale 1R arrays is constrained by intrinsic issues such as device
variability65 and sneak paths66. These sneak paths refer to parasitic currents along unintended pathways in the array, which can degrade the
write and read accuracy of memristor cells. Although self-rectifying
memristors, which exhibit minimal current under a small read voltage,
offer a promising solution67, their efficacy demands further large-scale
validation. As the most common solution, integrating an access transistor with each memristor in a one-transistor–one-memristor (1T1R)68
configuration effectively suppresses sneak paths while also enabling
precise conductance programming through stable current control69.
For signed weights, differential structures such as 2T2R70 offer viable
solutions. Various alternative unit cell designs, including 3T2R44 and
2T1R71 configurations, have also been proposed to achieve specialized functionalities. However, incorporating transistors results in substantial area overhead and also limits the 3D stacking potential17. The
1TnR scheme72 aims to improve the cell density but introduces critical
challenges for efficient array programming due to the sneak-paths
issue. Integrating passive selector devices with memristors (1S1R)73 is
an active research area, yet the performance limitations of selectors

detail the various strategies that have been devised to mitigate these
errors across different levels, outlining their specific design implementations and associated costs.

Device programming schemes
Accurate analogue CIM necessitates multi-bit-precision devices,
which critically depend on reliable programming schemes to achieve
the target conductance states. This choice of programming strategy
profoundly affects both programming errors, including accuracy
and post-programming conductance stability, and implementation
costs. These costs include programming duration, power consumption
and additional circuit overhead. Therefore, optimizing programming
strategies becomes essential for balancing computational precision
with practical constraints in hardware development.
Programming precision. Enhancing the programming precision has
been the focus of extensive research on individual memristor devices
and arrays. Early approaches relied on current-limitation techniques
as in Fig. 4a, which included setting the compliance current via access
transistor gate control80 or external circuits81. These methods offer
high parallelism but rely heavily on the device uniformity, typically
limiting the achievable precision to 1 bit and rarely exceeding 3 bits.
To improve the precision, fixed-condition write–verify schemes82
were introduced (Fig. 4b). Here, the programmed device conductance is read and compared with the target value after each write pulse
to determine if subsequent set or reset operations are needed. While
effectively improving the programming precision, it substantially
increases circuit complexity and time cost due to the time-consuming
verify steps, reducing the programming parallelism and demanding
high device programming linearity. To address the linearity issue, incremental write–verify schemes83—also known as incremental step pulse
programming (ISPP), as commonly used in NAND flash memories—were
adopted as illustrated in Fig. 4c. Previous studies have indicated that
incremental steps in write voltage pulse amplitude, particularly via
the word line84,85, offer superior efficiency compared with incremental steps in pulse width86. However, it must be pointed out that the
incremental schemes, although further enhancing precision, require
more complex control logic and additional caching of write conditions,
further limiting the programming parallelism. The reported programming precision remains below 8 bits87, limited by random telegraph
noise from incomplete CFs near the main conduction channel88,89.
Applying small, alternating positive and negative denoising pulses
can annihilate these channels, drastically reducing fluctuation and
enabling 11-bit precision48. This method requires repeated read/write
steps, incurring time and energy overhead. However, the small voltages
and parallel column-wise operation can mitigate these costs, making
it suitable for inference applications with infrequent programming.

Post-programming relaxation. Beyond programming-precision
limitation, a measurable post-programming relaxation effect is commonly observed in memristors, characterized by an unpredictable
conductance drift after devices are programmed to a target state36.
This drift severely compromises the actual device precision, often
rendering it considerably lower than the initially programmed precision79. Repeated programming8 offers a straightforward and effective
countermeasure, as illustrated in Fig. 4e. However, this approach is
time-consuming and faces scalability challenges in large-scale arrays.
Research on waveform engineering has identified specific waveform
shapes, such as triangular91 or positive–negative pulse pairs92 (Fig. 4f),
that can mitigate the relaxation effect to a certain extent. Furthermore,
introducing increased waiting interval (Tdelay) values between write
and verify steps in the write–verify schemes has been found to reduce
relaxation78,93 (Fig. 4g). Investigations have also revealed that the specific sequence of set and reset operations influences the relaxation
behaviour79,91,94 (Fig. 4h). Additional studies have proposed reducing the voltage change between programming steps (Vstep)95 (Fig. 4i),
particularly for minimizing the conductance change of the final write
operation (ΔGlast)79, as an effective drift countermeasure (Fig. 4j).
Some studies have attempted to reduce the conductance state overlap through non-uniform state distributions96, which is advantageous
for memory applications. However, such schemes are less suitable for
analogue CIM, as they require substantial algorithmic adaptations to
account for nonlinear quantization. Recent strategies have increasingly
combined multiple relaxation-mitigation techniques and prioritized
overhead reduction79. Nevertheless, most methods inevitably incur
additional costs in hardware design complexity, programming duration
and caching requirements, as summarized in Supplementary Table 2.
Consequently, the development of more efficient, relaxation-aware
programming schemes remains a critical objective.
In addition, optimizing the programming scheme of forming,
a critical one-time process that determines CF formation, offers
an efficient strategy to minimize relaxation. Approaches such as
high-temperature forming61 and reverse forming97 have shown promise in reducing relaxation by altering the filament morphology and
modifying the distribution of oxygen ions and vacancies. Refining
these forming strategies continues to be a key focus for future research.

Peripheral circuit precision compensation
Peripheral circuits introduce error sources including IR drop and
AD/DA errors, which further degrade the MVM accuracy in analogue
CIM. Mitigating such peripheral non-idealities to achieve precise
results remains a key challenge and requires ongoing research.
The IR drop issue within memristor arrays is primarily from parasitic resistances, which include the inter-cell resistance and the access
resistance15. This fundamentally restricts computation parallelism for
analogue CIM. Proposed methods to mitigate this include applying
column-specific scaling factors to restore distorted currents towards
ideal values101, although their applicability in practical scenarios is
limited. The 2T2R configuration70 offers a distinct advantage by inherently reducing the accumulated source line current to mitigate IR
drop while simultaneously enabling sign representation of synaptic
weights, establishing it as a practical solution. Compact models that
capture parasitic resistance-induced voltage drops have also been
implemented, enabling pre-distortion compensation by modifying
the target weight matrix (Fig. 4p). This approach has been shown to
achieve accurate fully parallel matrix multiplication in a 128 kB array
with all 1,024 inputs activated15.
DAC and ADC non-idealities, although often overlooked, also
critically impact the computational accuracy. Research has indicated
that they can degrade the model inference accuracy more severely than
the device relaxation effect under certain conditions77. To address this,
a progressive parameter search scheme was proposed8,76. It utilizes
on-chip inference results and digital system simulations to find the
optimal chip-operating conditions and store the offsets for subsequent
compensations, as shown in Fig. 4q. A similar strategy has been proposed
by leveraging results from on-chip and digital MVM computations, using
a gradient descent approach to refine target weights102, as depicted
in Fig. 4r. This iterative refinement progressively minimizes the MVM
error, effectively mitigating both programming and peripheral circuits
errors. By relying solely on the results of on-chip inference, this method
circumvents the necessity for high-resolution ADCs to read individual
devices, streamlining the overall system design. Whereas these
approaches effectively mitigate array-level errors, the additional circuit
overhead introduced by the necessary digital systems cannot be ignored.
Beyond the errors of peripheral circuits, the nonlinear I–V characteristics of memristor devices introduce another critical error source
during analogue MVM operations as discussed earlier. Whereas strategies such as restricting the read voltage to a pseudo-linear subregion103
or pre-tuning weights104 have been explored, their effectiveness is often

novel array architectures and alternative signal domains17 (Fig. 4s).
These include encoding inputs using fixed voltage pulses (for example,
via pulse width or pulse number modulation) and encoding the computation results through non-current output schemes, such as the integration of output charge105. Furthermore, some novel ADC designs106,107

such as pulse width108 or delay time109 are being co-developed with these
architectures. Specifically, a voltage-regulating current-sense topology has been proposed to reduce the area and power consumption of
conventional ADCs106. In addition, a memristor-assisted adaptive ADC
design has been developed, achieving increased energy efficiency and a
smaller chip area107. As for alternative output encoding schemes, MVM
results can be translated into pulse width to enable efficient array-level
cascading108, or they can be encoded as the delay time of an inverter
chain, enabling high computing parallelism in a static-power-free manner109. Moreover, charge-domain CIM also offers an effective means
to high energy efficiency and throughput110. These implementations
show promising potential for achieving accurate signal readout with
reduced overhead, collectively addressing the coupled challenges of
peripheral non-idealities and device nonlinearity.
In addition, inspired by the error correction code (ECC) in digital
memory, customized ECC for CIM systems has been explored111 to
enhance computational accuracy rather than ensure bit-level correctness (Fig. 4t)112. For instance, Li et al. have demonstrated that on a
DenseNet-40 network for CIFAR-10 classification, a tailored MAC-ECC
(multiply-and-accumulate error correction code) can recover the
inference accuracy from a 6.3% degradation to near-software baseline
(only 1.1% loss)111. Although promising for reducing inference errors
and even achieving iso-accuracy in analogue CIM, this approach introduces area and power overheads from dedicated parity arrays and
decoding circuits. Moreover, the efficacy of the ECC is bounded by the
system’s native bit error rate (or BER). Its advantage diminishes as the
BER approaches the ECC scheme’s capability and can fail completely
beyond that threshold111. More robust ECC schemes would improve
coverage but at a cost of increasing circuit complexity and overhead,
posing trade-offs between reliability and efficiency.

Algorithm–architecture co-design
To implement state-of-the-art neural networks in practical applications, algorithm–architecture co-design becomes essential to achieve
improved performance for memristor-based CIM. On the architecture
side, it is favourable for the CIM hardware to integrate both analogue
CIM and a digital core (such as digital CIM or a digital processor) as
mixed-precision CIM (mix-CIM) to compensate for the accuracy limitations of analogue-only CIM systems, thus meeting diverse precision
requirements of complicated algorithms. Meanwhile, on the algorithm
side, optimizations are also needed to take the non-ideal characteristics of CIM hardware into consideration and make the algorithm
error-tolerant, thereby enhancing the system’s robustness. In this
section, recent advances are reviewed on mix-CIM architectures and
algorithm-level optimizations for error-tolerant CIM.

whereas digital cores ensure high computing accuracy. For complex
neural networks, the flexible allocation of these hardware types, relying
on the compiler and on-chip controller, is crucial to balance efficiency
and accuracy. The compiler maps sensitive weights to digital cores via
software simulation, and the controller manages data communication

Cell-wise mix-CIM. Compared with the layer-wise and bit-wise

enhancing hardware utilization.

both the layer-wise and cell-wise mix-CIM113 or utilizing both bit-wise

must be precisely controlled, as excessive digital usage degrades the
overall energy efficiency.

Algorithm-level optimizations for error-tolerant CIM
Owing to inherent hardware non-idealities in CIM, algorithm robustness is essential for reliability. Algorithm-level optimizations fall into
two categories: hardware-aware training and model-driven approaches
(Fig. 5b). Hardware-aware training retrains networks with hardware
characteristics in mind, greatly improving the accuracy and scalability
for complex networks, albeit with high preprocessing overhead. By
contrast, model-driven optimizations rely on mathematical analysis
without retraining, offering lower cost and better generalization,
although typically yielding less accuracy gain and scalability.
Hardware-aware training. Hardware-aware training is further divided
into two subcategories on the basis of hardware involvement during
training (Fig. 5c). Hardware-out-of-the-loop (HOL) methods, such as
noise-injected training116 and quantization-aware training117, incorporate a hardware non-ideality model into the training loop to enhance
robustness. These approaches are easy to implement and can achieve
accuracy that is comparable to floating-point implementations for
large-scale DNNs77. However, their effectiveness is often limited
by discrepancies between the model and actual hardware. To
address this, hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) methods integrate real CIM
hardware into training by using measured array outputs for gradient calculation and weight updates. In situ learning, a common HIL
approach, updates the network online after mapping to accommodate hardware non-idealities7, offering substantial accuracy gains
but incurring high programming overhead due to repeated weight
updates. To mitigate this, a chip-in-the-loop progressive fine-tuning
strategy deploys the network layer by layer, retraining unmapped layers
using actual outputs from mapped layers, achieving a 1.99% accuracy
improvement on CIFAR-10 with ResNet-208. Whereas HIL methods
generally yield higher accuracy, they require tight collaboration
between digital and CIM cores, leading to heavier preprocessing
overhead.
Model-driven optimizations. Model-driven optimizations include
three common approaches (Fig. 5d). Matrix expansion13 introduces redundancy by expanding a small weight matrix into a larger
one, reducing the word error rate from 42 to 7.9% on LibriSpeech with
MLPerf RNNT13. However, it incurs additional hardware overhead
due to larger array requirements and extra post-processing. Local
quantization118 first calculates the weight sensitivity and then programs
sensitive weights with higher precision while relaxing precision for less
sensitive ones—improving the accuracy without increasing programming overhead. Matrix regularization addresses ill-posed problems
that are highly sensitive to perturbations. Techniques such as singular
Nature Materials | Volume 25 | July 2026 | 1110–1124

https://doi.org/10.1038/s41563-026-02600-y

value truncation, Tikhonov regularization and ridge regression119
enhance robustness against hardware non-idealities120. Although still
in the early stages, this approach holds promise for broadening CIM
applications.

Generally speaking, hardware-aware training strategies, especially
HIL approaches, tend to result in more overhead than model-driven
optimization strategies. In practice, the above-mentioned methods
may also be utilized together for better performance.

Outlook
Over the past 15 years, CIM hardware has evolved through three
main stages (Fig. 6). In the first stage (~2010–2016), small-scale
memristor arrays (≤1 kb) demonstrated MVM acceleration for
simple tasks such as MNIST digit recognition, achieving an accuracy
of 1–3 bits. The second stage (~2018–2020) saw larger arrays (1–100 kb)
enabling more complex tasks such as image recognition and
signal processing with 4–6-bit accuracy, claiming high energy efficiency. In the third stage (~2021–2025), CIM system-on-chip (SOC)
technology emerged with ~100 kb to 100 Mb memristors, supporting more sophisticated workloads including regression tasks with
6–8-bit accuracy.
Looking ahead, CIM hardware is expected to continue advancing towards higher computing accuracy (>8 bits), larger integration
density (>1 Gb) and more complex workloads (such as LLMs and artificial general intelligence (AGI)). To realize these goals, future CIM
hardware should not only rely on continued optimizations at the individual device or array level but also pay more attention to a holistic,
cost-aware co-design. Several important directions for future research
are elaborated as follows (the rightmost panels of Fig. 6).

3D integration
Owing to the reticle size constraint and physical scaling limit of feature
size, 3D integration technology becomes essential for high-density,
high-performance CIM hardware. Different 3D integration technologies could be adopted, including device-level (for example, 3D vertical
memristors), layer-level (for example, monolithic 3D integration) and
chip-level (for example, chiplet) 3D integrations. These technologies
could enable a much higher integration density (>100 Mb mm−2), better
versatility (for example, mixed-precision computing cores, on-chip
buffers and routers) and a higher interconnect bandwidth (>1 TB s−1).
Despite those promising advantages, 3D integration may introduce
other critical challenges to the reliability and accuracy of analogue
CIM that merit further studies to address them. For example, such
increased fabrication process complexity may lead to yield loss and
extra reliability issues, and the elevated temperature due to limited
thermal dissipation in 3D stacked structures may degrade device conductance stability and impact the computing accuracy of analogue
CIM. Besides, the increased coupling, parasitics and noise from 3D
interconnects may introduce signal-integrity issues.

Analogue–digital mixed-CIM architecture
For more sophisticated scenarios such as LLMs and AGI, an adaptive
CIM system is envisioned by integrating both analogue and digital
cores and dynamically partitioning computations between these
cores on the basis of specific precision demands, thus optimizing the
trade-off between accuracy and energy efficiency. Meanwhile, this
mixed-CIM architecture could greatly expand the applicability of analogue CIM, spanning from low-power edge devices to AI infrastructures
and supercomputers.
