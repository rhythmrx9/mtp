I. I N T R O D U C T I O N
Rapid advances in machine learning (ML) and artificial
intelligence (AI) have touched human lives in an unprecedented way as the application space has proliferated over
the last decade [1]. Deep neural networks (DNNs), in particular, have had enormous success in several ML tasks,
such as image classification [2], [3], object detection [4],
and natural language processing [5]. However, the capabilities of deep learning algorithms have been accompanied
by an increase in computational complexity [6]. Designers
have proposed special-purpose accelerators [7]–[11] to
improve the time and energy required to execute DNNs.
These accelerators are designed to efficiently perform
matrix–vector multiplication (MVM) operations and the
core computational kernel in DNNs. Despite their extensive
use of on-chip scratchpads to exploit data reuse, these
accelerators are eventually still limited by the off-chip
memory bottleneck [12], motivating closer integration of
memory and processing units as the next step in the
quest for further efficiency. Taking this concept to its
logical end leads to a new computing paradigm, “inmemory” computing, wherein memory arrays that store
data are also capable of performing compute operations.
Although “in-memory” computing can be explored in complementary metal–oxide–semiconductor (CMOS) technology [13]–[18], fundamental design conflicts in CMOS
memories as well as their limited density and slowing
scaling trends in CMOS are limiting factors. To that end,
nonvolatile memory (NVM) technologies [19], [20], such
as phase-change memory (PCM) [21], resistive random
access memory (RRAM) [22], [23], and spintronics [24],
offer immense promise as an alternative to CMOS due
to their high storage density and the ability to perform
massively parallel in situ MVM operations. This led to
a growing interest in exploring NVM technology as the
substrate for the next generation of ML hardware [19],
[25], [26].
The basic computational fabric of NVM technologies is
a 2-D cross-point arrangement of NVM devices, commonly
known as a crossbar. Due to the intrinsic physics of these
NVM devices, they can store multiple states in a highly
dense memory with a bit-cell area of ∼4F 2 [27]. Furthermore, due to the resistive nature of NVM devices, crossbars
can be used to perform in situ MVM operations by programming a matrix of conductances into the 2-D array of
NVM devices that constitute the crossbar, applying a vector
of input voltages to the rows (columns) of the crossbar,
and reading out the currents from the columns (rows)
of the crossbar. This mode of computation uses Ohm’s

law and Kirchoff’s laws of current summation to natively
realize massively parallel analog MVM operations inside
memory and can achieve significantly higher energy efficiency over digital accelerators by eliminating sequential
memory accesses. Furthermore, the high density of resistive crossbars can be leveraged to achieve large on-chip
storage, thus avoiding frequent data transfer between
the off-chip memory and the processor. This promise has
fueled great interest over the past decade in designing
systems that can leverage the high computing efficiency
as well as the high-density storage of crossbar-based MVM
units [28]–[30].
The analog nature of computing in NVM crossbars introduces various layers of approximation, which can lead to
unique accuracy-energy tradeoffs [32]. The two primary
forms of approximations are: 1) functional inaccuracies
due to the intrinsic device and circuit nonidealities and
2) approximations made to peripheral circuitry in order
to minimize its overheads. Nonidealities signify effects
that cause a deviation from ideal MVM computations in
resistive crossbars. Undesirable device characteristics and
circuit behavior can manifest themselves in three kinds of
nonidealities: 1) linear nonidealities; 2) nonlinear nonidealities; and 3) random device and transistor variations. Linear nonidealities arise from parasitic resistances in metal
lines, source resistances in input drivers, and sink resistances in sensing circuits. On the other hand, nonlinear
nonidealities result from read and write nonlinearities in
the NVM devices and the access device characteristics.
Moreover, as the NVM process is in its nascent stage
of development, device variations are a major challenge.
Such variations can appear in the form of intracrossbar
and intercrossbar variations. The cumulative effect of the
aforementioned nonidealities can cause functional errors
at the MVM unit level. In the context of large-scale systems,
these errors can accumulate and degrade the application
accuracy significantly [33], [34]. Overall, considering the
energy benefits of crossbar-based computing systems over
their digital counterparts, the approximations due to nonidealities lead to a broader energy accuracy tradeoff across
different computing paradigms.
The outputs produced by analog MVM units need to
be communicated to other such units for the evaluation of large-scale neural networks. As analog signals
are less tolerant of noise, global communication in any
large-scale system needs to be digital. As a result, MVM
units need peripheral circuits, such as digital-to-analog
converters (DACs) at their inputs and analog-to-digital
converters (ADCs) at their outputs. These peripheral circuits within MVM units, particularly the ADCs, have been
shown to account for a large fraction (up to ∼80%)
of the total energy consumption of the MVM unit [35].
In addition, ADCs consume close to 70% of the area of
the MVM unit. Thus, the power and area of peripheral
circuits significantly reduce the benefits of the crossbars
in terms of compute efficiency and high density. However,
as the power consumption of ADCs is a function of its

II. A N A T O M Y O F R E S I S T I V E
CROSSBARS
Let us first introduce the concept of crossbar organization.
Fig. 2 shows the conceptual NVM-based crossbar structure,
where NVM devices are connected as a matrix similar to a
standard memory array. In such resistive crossbars, each
memory cell is connected to its corresponding word-line
(WL) and bitline (BL). In principle, MVM operations can
be efficiently achieved in a resistive crossbar using Ohm’s
law for multiplication and Kirchhoff’s current law (KCL)
for summation. The MVM vector operands are applied as
analog voltages to WLs of the crossbar. Thus, the resulting
current passing through a memory cell is the multiplication
of the applied voltage (V ) at the WL connected to that
cell and the NVM device conductance (G). Furthermore,
KCL sums up the currents from all cells connected to the
same BL resulting in the multiply-and-accumulate (MAC)
operation of V and G. Such inherent MAC computations in
crossbars enable parallel MVM operations where the inputs
are V and G, while the MAC output is represented as the
current passing at BL (IBL ). In the following, we discuss,
in detail, the basic building blocks of a resistive crossbar,
including the NVM cell structure based on various device
technologies, selectors, and peripherals required for analog
computing.

A. Device Technologies
Device technologies are important to understand the
key device parameters that play a key role in large-scale
crossbar designs. The scalability of resistive crossbars as
an MVM macro is primarily dependent on the following.
1) Resistance ON/OFF ratio: the ratio between the resistances of the two extreme states in the device.
2) ON-resistance: the low-resistance state (LRS) of the
device.
3) Bit precision: number of states that can be reliably
programed and read from the device.

These parameters affect the resistive crossbar size, thus
playing a role in both the MVM functionality as well as
energy and latency. We will study the impact of these
parameters on MVM functionality in light of peripheral
circuits and device/circuit nonidealities in Section II-D.
Energy and latency metrics of crossbars will be discussed in
detail in Section IV. Here, we will elucidate different NVM
device technologies that have been explored for crossbars.
The basic building block of the MVM compute macro
based on crossbars is the NVM devices. However, CMOS
memories have also been explored to build this macro,
which we will discuss later. NVM devices are mostly
two-terminal devices based on phase-change materials (e.g., PCRAM) [37], oxide-based materials (e.g.,
RRAMs) [38], or magnetic materials [e.g., spin-transfer
torque (STT)-MRAM] [39]. On the other hand, some
spintronic devices can also be three-terminal devices, such
as spin-orbit torque (SOT)-based devices [40], which have
decoupled read and write paths. In this section, we will
discuss how the inherent physics of these devices enable
multibit storage and in situ multiplication operations.
1) Two-Terminal Devices: Two-terminal devices usually
consist of a layer sandwiched between two electrodes. This
layer is a phase-change material in the case of a PCM
device, an oxide-based material in the case of an RRAM
device, and a stack of ferromagnetic (FM) layers in the case
of STT-MRAM.
a) Phase-change materials: PCMs are chalcogenides
that have large contrast in electrical and optical properties resulting in a significantly high ON / OFF-resistance or
reflectance ratio. The device structure follows a mushroom
cell structure representing the shape of the switching
volume above the heater, as shown in Fig. 3. These materials can exist in multiple intermediate states between two
extreme resistance states, amorphous (high-resistance),

B. Circuits: Peripherals and Access
The aforementioned MVM unit based on resistive crossbars needs to be supplemented by peripheral circuits and
access devices for suitable interfacing between the analog
compute unit and digital communication and data flow.

and peripheral circuitry, including input encoding and

provide efficient data access in a memristive cell, including
two-terminal selectors and three-terminal selectors (transistors). The access devices are used to avoid sneak paths
in resistive crossbars during the write operation. Next,
we consider several peripheral circuitry needed for MVM
operations based on the type of input encoding. It is worth
mentioning that digital inputs can be applied to a memristive crossbar for computing MVM using several encoding
schemes that include voltage encoding and time encoding.
In voltage encoding, the voltage amplitude is modulated
according to the digital input, while the pulsewidth is modulated according to the digital inputs in time encoding. For

To that end, selector devices and access transistors have

problem [79]–[85].

the oxide/electrode or the bandgap of oxide/oxide with

In addition, Cu-containing mixed ionic–electronic conductance (MIEC) offers bidirectional exponential I –V characteristics that can be adopted in 1S-1R crossbars [80].

threshold switch (OTS) based on doped chalcogenide
materials has been shown to be a promising TS [85]. Moreover, field-assisted superlinear threshold (FAST) selector has been reported in [84] with steep turn-on slope
(<5 mV/decade) and high ON -current JON (>5 MA/cm2 ).

b) Analog-to-digital conversion: Analog-to-digital conversion is a critical step in the MVM operation using
resistive crossbars, as the analog voltages need to be

memristive crossbars, including successive approximation

much larger than the same-precision SAR ADC [95].
SAR ADC is the most common ADC architecture adopted
in analog in-memory computing memristive crossbars due
to its simplicity and low area/energy overhead compared
with other ADC architectures [95]. The SAR architecture
exploits a binary search algorithm in a feedback loop,
including 1-bit ADC (comparator). Fig. 8(b) shows a conceptual schematic of a standard SAR ADC consisting of a
comparator, n-bit DAC, and SAR logic. The SAR ADC algorithm starts with setting the SAR MSB value to “1,” and the
rest of the bits are zeros, producing VDAC = VREF /2. Then,
the comparator is enabled to decide whether Vsampled is
higher or lower than VDAC . The resulting decision is fed to
the SAR logic to configure the DAC to produce VDAC equal
to either VREF /4 or 3VREF /4. To produce N bits, the SAR
loop takes N clock cycles. Various ADC architectures
and precisions have been adopted in memristive crossbar
MVM units [29], [30], [88]. For instance, ISAAC [29]

and PUMA [30] adopted an 8-bit SAR ADC for higher
precision computations, while XNOR-RRAM implemented a
3-bit flash ADC for binary input/weight computation [57].
Note that the ADC area and energy costs are huge and
dominate the whole crossbar area and energy [30]. Typically, as reported in [35], ADC consumes 80.61% and
68.13% of the crossbar power and area, respectively.

ADC precision further reduces its area and energy.
It is worth mentioning that the ADC precision required
to compute exact MVM operation in an N × N crossbar
having K input precision and M weight precision should
be ceil(log2 ((2K − 1) ∗ (2M − 1) ∗ N )). Moreover, the energy
consumed by the ADC is a function of its precision [36].
By varying the precision of the ADC, we can introduce
functional errors as well as lower the energy consumption
of the NVM crossbar primitive. Researchers have proposed
techniques to reduce the required ADC precision as an
attempt to improve efficiency. As an example, ISAAC proposed to store the weights in its original or flipped form
to increase the number of zero sums, hence reducing
ADC precision. Moreover, recent works have proposed to
adopt lower-than-required ADC precision due to the sparse
nature of ML workloads and shown negligible accuracy
degradation [57]. To conclude, peripheral design choices,
such as input encoding, input/weight precision, ADC architecture, and precision, are key factors to improve energy
consumption, area, and performance at the crossbar and
system levels.

III. C R O S S B A R N O N I D E A L I T I E S
As discussed earlier, the analog nature of computing in
NVM crossbars can lead to approximations in their functionality as MVM units. Such approximations arise from

2286

device and circuit nonidealities originating from metal
lines, peripheral circuits, and nonideal devices. While we
will study how such approximate analog computing systems can outperform digital counterparts in Section IV, it is
important to delineate the sources of these approximations
and their impact on the functionality. In this section,
we provide a detailed analysis and perspective on the
implication of the imperfections in resistive crossbars on
MVM computations. Later, in Section V-C, we will delineate several techniques to overcome the effects of these
nonidealities. The mentioned device–circuit nonidealities
can be categorized into two types: 1) read nonidealities
and 2) write nonidealities.

A. Read Nonidealities
Generally, read nonidealities refer to the imperfections
in the devices and circuits that can lead to functional read
errors in MVM computations. Furthermore, read nonidealities can be categorized into two parts:
1) linear read nonidealities;
2) nonlinear read nonidealities.
1) Linear Read Nonidealities: Linear read nonidealities
comprise three kinds of parasitic resistances: 1) wire
resistance; 2) source resistance; and 3) sink resistance.
Fig. 11 shows a typical memristive crossbar with parasitic
resistances.
a) Wire resistances: These originate from the
interconnects between memristive cells in BLs and
WLs. Typically, the wire resistance can be calculated as

2) Nonlinear Read Nonidealities: Nonlinear read nonidealities originate from the nonlinear behavior of both
NVM devices and access transistors. The read operation is
affected by the nonlinear current (I ) versus voltage (V )
characteristics of the NVM devices. Besides NVM devices,
the transistor characteristics can also introduce nonlinearity in the computations.
a) Device read nonlinearity: Different device technologies can exhibit different kinds of nonlinear behavior due
to the fundamental dissimilarities in current conduction.
For example, transport in amorphous PCM devices has
been described by the Poole–Frenkel transport [111] of
carriers, which results in the current being linear at small
voltages and exponential at higher voltages. In the crystalline state, PCM devices exhibit Ohmic behavior at low
voltages and non-Ohmic behavior at high voltages due to
Joule heating. The compact model for such nonlinear I –V
behavior has been previously studied [112], [113].
Unlike PCMs, RRAM conduction relies on tunneling
mechanism that results in an exponential dependence of
current on voltage. This can be expressed as [59] I(d, V ) =
I0 exp(d/d0 ) sinh(V /V0 ). Here, d is the gap size between
the tip of the filament and electrode; I0 , d0 , and V0 are
fitting parameters.
Similarly, spin devices show voltage-dependent resistive characteristics. In addition, the nonlinear I versus

V characteristics in spin devices can be modeled using
nonequilibrium Green’s function (NEGF) [114]. Fig. 12(a)
shows the nonlinear I –V characteristics of: 1) PCM;
2) RRAM; and 3) spintronic technologies for RON = 10 kΩ.
It can be observed that all the NVM technologies exhibit
I –V characteristics that deviate from the expected linear
resistive behavior. Such nonlinear behavior can result in
functional errors when MVM units are constructed using
these devices as MVM operations demand linearity of the
output (current) with the input variables (V and G).
Indeed, nonlinearities during the read process in resistive
crossbars can pose a challenge toward accurate MVM
functionality.
b) Access device nonlinearity: The nonlinearity of
access devices can play a role in introducing functional
inaccuracies in MVM units. Various access devices have
been adopted in crossbar-based MVM units, as mentioned
in Section II-B1. Such access devices (two-terminal selectors or transistors) impose different nonlinear characteristics on the analog computing operations in crossbars.
i) Two-terminal selectors: Two-terminal selectors can
switch the current passing into the memory device by exponential switching or threshold switching, as mentioned in
Section II-B1. Interestingly, 1ES-1R-based crossbars show
more nonlinearity effects over different technologies than
1TS-1R-based crossbars. According to [79], 1S-1R cell
I –V characteristics strongly depend on the selector type
(ES or TS) after the RRAM state has been changed,
which is clearly shown in the linear scale I –V curves
in Fig. 12(b). ES inherently shows a slow slope of several
hundred mV/decades due to the exponential switching
nature. Therefore, a certain amount of the cell voltage
is continuously applied to ES even after it is turned on
during the 1ES-1R operation, resulting in the nonlinear
I –V curves, as shown in Fig. 12(b-i). On the other hand,
the applied voltage to 1TS-1R cell beyond TS threshold
voltage is fully transferred to the RRAM due to the fast
slope that is at most 5 mV/decade [79]. As a result,
the linear I –V trace of the 1TS-1R device governed by the
RRAM is shown in Fig. 12(b-i).
ii) Transistors as access devices: Generally, 1T-1R
cells behave more linearly than 1S-1R. The reason for
that is transistors in 1T-1R cells operate in the linear
region during the MVM operation. Moreover, there is
no threshold for the applied voltage to reach before
switching since it is a three-terminal switch. To that
end, 1T-1R exhibits less significant nonlinearity than
two-terminal selector-based cells with the cost of a large
cell area.

effective ON / OFF ratio of the crossbar. Thus, this impact
can be correlated with a higher ON / OFF ratio when a
similar trend is observed. Packing a lower number of bits
per device might reduce the average NF, but it harms the
storage density of the crossbars, which will reduce the area
efficiency.

design choices has their own negative implications. Thus,
careful design space exploration needs to be performed to

IV. A R C H I T E C T U R E
Typical ML workloads have large model sizes (hundreds
of million parameters) and are dominated by MVM operations. Furthermore, depending on the application, an inference workload (i.e., batch size of 1) can have low data
reuse. For instance, convolutional networks exhibit both
weight and input data reuse, but recurrent networks have
weight data reuse only, and MLPs have no data reuse [30].
Since CMOS-based architectures have limited on-die storage (tens of MBs in GPU) due to the low storage density
of SRAMs, ML models are typically stored using off-chip
memory, such as DRAM. As shown in Table 4, the energy
cost associated with DRAM access is orders of magnitude
higher than the energy cost of SRAM access and computation. Consequently, the execution of ML workloads on
2294

P ROCEEDINGS OF THE IEEE | Vol. 108, No. 12, December 2020

CMOS-based systems is dominated by off-chip memory
accesses. To this effect, the in-memory computing benefits
of resistive crossbars can be leveraged to design ML accelerators for overcoming the off-chip memory bottlenecks.
The key features of a resistive crossbar that drive the
design of a crossbar-based accelerator are: 1) high storage density and 2) high write cost (energy and latency).
First, the compact cell structure (4F 2 cell area for the
in-line two-terminal selector configuration) [103] and the
multibit storage device (2–6 bits) [89] enable resistive
crossbars to achieve order-of-magnitude higher on-chip
storage density compared with SRAM (124F 2 [134]).
Large on-chip memory can enable efficient data reuse
to optimize inference efficiency. Second, the high write
voltage [103] and multiple programming cycles (programverify approach [45]) result in much higher write cost
(energy and latency) than SRAM. Expensive memory
writes limit the applicability of a time-multiplexed architecture, where the crossbars are reused across layers by
reprogramming weight matrices and executing the corresponding MVM operations. Consequently, a spatial architecture where the DNN is partitioned such that the weights
(stationary data) are pinned to crossbars located across
multiple cores is more efficient as it leverages the benefits
of high storage density while alleviating the costly writes.

A. ReRam-Based Inference Accelerators
In an inference workload, crossbars typically perform
MVM operations with stationary matrices, i.e., DNN
weights. Thus, the spatial architecture can be mapped
with DNN weights during configuration (writing weights
to crossbars) and can be subsequently used for millions of
inferences, thereby amortizing the cost of configuration. In
addition to the MVM operations performed by crossbars,
DNNs require several other operations, such as vector
additions, vector multiplications, scalar, and nonlinear
operations. Furthermore, other nonstationary data, such
as inputs and partial sums, need to be stored and moved.
Consequently, designing a computation core requires augmenting the resistive crossbar with CMOS-based digital
logic units as well as memory units to provide the required
generality for DNN workloads. A naive approach toward
integrating the CMOS and NVM technologies is not viable
because of the huge disparity in compute and storage
density between the two technologies. CMOS digital logic
(MAC) has an order of magnitude higher area requirement than a crossbar of equal output width (∼20×) [30].

D. Training Accelerators
Training workloads pose additional requirements for
designing crossbar-based systems, namely: 1) larger
shared memory and 2) frequent crossbar writes. During
inference, the computed activations can be discarded once
they are fed to the following layers. However, during
training, these computed activations need to be stored,
as they are also required during the weight update operations for computing the gradients. Therefore, a system
for DNN training has much higher storage requirements
for nonstationary data (i.e., shared memory) than a system for DNN inference. For example, a relatively simple
ResNet-50 DNN model operating on the ImageNet data set
generates ∼50 million activations during the forward pass,
resulting in a 100 MB (2 B/activation) storage requirement per image. The storage requirement further increases
for larger networks, bigger data sets, higher resolution
inputs, and concurrent processing of multiple images due
to pipeline parallelism. For even larger DNNs, the storage
requirements can be multiple gigabytes. Thus, the on-chip
memory of crossbar-based inference accelerators is often
insufficient, and these accelerators have to be accompanied
with high bandwidth and high capacity external memory
to enable a training system that is capable of handling
large-scale DNNs. Consequently, a training system also
requires the traditional memory hierarchy (with multiple levels), to efficiently hide the main memory latency.
Second, while crossbar writes are infrequent in inference
(configuration-time only), the crossbar is written at the
end of every step/batch during the training flow. For
example, a typical ResNet-50 DNN model training on the
ImageNet data set with batch size 64 and 100 epochs
requires 18 million crossbar writes. It is worth noting that
while the architectural solutions, such as larger shared
memory and memory hierarchy, can provision the memory
requirements for training, the expensive crossbar write is a
fundamental impediment toward building crossbar-based
training accelerators.

E. Overcoming Expensive Crossbar Writes for
Training
Recent research has proposed a parallel write technique,
as discussed in Section II-C, to overcome the fundamental
bottleneck of writing into NVM crossbars. Through this
approach, weight gradient computations and updates can
be performed in situ in resistive crossbars [140]. While
such an in situ and parallel weight update scheme can
mitigate the requirement of expensive crossbar writes in
ML training, they are limited to low-precision operations.
To that effect, researchers have explored bit-slicing to
incorporate parallel write to enable high-precision weight

2) Bits to Handle Overflow: For MVM, the matrix weights
are inputs to the operation, and they do not change.
In contrast, for weight update, the matrix weights are
accumulated with the values resulting from the outer product. As a result, the weight slice stored in a crossbar cell
may overflow, either from multiple accumulations within
one weight update or over multiple OPAs. We handle this
overflow by provisioning weight slices with additional bits
to store the carry [shaded bits shown in Fig. 27(d)].
Propagating carry bits to other slices would require
serial reads and writes that incur high overhead. For this
reason, we do not propagate the carry bits immediately.
Instead, they are kept in the slice and participate in future
MVM and weight update operations on the crossbar.
The carry bits cannot be kept in the weight slice
indefinitely because, eventually, the weight slice may get
saturated, i.e., crossbar cell at maximum/minimum state
for positive/negative update. Saturation is detrimental
to trainability (desirable loss reduction during training)
because it freezes training progress due to the absence
of weight change. For this reason, we employ a periodic
carry resolution step (CRS) that executes infrequently
to perform carry propagation using serial reads and
writes.
3) Number of Slices Versus Bits Per Slice: When slicing
matrix bits across multiple crossbars, there is a tradeoff
between the number of slices and the number of bits per
cell in each slice. MVM operations favor using more slices
and fewer bits per slice. The reason is that energy increases
linearly with the number of crossbars and nonlinearly
with the precision of a crossbar due to the increase in
ADC precision required supporting it. Therefore, using
more slices with fewer bits each is better for energy
consumption.

G. Challenges in ReRam-Based Accelerators
Despite their energy-benefits, further improvements in
ReRam-based accelerators are impeded by costly ADCs
required in MVMUs. Typical MVMUs used in ML accelerators use 8–10-bit ADCs to guarantee correct dot-product
operation, i.e., no degradation from quantization during the analog-to-digital conversion. Such high-resolution
ADCs consume significantly higher energy and area compared with the resistive crossbar, thereby dominating the
energy and area distribution of an MVMU (as shown
in Fig. 30). Recent research has explored microarchitecture
optimization for reducing the ADC overhead by dynamically scaling the ADC precision requirements [145]. Here,
the lower bits of input and weight data use lower ADC
precision, whereas the higher bits of input and weight
data use high ADC precision for their crossbar operations. The relationship between the ADC energy versus
the effective number of bits (ENOB) represented by the
ADC has also been explored by researchers [36]. Further
research on approximate computing to reduce ADC precision in certain layers of the ML model, domain-specific
ADC designs for ML, and ADC designs at scaled technology nodes will be instrumental toward improving
ReRam-based accelerators. In addition to overcoming
the impact of peripheral overheads, further research on
enhancing the reliability of parallel write for large-sized
crossbars can enable the adoption of ReRam-based training
accelerators.

c) Hardware compensation: The errors in the resistive
crossbars can also be compensated by a hardware technique [33]. The outputs of the resistive crossbars, in this
case, are multiplied by a compensation factor (CF) to produce close to ideal outputs. CF is calculated by measuring
the R.E between ideal output current (Iideal ) and nonideal

be divided into two steps: 1) design time calibration phase
and 2) runtime mitigation phase. In the calibration phase,
the host processor to the resistive crossbar accelerator computes CFs based on the R.E between output obtained from
the processor (ideal) and output obtained from resistive
crossbars (nonideal) for a characterization data set. The
computed CFs are then locally stored in the compensation
logic of each crossbar. In the mitigation phase, the compensation logic produces CFs for each column, and the
output current computed from the crossbar is expressed as

2) Training: Training a DNN on the hardware can
potentially address the functional errors arising from the
read nonidealities and fixed-point arithmetic. The inherent adaptability of the gradient descent algorithm can be
leveraged in this aspect. This is the reason why retraining
DNNs using software models of crossbars is the most
popular technique to recover accuracy degradation during inference. However, although such a technique has
proven to account for the device- and circuit-level nonidealities to produce a more noise-resilient DNN, hardware
shortcoming cannot be exhaustively mitigated using these
methods.
a) Algorithmic Compensation: The limited ON / OFF ratio
in devices and dynamic range of inputs and outputs require
algorithmic techniques to circumvent. This is achieved
through normalization of the activations and scaling the
limited weight ranges appropriately [158]. Another problem of DNN training on hardware is the large difference between the forward pass inputs and backward pass
errors, which increases with layer depth. To address this
problem, researchers have proposed noise management
techniques, such as normalization of inputs and rescaling
of outputs [159].
The compensation techniques to circumvent erroneous
computations in analog computing primitives have proven
to be very effective. It indicates that analog computing
system design is strongly tied to the application, and algorithmic modifications need to be performed to truly realize
the potential of resistive crossbars as a suitable candidate
for such systems. Some of these algorithmic compensations
may require digital logic for on-chip compensation. This
shows that resistive crossbar-based approximate computing systems need rigorous hardware–software codesign to
be viable as future AI hardware.

VI. O U T L O O K
NVM crossbars are promising candidates for accelerating
ML workloads with the potential of achieving orders of
magnitude higher area and energy efficiency over traditional CMOS computing systems. The area efficiency
arises from the ability of NVM devices of storing multiple bits within a cell area of 4F2 (12F2 considering
1T-1R cells [160]) compared with 124F2 occupied by six
transistor SRAM cells [134]. Such high storage densities,
coupled with their ability of in-memory MVM operations,
enable dataflow architectures with spatially distributed
NVM crossbars. The weights in most neural networks
can be entirely mapped onto these spatial architectures,
thus eliminating the need for costly DRAM accesses that
plagues the CMOS computing systems currently. The inherent parallelism within the MVM units as well as their
spatial arrangement leads to significantly lower energy and
latency consumption than state-of-the-art CMOS digital
systems.
Despite significant developments in the domain of NVM
crossbar-based computing, there are several challenges at

various levels of design that need to be addressed. We have
described how device- and circuit-level nonidealities can
impact the MVM functionality of NVM crossbars, severely
affecting the application accuracy. Although accurate crossbar models and scrupulous functional frameworks can
help us build compensation techniques to mitigate the
detrimental effects, NVM crossbars face challenges of data
reliability and endurance. PCM and RRAM technologies
suffer from limited endurance issues (107 cycles) [161].
Moreover, resistance drifting phenomenon [162] in PCM
technology can affect data retention, which can have a
detrimental effect on NVM crossbars when used as MVM
units. Data retention can severely affect neural network
inference operations as the weights can vary over time.
Limited endurance, on the other hand, can affect neural
network training due to a limited number of reliable
write operations. These challenges imply that significant
material research is necessary to make NVM technology
viable for ML workloads.
In addition to data retention and endurance issues,
the impact of device variability still remains a major challenge. The dynamic nature of these variations can produce
unpredictable and undesirable MVM outputs that may vary
remarkably within the crossbars and across the crossbars.
Under the effect of device variations, the conductance
ON / OFF ratio can pose a limitation on the number of
rows that can be simultaneously activated, hence limiting
the crossbar size. A high ON / OFF ratio is, thus, desirable
for enabling larger crossbars so that the MVM computations have enough sense margins for appropriate sensing
through the ADCs. Another implication of device variations
is that they induce noise into the MVM computations, thus
reducing the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) for proper sensing
in the ADCs. In such a scenario, the need for high precision ADCs can become redundant. In summary, device
variations play a crucial role in governing NVM crossbar
designs. The origin of these variations has been studied
in detailed for various technologies [163], [164]. Their
impact, however, has been studied at an array level by simple statistical modeling. There is a need to perform detailed
system-level modeling to truly understand its effects on
DNN performance. Moreover, to leverage the inherent
resiliency of DNNs toward erroneous computations, algorithmic techniques should be explored to overcome the
effect of variations.
Besides the functional accuracy of NVM crossbars,
another glaring issue that plagues the application of
the technology for ML workloads is the extremely high
energy and area consumption of ADCs. This effectively increases the energy consumption of the NVM
crossbar, reducing its benefits over contemporary digital systems. Researchers have proposed using low precision ADCs [57], which induces approximations in the

computations and leverages the inherent resiliency of the
DNNs. In addition, ML workloads have a considerable
amount of sparsity in both inputs and weights. Moreover,
the bit-serial nature of computations in crossbars can
further increase the degree of sparsity. Previous works
have focused on structured sparsity [165]–[167]. Such
sparsity can reduce the ADC requirements for every array,
remarkably reducing the energy consumption of NVM
crossbars. Moreover, as the MVM computations are inherently approximate, ADCs with lower SNR can be sufficient
for reasonably accurate conversions. Thus, ADC design is a
crucial piece of the puzzle for realizing the area and energy
efficiency promised by NVM crossbars. Detailed functional analysis is necessary to determine the ADC requirements considering the sparse and approximate nature of
computing.
Finally, to meet the growing demands of compute and
storage for future applications, NVM crossbars need to
follow the scaling trends that have been a key reason for
the success of CMOS systems. Scaling can be achieved by
reducing the individual cell sizes as well as the reduction
of interconnect and peripheral circuitry. However, different
kinds of scaling have their own challenges. Device scaling
can lead to more variability, while interconnect scaling may
lead to an increased effect of parasitics. With careful crossbar design, larger crossbars can also lead to the sharing
of peripheral circuitry, thus reducing the overall area of
the chip. Moreover, mixed-signal CMOS peripherals need
to be scaled effectively to improve the area efficiency of the
array. Low-resistance interconnect needs to be explored to
reduce the impact of parasitics. Finally, following CMOS
3-D stacking trends, NVM devices can also achieve higher
density by horizontal and vertical 3-D stacking through
improved lithography techniques. Such 3-D RRAM arrays
have been demonstrated experimentally in the recent past.
In summary, technology scaling is a key component of
the development of NVM crossbars for future computing
systems.
NVM crossbars have been heavily explored as approximate MVM units for ML workloads. Extensive research
across the stack of devices, circuits, and architectures has
exploited the rich device physics to perform MVM computations in high-density NVM arrays and build large scale
systems using these analog computing cores. Despite the
promises, significant effort needs to be put to overcome
the challenges of variability that cripples the analog nature
of computing. Furthermore, to truly deliver the promises
of energy and area efficiency offered by this technology, hardware–software codesign needs to be performed.
Through thorough and directed research in materials,
devices, circuits, and architectures, in-memory computing
with NVM crossbars can become an integral part of the
future computing industry.
