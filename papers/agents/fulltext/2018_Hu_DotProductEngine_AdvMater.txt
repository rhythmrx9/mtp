Using memristor crossbar arrays to accelerate computations is a promising
approach to efficiently implement algorithms in deep neural networks. Early
demonstrations, however, are limited to simulations or small-scale problems
primarily due to materials and device challenges that limit the size of the
memristor crossbar arrays that can be reliably programmed to stable and
analog values, which is the focus of the current work. High-precision analog
tuning and control of memristor cells across a 128 × 64 array is demonstrated, and the resulting vector matrix multiplication (VMM) computing
precision is evaluated. Single-layer neural network inference is performed
in these arrays, and the performance compared to a digital approach is
assessed. Memristor computing system used here reaches a VMM accuracy
equivalent of 6 bits, and an 89.9% recognition accuracy is achieved for the
10k MNIST handwritten digit test set. Forecasts show that with integrated
(on chip) and scaled memristors, a computational efficiency greater than
100 trillion operations per second per Watt is possible.

Crossbars built out of memristors offer a scalable nanotechnology[26] with broad conductance tunability,[27] and various
bioneurological qualities such as stochasticity and transient
plasticity.[28,29] In order to be implemented practically, demonstrations of sufficient memristor crossbar yield, repeatability,
and controllability are required for these targeted applications.
Many recent works have explored using memristor crossbar
arrays for computation, particularly in neural network applications.[30–43] These works considered various memristor technologies, but predominantly chalcogenide phase change memories
(PCM) and transition metal-oxide memristors. However, the
majority of these works relied heavily on simulations to forecast
performance and accuracy for computations within crossbars,
without demonstrating actual computations within memristor
crossbars. In many cases, the coupled nonlinearities, sneak
currents, and other circuit issues were ignored, which are
critical to consider when assessing the promise of memristors
for computation. The few previous experimental works in full
memristor arrays have been limited to small sizes (<1024 memristors), binary device states, or exhibited limitations on reconfigurability. Large neural networks (165 000 synapses) were
demonstrated with PCM arrays,[42] but this work was limited by
a sequential interface and could not carry out VMM computations in a single step with access to all word-lines and bit-lines
simultaneously, as would be required for an actual computational accelerator. Face recognition with a 128 × 8 1T1R array
was demonstrated with online learning,[41] and sparse encoding
was demonstrated within small memristor arrays.[40] However,
neither work demonstrated or reported computations within
the array, nor crucially how the VMM computational accuracy
relates to the final performance of the application. The current lack of understanding for how materials, device, and circuit issues relate to a memristor array’s computing capability
is a major obstacle for designers attempting to simulate and
develop complex architectures utilizing memristor crossbar
arrays for real computational applications.
In this work, we present a platform for reprogrammable
analog computations with integrated transistor-memristor
arrays that we call the Dot Product Engine (DPE). Our platform enables exploration of dense VMM computations with
arbitrary target matrices converted directly from digital software algorithms. Further, our material stack design for the
1T1M memristor cells enables the precise analog tuning of
the memristor conductance over a wide range to implement
these arbitrary target matrices faithfully. Our approach differs
from online trained implementations[34,41] in which the derived
matrix values are trained specifically for that crossbar array’s
circuit properties. Instead, our approach enables general acceleration of any matrix operations, taking digital input vectors
and matrices, converting into the analog domain for low power,
high speed computation, and then providing digital outputs.
We demonstrate VMM within memristor arrays up to 128 × 64
in size at 10 MHz, yielding over 16 000 multiplications and
additions in a single clock step, and leading to a forecasted performance of about 115 trillion operations per second per Watt.
The resulting VMM outputs have 6 bits of precision, and we
demonstrate multiple reprogramming iterations of our memristor arrays for different applications. Finally, we implement a

single-layer neural network to classify the MNIST database of
handwritten characters, showing 89.9% recognition accuracy.
The DPE system was designed to perform precise programming of individual memristor cells, reprogramming of the
memristor conductance matrix, and, critically, VMM computation in a single step within the memristor crossbar. The
VMM computation is performed by applying an input vector
of voltages to the rows as brief simultaneously pulses (Ohm’s
law) and the resulting summed currents (Kirchhoff’s law) are
collected along the columns (Figure 1a). These currents are
converted to a voltage (via a transimpedance amplifier, or TIA)
and measured after a short delay from the input voltage pulses.
The memristor array is programmed so that the individual conductances of the memristor cells comprise the desired computational kernel, and as memristors are nonvolatile, the memristor array maintains the programmed computational kernel.
As many applications such as neural network inference do not
require frequent reprogramming, the memristor crossbars can
be programmed once and then ignored. However, a new application can easily be implemented by simply reprogramming
the memristor conductance matrix values and supplying the
appropriate inputs to the rows, as we will demonstrate below.
Within the memristor array, we utilize one transistor-one
memristor (1T1M) cells in which the transistor limits and
controls current during programming to facilitate high accuracy programming but is left fully open in computation mode.
The CMOS–memristor integration is carried out in a foundrycompatible back-end-of-the-line (BEOL) process (Figure 1b). A
transition metal-oxide memristor layer is deposited and patterned atop CMOS access transistors and wiring with 2.0 µm
technology. While such 1T1M integration can increase the area
compared to purely passive crossbar arrays,[34] even 1T1M-based
architectures can reduce silicon area compared to purely digital
approaches.[11] Passive or 1S1R arrays typically utilized to provide a degree of isolation during programming through device
nonlinearity are not suitable for many VMM applications, as
the nonlinearity directly invalidates the utilization of Ohm’s
law and reduces the programming accuracy of analog levels,
substantially compromising VMM computation accuracy. Our
memristor device stack consists of Ta/HfO2/Pd (cross-section
shown in Figure 1b) and has been developed to provide multilevel conductances implemented by continuous tuning of the
chemical composition of a Ta-rich conduction channel within
the switching matrix.[44,45] To achieve constant resistance under
different voltages (i.e., a linear IV relationship), we selected a
resistance range of 1.1–10.0 kΩ (or conductance of 100–900 µS)
for accurate analog VMM operation.[46] With other memristor
material systems, we previously demonstrated experimentally
that 64 reprogrammable conductance levels (6 bits) can be
achieved in individual 1T1M cells.[27] Here, we extend that work
from individual cells to full arrays constituting thousands of
cells by programming a 128 × 64 array to display the Hewlett
Packard Enterprise logo and mapping the greyscale logo image
to ≈180 distinct memristor conductance levels. All programming and computational signals are generated from peripheral printed circuit boards (PCBs) connected to the memristor
arrays through probe-card connections (Figure S1, Supporting
Information), and the design allows for an expandable set of
row and column boards that can simultaneously drive (or read)

Utilizing the DPE platform, we are able to individually access
and precisely tune memristor cell conductances (Figure 2a)
in an open-loop mode with single pulses (less precise and
accurate) or with feedback (more precise and accurate).[27] The
programming algorithm (Figure 2b) is applied across every
cell in the entire array with high efficiency. Memristor cells
were fabricated with chemically inert bottom row electrodes
and reactive top column electrodes, so a positive voltage from
row to column induces a RESET operation on the memristor
cell (Figure 1b), while a positive voltage from column to row
drives a SET operation. A selector line corresponding to the
gates of the transistors in a selected column is biased at different voltages to enable different current compliances for
the access transistors. All other unselected rows and columns
remain floating during individual programming. In Figure 2a,
the typical memristor switching curves are obtained by
applying a sequence of rising voltage amplitude pulses, with
lower voltage (0.2 V) read pulses applied in between to determine the resulting cell conductance (read gate voltage is fixed
to 5 V, putting transistors in the linear region). By varying
the gate voltage applied during the programming pulses, the
maximal current through the device is changed, controlling the
final conductance state during SET operations. More details on
programming and reading operations are provided in the Supporting Information.

To improve the accuracy of our analog VMM computations, each column output is linearly calibrated to account
for the above circuit parasitics. The calibration parameters
are determined by first running one hundred test input patterns and finding the linear scaling that best matches the
expected results. This is a one-time linear tuning of the TIA

circuit in each column, without any performance overhead.
When necessary, an additional calibration process can also
include compensation to reduce the impact of very high or
low conductance defects, as discussed in Note S4 in the Supporting Information. A simple linear scaling reduces errors
significantly, as shown in Figure 3e. Here, it is shown that
the final error for the DPE system is typically well below
50 µS and corresponds to ≈6 bits of VMM output accuracy.
This is expected to improve further with state-of-the-art fabrication that has higher device yield (fewer defects), lower wire
resistances, and by operating memristors at lower conductance states.
Here, we experimentally demonstrate a single-layer neural
network inference application with the DPE for handwriting
recognition. Neural networks are a key application of interest
for acceleration,[6,11,20,34,38] and this application is characterized
by frequent reuse of matrix convolution kernels (and thus an
advantage of using memristors by reducing data-fetching), tolerance for defects, and reduced precision requirements.[50–52]
We program a software-trained single-layer network in a 96 × 40
portion of a 128 × 64 memristor crossbar for handwritten digit
recognition on the full 10 000 MNIST dataset.[53] To our knowledge, this is the largest demonstration utilizing memristor
crossbars in hardware to date. Implementing this single-layer
neural network in the DPE platform requires reshaping and
partitioning of the neural network weight matrix and input
images. A software single-layer network for MNIST classifies 784 pixel inputs (28 × 28) into 10 possible classes (0 to 9),
yielding a network size of 7840 weights. To fit this network into
a smaller array capable of supporting 4096 elements, we resize
the MNIST images to 19 × 20 and retrain the weight matrix
in software. Ideally (without reshaping), this implementation
would use a 380 × 10 array, but we reshape this into an equivalent 96 × 40 array. The overall recognition accuracy of the neural
network in software did not degrade due to the image resizing,
remaining at 92.4%. To perform the digit classification using
the DPE, input images are processed by unwrapping the image

into four sets of 96 values (four zeroes are added to the end of

trained weight matrix (right side of Figure 4a). Only ten output
columns are needed for each of the four sets, performing the
partial synaptic weight matrix multiplication. A full-image classification is concluded after four such computations, resulting

Our experimental demonstration of inference on the 10k
test patterns from MNIST shows the promise of our hardware
acceleration. The full recognition accuracy for all 10k testing
patterns as a function of the input digit shows that our hardware implementation can even outperform the software-trained
network (for digits 3,0, see Figure 4c). With linear correction,
the accuracy of the hardware DPE neural network implementation reaches 89.9%, only a 2.5% reduction compared to the
ideal software accuracy. This remaining accuracy loss is due to
device programming inaccuracies and cumulative finite wire
resistances across the array. It is expected that more faithful
array programming, fewer memristor cell defects, and especially the use of multilayer neural network implementations
will help close the accuracy gap to software levels (see multilayer simulations[54]). Additionally, using nonlinearity in the
activation function would be expected to increase performance
further. Our results directly computed the forward inference
using existing trained neural networks, showing that these may
be utilized directly without redesigning or retraining for the
particular hardware. This is well-matched to the paradigms of
IoT and Edge computing, in which the goal is to deploy and
use trained networks in mobile applications where low power
and low cost (small chip area) are key, but speed and performance cannot be compromised. Additionally, in such applications, a reduced precision can often be tolerated. Consequently,
a key figure of merit is the computational power efficiency of
the system. The present design, including power consumption
in the peripheral circuitry, is estimated to yield 115 TOPS W−1
(Tera operations per second per Watt, see Table S2 in the Supporting Information).
In this work, we have experimentally validated the potential
for analog computing in nonvolatile memristor crossbar arrays.
We demonstrated VMM computations in arrays up to 128 × 64,
supporting the assumptions in architectures showing significant
acceleration of neural network and signal processing computations compared to digital implementations.[11,54,55] We further
showed that multiple, stable conductance levels can be realized
in 1T1M cells composed of Ta/HfO2 memristors with 6 bits
of precision. Given the low-precision requirements in many
applications,[50–52] this exceeds the demands considerably. We
showed that large memristor crossbars can carry out singlestep VMM operations in-memory leading to high-throughput
and low-energy consumption. The computing accuracy was
shown to be acceptable for machine learning applications
including image recognition, and we also demonstrated reprogrammability for multiple applications. Direct implementation
of the forward inference of a neural network for MNIST image
recognition was shown, yielding 89.9% accuracy for a single
layer. Reducing stuck-ON cells and using multilayer neural
networks are expected to increase the accuracy to state of the
art.[56]

Adv. Mater. 2018, 30, 1705914

A key question is how the power efficiency of such an analog
DPE system compares to the state of the art, both in performing
VMM computations and more broadly in convolutional neural
network applications. This requires forecasting the DPE when
implemented in an integrated chip and using scaled technology
nodes. Although the present system operates at 10 MHz, this is
due to the larger parasitics of 2 µm technology and probecard
interfaces. By assuming <100 nm technology and integrated
control electronics, an operating frequency of 150 MHz is easily
achievable, along with analog–digital converters (ADCs) operating at <133 µW[57] per column channel. Given the 16 320 multiplications and additions performed in a 128 × 64 array, this
leads to a computational efficiency of 115 TOPS W−1 (Tera Operations per Second per Watt). In comparison, digital technology
performing the same VMM operations at only 4-bit accuracy
using 40 nm CMOS is estimated to operate at 7 TOPS W−1.[40]
In addition, the high power and area cost of ADCs could be
alleviated by only applying ADCs to final outputs of multilayer
neural networks. For a large family of neural network algorithms, ADCs could also be replaced with simple circuits such
as threshold gates, comparators, or amplifiers, even further
reducing DPE power and area.
The above shows the power efficiency in replacing digital
VMM blocks with analog, memristor-based DPE circuits. An
important question is whether such computational efficiency
is maintained at the application level, for example in full convolutional neural network (CNN) inference where a streaming
input of images is classified. In an earlier architectural study
and performance estimation,[11] we evaluated CNN applications computed by a DPE-based system composed of many
128 × 128 arrays, each with only 2 bits per cell and a 10 MHz
operating frequency. All ADC elements, data routing, and buffering are taken into account, along with the costs in breaking
down the larger images across multiple arrays. The results show
a 14.8x, 5.5x, and 7.5x improvement in throughput (inferences
per second), energy, and computational density (inferences
per second per chip area) over the leading Digital ASIC implementation[6] for the same task. Thus, the present work experimentally validates the assumptions for that architectural study,
providing a base-line for future analog computing systems and
the potential to accelerate and significantly lower energy consumption for important applications.

Experimental Section
Transistor and Memristor Integration: An array of NMOS transistors
were fabricated in a commercial fab with low wire resistance. The
transistors had a feature size of 2 µm. The memristor arrays were
fabricated in a university clean room aligned to the underlying
transistors following an argon plasma to remove protective metal-oxide
layers. Photolithography patterning was used, along with thin-film
deposition and liftoff. Sputter deposition of 5 nm silver (Ag) and 200 nm
palladium (Pd) was used for the metal vias. After lifting-off in warm
acetone, the sample was annealed at 300 °C for 30 min in nitrogen
with a flow of 20 sccm. A 60 nm Pd layer with a 5 nm tantalum (Ta)
adhesive layer was sputtered to serve as the bottom electrode. A 5 nm
HfO2 switching layer was deposited by atomic layer deposition using
water and tetrakis(dimethylamido)hafnium as precursors at 250 °C,
to ensure high film quality and step coverage. The patterning of the
switching layer was done by photolithography and reactive ion etch

Control Electronics and Chip Interface: A dedicated system of PCB
was designed and manufactured to accomplish all memristor state
programming (reading and writing) as well as computational operations.
The novel operating mode for computations involved simultaneous
driving of all rows in the array along with simultaneous reading of the
currents in all columns. The designed PCB system was able to perform
these operations with driving pulses from 164 ns to greater than 4 µs.
Table S1 (Supporting Information) had detailed specifications.
Interfacing of the control PCB system to the memristor arrays was
accomplished on a probe station with a specially designed cantilever
probecard system for high-speed signal transmission and the flexibility to
test and operate new memristor chips without the need for wire-bonding.
Images of the setup were available in the Supporting Information. All
system configurations and final result readout were performed by a
control computer communicating with a microcontroller in the PCB
system. The microcontroller supported a small DPE instruction set,
receiving configuration instructions to control the column and row
boards for different write or read operations at arbitrary positions in the
crossbar array. Communication with the computer limited all operation
times, and to achieve higher speed and efficiency, instructions for fully
array-level read and write operations were also implemented in the
firmware. In this way, for example, one array-level read command by the
firmware would read all devices in an array (more than 8000 devices)
in less than 2 s. A graphical user interface, data visualization scripts,
programming scripts, and computing scripts were developed to support
all operations described in this work.
Single-Layer Neural Network for MNIST Classification: The singlelayer neural network was trained in MATLAB using softmax regression.
Softmax is a simple and well-suited model for MNIST pattern
recognition since there were only ten possible classifications. 60k images
from the MNIST database were used for training, employing gradient
descent to find the target synaptic weights related to the cost function
defined in softmax regression. When the trained network was used for
classification, outputs were exponentiated and normalized to generate a
probability distribution. This last step was not implemented in the DPE
since the present memristor crossbars would not directly compute the
nonlinear function.
