YULHWA KIM and HYUNGJUN KIM, Pohang University of Science and Technology, South Korea
JAE-JOON KIM, Seoul National University, South Korea
In Analog Computing-in-Memory (CIM) neural network accelerators, analog-to-digital converters (ADCs)
are required to convert the analog partial sums generated from a CIM array to digital values. The overhead
from ADCs substantially degrades the energy efficiency of CIM accelerators so that previous works attempted
to lower the ADC resolution considering the distribution of the partial sums. Despite the efforts, the required
ADC resolution still remains relatively high. In this article, we propose the data-driven partial sum quantization scheme, which exhaustively searches for the optimal quantization range with little computational
burden. We also report that analyzing the characteristics of the partial sum distributions at each layer gives
an additional information to further reduce the ADC resolution compared to previous works that mostly used
the characteristics of the partial sum distributions of the entire network. Based on the finer-level data-driven
approach combined with retraining, we present a methodology for extreme partial-sum quantization. Experimental results show that the proposed method can reduce the ADC resolution to 2 to 3 bits for CIFAR-10
dataset, which is the smaller ADC bit resolution than any previous CIM-based NN accelerators.

INTRODUCTION

Processing of deep neural network (DNN) requires a large amount of data transfer and computation, so hardware accelerators for efficient DNN processing are being actively studied. Recently,
computing-in-memory (CIM) hardware is garnering attention as a new computing platform
for DNNs [2, 15, 20]. CIM hardware uses memory arrays as both data storage and processing
units, thus minimizing data transfer from memory to other processing units. As a result, unlike
the conventional Von-Neumann architecture, CIM hardware can avoid memory bottleneck due to
the large amount of data transfer.
While CIM accelerators have the potential as an efficient computing platform for DNNs, they
meet several challenges due to the nature of analog processing inside the memory array. Since the
digital logic is typically used outside the array, digital-to-analog converter (DAC) is used to
feed input values to analog CIM arrays. The input resolution of CIM arrays is limited to the DAC
resolution, and CIM-based NN accelerators need to handle 2- to 8-bit input values to process general NNs [4, 13, 20]. However, area and energy overhead of DAC is proportional to its resolution,
and hence multi-bit DACs degrade the area and energy efficiency of CIM accelerators. Therefore,
to support bit-scalable computation with low-resolution DAC, most of CIM accelerators process
input values in bit-serial manner [8, 15, 20]. In this way, the resolution of DAC can be lowered
to 1 bit, and CIM arrays can utilize word-line drivers of typical memory arrays to feed input
values.
CIM accelerators also use analog-to-digital converter (ADC) to interface the analog
multiplication-accumulation (MAC) results inside the CIM array to external digital logics. The
MAC results typically represent the partial sums. Since partial sums are high-resolution values,
CIM accelerators require high-resolution ADCs. The overhead of ADCs also increase as their resolution increases so that ADCs significantly degrade area and energy efficiency of CIM accelerators [2, 15, 20]. Meanwhile, partial sums need to be quantized if low-resolution ADCs are used,
and hence there is a great need for efficient and accurate partial-sum quantization algorithm to
reduce the ADC overhead.
As a result, several previous works developed partial-sum quantization algorithms for CIMbased NN accelerators [1, 12, 17]. Previous works tried to reduce the mean-square-error (MSE)
of partial-sum quantization and relied on the min/max values or standard deviation (σ ) of the partial sum distributions to decide the quantization range [1, 12, 17]. However, due to non-linearity
introduced in NN computation, minimization of MSE does not guarantee the minimization of NN
accuracy degradation, and the min/max or the 3σ of partial sum distribution does not represent
the quantization range properly. Therefore, previous approaches result in sub-optimal partial-sum
quantization results, and higher than 4-bit ADCs are needed to obtain reliable NN accuracy.
In this article, we propose a methodology for more aggressive partial-sum quantization. To design more accurate partial-sum quantization with lower resolution, we first investigate the characteristics of partial sum distributions in bit-scalable CIM hardware. Based on the observation that it
is important to quantize the partial sum values near 0 more accurately to reduce NN accuracy loss,
we propose a new type of quantizer. Then, the quantizer is further optimized with a data-driven
method that finds the better quantization range than previous methods that used 3σ of partial sum
distribution. Finally, we demonstrate that retraining NNs with partial-sum quantization recovers
significant amount of the NN accuracy loss, so that we can achieve extreme quantization of partial
sums.
The key contributions of this article are as follows:
(1) We propose the zero-tread quantizer that reduces the quantization error near 0 values based
on the extensive analysis of partial sum distributions in bit-scalable CIM accelerators.

BACKGROUND

2.1 NN Accelerators with Analog CIM Array
Analog CIM accelerators conduct MAC operations on the memory array, and they have potential to
reduce massive amount of data movement and enable parallel computations. In the CIM array, the
resolution of input and weight values are fixed when the bit resolution of the DAC and a memory
cell are decided (Figure 1 (left)) [2]. However, the resolution of input neurons and synaptic weight
values may vary depending on the network architecture and target tasks, so general CIM-based
NN accelerators need to support bit-scalable computation. To overcome the hardware limitation
and support bit-scalable operations, bit-scalable CIM arrays (Figure 1 (right)) have been widely
used [15, 20].
In the bit-scalable CIM accelerators, input values are processed in bit-serial manner and weight
values are processed in bit-parallel manner. First, rather than mapping input values to multi-level
input voltage, bit-scalable CIM arrays receive 0/1 values for n cycles to process an n-bit input.
Since the binary values of input bitplanes are fed to CIM arrays at each cycle, the bit-scalable CIM
accelerators can replace a n-bit DAC to an 1-bit word-line driver that is used for conventional
memory array. Next, weight values are stored in the memory cells, and the bit-parallel mode use
multiple cells to represent multi-bit weight values (Figure 1 (middle)). For example, when each
memory cell can represent 1-bit value, 4 memory cells are used to represent 4-bit weight value. As
different weight bitplanes should be accumulated separately in the analog domain, memory cells
for different bitplanes should be allocated in different columns or different CIM arrays.
Although the bit-scalability issue of CIM accelerators are resolved by the bit-serial input/bitparallel weight computation, still there are some remaining challenges in CIM accelerator design.
One of the most important challenges is area and energy overhead caused by peripheral circuits

between analog CIM array and external digital system. The area and energy consumption of an
ADC increases significantly as its resolution increases, so high-resolution ADC seriously affects
the overall efficiency of the entire systems [18]. Conventional CIM accelerators adopted bit-serial
input processing to eliminate DAC overhead, but ADCs are still required to convert partial sums
of analog CIM array and the partial sums are high-resolution values [15, 20]. Thus, it is hard to
lower the resolution of ADC, and conventional CIM accelerators use multi-bit ADCs in spite of
the overhead. As a result, the overhead of ADCs seriously degrade the energy efficiency of CIM
accelerators.
In the fully digital CIM arrays, ADCs are not needed [3] as the MAC operation is conducted
with digital circuits. However, the digital-based CIM array requires multiple read (as many times
as the number of rows in a array) from the memory array, and it also needs digital MAC operation
while an analog-based CIM array enable matrix-vector multiplication with a single read operation.
Therefore, the digital approach introduces much more area/energy/latency overhead compared to
the analog approach, and there is a great need to lower the resolution of ADC in the analog CIM
arrays to achieve full potential of CIM accelerators.
2.2 ADCs and Partial-Sum Quantization
In analog CIM accelerators, ADCs are used to convert analog partial sums generated from each
memory array into digital values. The partial sums are the cumulative values of the multiplication
result that occurs in each memory cell, so their resolution tends to be high. Let us assume that
the row count of the memory array is Nr ow ; we use [0, 1] for binary input values and [−1, 1]
for binary weight values as in typical bit-scalable CIM arrays [15, 20]. The possible multiplication
results between an input and a weight are [−1, 0, 1]; the multiplication results from several cells are
accumulated in each column of the array. As the number of memory cell connected to a column
is the same as the row count, the possible range of partial sum is [−Nr ow , +Nr ow ]. Thus, when
the row count is 128, the possible range of partial sum is [−128, 128]; 8-bit ADCs are required for
one-to-one conversion.
Since area and energy consumption of such high-resolution ADCs are much larger than those of
other circuit components, the ADCs degrade the efficiency of CIM accelerators significantly. Thus,
ADC resolution needs to be lowered to solve theses problems, and there are several works tried to
lower the ADC resolution. While the naive method requires 8-bit ADCs, ISAAC [15] reduced the
resolution by 1 bit by flipping the weights if the sum of weights in a column is large. This method
still guarantees one-to-one conversion of the partial sums, but if we further reduce the resolution
of ADC without any change in the NN algorithm, then there exits a gap between the analog partial
sum values and the digitized partial sum values. The gap is typically called the quantization error.
Thus, to lower the resolution of ADC, we need to design an algorithm to reduce the quantization
error.
The quantization algorithms can be categorized into two classes: uniform quantization and nonuniform quantization. In the uniform quantization, the intervals between quantization edges are
the same (Figure 2(a) (left)). It can be implemented with a typical ADC that generates voltage
level of each quantization edge by dividing a single reference voltage Vr ef with a resistor ladder
(Figure 2(a) (right)). However, the non-uniform quantization has no restriction on the size of intervals (Figure 2(b) (left)). This type of quantization adaptively determines the quantization edges
taking into account the distribution of partial sums so that it can store more information in the
quantized values compared to the uniform quantization. Non-uniform quantization requires special ADCs that can program voltage level of each quantization edge separately (Figure 2(b) (right)).

2.3

Previous Works on Partial-Sum Quantization

There are several previous works that tried to lower ADC resolution with partial-sum quantization [1, 12, 17]. Previous works found that the distribution of the partial sums does not typically
cover the theoretical maximum range [−Nr ow , +Nr ow ], and they optimized the partial-sum quantizer with various approaches based on the finding. NeuroSim [12] tried to minimize MSE of overall
partial sums with Lloyd-Max algorithm. LBW [1] and EEQ [17] defined quantization range, which
is the input range of the quantizer, and divided the quanitzation range with pre-defined rule and
given resolution. LBW [1] defined the quantization range with theoretical min/max values of partial sums; EEQ [17] used [μ − 3σ , μ + 3σ ] (μ, σ : mean, standard deviation of partial sums) as the
quantization range. The methods assumed that the partial sums follow the Gaussian distribution.
We summarized the previous works related to lowering ADC resolution and partial-sum quantization in Table 1. Previous algorithms were mostly evaluated on the relatively easy task, image
classification on the CIFAR-10 dataset, and the results show that 4- to 6-bit ADCs are required to
maintain NN accuracy. In this article, we analyze the characteristics of partial sums in depth and
find that using Gaussian distribution for partial sum has limitation. To overcome the limitation and
reduce the ADC resolution more aggressively, we propose a data-driven partial-sum quantization
method. In addition, we provide evaluation results on a more complex task, image classficiation
on the ImageNet dataset.

quantization resolution so that the number of conversion levels becomes odd. If we divide the
quantization range with the odd number of levels, then we can generate a quantization interval
that is flat near zero. Then, we abandon one level in the outer-most interval in the negative region.
The zero-tread quantizer assigns one less level to the negative region, but this approach can reduce
the quantization error for near 0 input values on which we put more importance.

Finding the optimal δ for the partial sum quantization has different characteristics from finding the optimal quantization range for other NN parameters. NN parameters such as weight and
activation are first trained with floating-point (FP) values and then quantized to integer values
for efficient NN inference [4, 6]. Due to the high dynamic range of FP values, there are an infinite
number of candidates for the quantization ranges for weight and activation values. Hence, it is not
possible to find the optimal quantization range using exhaustive search. As a result, the quantizations of weight and activation values are typically done by minimizing the MSE of the parameter
distribution or via complex training process [4]. In contrast, partial sums are integer values defined
in the narrow range [−Nr ow , +Nr ow ] as discussed in Section 2.2, and hence the optimization of the
quantizer for the partial sums is a simple task that finds the optimal value of δ from the finite number of the candidates: integers in the range [1, Nr ow ]. Thus, rather than applying complex training
algorithms to find optimal δ , we iteratively scan and evaluate the NN accuracy with each δ value
using the sub-training dataset.
In addition, optimization of the partial-sum quantizer does not require as many data as conventional NN training. This intuition is based on two factors. First, the quantization range is represented by a single integer parameter δ only. Second, a single image can generate multiple data
samples for the partial sum quantization, because multiple CIM arrays are used to accelerate a single DNN model and each CIM array generates multiple partial sums. Based on the observation, we
compare the quality of the data-driven quantization depending on the size of the sampled dataset.
From the CIFAR-10 training dataset in which 5,000 images are given for each class, we sampled the
equal number of images per class for the experiment. We conducted data-driven quantization with
sampled training images and then measured the inference accuracy of the partial-sum quantized
model with the test dataset to measure the quality of the quantization. We varied the sampled image count per class from 10 to 5,000 and used VGG-9 as the target DNN model. The partial sums
were quantized with 1 to 6 bit resolution. For simplicity, we only quantized the partial sums of the
second layer.

3.3

Evaluation of the Zero-Tread Quantizer and the Data-driven Optimization

We apply the proposed algorithm to VGG-9 [16] and ResNet-18 [5] networks with 2,4,8-bit activation and weight values, respectively. We assume that the NNs are implemented on the analog CIM
accelerators with array row count 128 and the partial sums are quantized with 2- to 6-bit ADCs.
The quantization algorithm is optimized with the proposed data-driven method. We compare the
two quantizer types that we discussed in Section 3.1: zero-tread quantizer and zero-rise quantizer.
For the evaluation, we measure the classification accuracy with each partial-sum quantizer on the
CIFAR-10 dataset. Note that the δ value is selected with sub-training dataset, but the NN accuracy
is measured with the original test dataset throughout the article for fair evaluation.
Combination of the zero-tread quantizer and the data-driven optimization algorithm achieves
reliable NN accuracy with 3- to 4-bit ADCs that are smaller resolution than that of previous works
(Figure 6). The time cost of searching optimal quantization range is varied from 1 minute and
48 seconds to 20 minutes and 42 seconds depending on the bit resolution of the NN parameters
as the cost of emulating bit-serial computation of the CIM accelerators is proportional to the bit
resolution of NN activation and weight values. For the experiment, we used NVIDIA Titan Xp.
Note that the time cost was tolerable in all cases as the data-driven optimization is introduced to
inference operation only.
There are two reasons for the improvement. First, the proposed zero-tread quantizer shows great
improvement on NN accuracy compared to the conventional zero-rise qunatizer. The zero-rise
quantizer is symmetric with respect to the mean of partial sum, so the MSE of the zero-rise qunatizer is usually smaller than that of the zero-tread quantizer. However, the zero-tread quantizer
shows the better NN accuracy than the zero-rise quantizer, which implies that reducing quantization error for input values near 0 is more important than minimizing MSE. Second, previous works
defined the quantization range based on the min/max value or 3σ of partial sums, or MSE of the
quantization error, but we search for the optimal δopt from sub-training dataset. Simulation results
show that the proposed data-driven method finds the optimal quantization range and provides the
smaller NN accuracy loss than the previous partial sum quantization methods (Figure 7).

output with 15 levels. Unlike ADCs for general applications, ADCs for CIM readout do not need to
have the power-of-2 levels. Controlling the resolution with the number of conversion levels rather
than bit resolution allows us to try fine-grained optimization and seek for further area/energy
reduction [14, 19]. Therefore, in this work, we try to optimize the number of conversion levels
instead of bit resolution.

Layerwise Optimization

To investigate more aggressive methodology for partial-sum quantization, we analyze the distribution of partial sums in networkwise and layerwise manner, respectively (Figure 8). The networkwise partial sum distribution shows the Gaussian-like distribution with a mean of zero, but
the distribution of partial sum for each layer varies substantially. The NN layer is the basic unit
that comprise NNs. Each layer extracts different information from the input feature maps, and each
layer can be biased to extract information. Consequently, the variance and shape of the partial-sum
distribution for each layer becomes very different. Previous works and the methodology proposed
in the previous section optimized the partial-sum qunatizer in a networkwise manner. Therefore,
there is a possibility for further improvement if the partial sum quantizer is optimized in a layerwise manner.
When we optimize the quantizer in the networkwise manner, finding the optimal quantization
range [x − δ, x + δ ] is the same as finding optimal δ value, because x is set to 0, the mean of
overall partial sums. When we analyze the the partial sum distributions in a layerwise manner, it
is observed that the mean of partial sums is not always 0. For example, partial sums for the sixth
to seventh layers are not symmetric with respect to 0, so the mean values of these partial sums
are not 0 (Figure 8(b)). If we use the mean value as the center of quantization range, then it can
reduce MSE of quantization, but it turns out that it does not minimize the NN accuracy loss as we
discussed in the previous section.
As both the 0-centered quantizer and the mean-centered quantizer are good candidates for
the quantizer design, we compare the NN accuracy of the two quantizers (Figure 9). Here we
use the number of conversion levels to represent ADC resolution. The evaluation results show
that the 0-centered quantizer achieves the better NN accuracy than the mean-centered quantizer
(Figure 9). This result indicates again that near-0 input values are important for the partial sum
quantization, so setting the center value of the quantizer to be 0 is effective regardless of the mean
values.

EXTREME PARTIAL-SUM QUANTIZATION WITH RETRAINING

4.1 Retraining of NN Parameters
In the previous section, we proposed the methods to design and optimize the partial-sum quantizer.
Distortion in the partial-sum quantization was reduced by the proposed methodgologies, and the
ADC resolution required to prevent NN accuracy loss was significantly reduced. The proposed
methods optimized the quantizer without training of NN parameters, so the optimization process
did not need heavy computational burden. However, as the resolution of the quantizer decreases,
there is a fundamental limit to reduce the quantization error. Therefore, to achieve extreme partialsum quantization, the NN parameters need to be retrained so that the network can adapt to the
new constraints in terms of the partial sum resolution.
In the retraining stage, the partial-sum quantizer optimized with the proposed data-driven approach is applied to the NN model, and the NN parameters such as synaptic weights are trained
to adjust the NN model to recover the accuracy loss due to the partial-sum quantization. As the
term “retraining” indicates, we do not train the parameters from scratch and instead initiate the
training process with the pre-trained parameters. For example, when mapping VGG-9 with 4-bit
activation and weight values to the CIM accelerators, we first adopt the VGG-9 model pre-trained
with conventional activation and weight quantization techniques [6]. We optimize the partial-sum
quantization on this model to reduce the ADC resolution for the CIM implementation, and then
the NN parameters of VGG-9 are retrained awaring partial-sum quantization. As we do not have
to train the parameters from scratch, the retraining process can save the training iterations, and
reducing training iteration is important for training with partial-sum quantization. For example,
training the VGG-9 model from scratch required 120 training epochs, but retraining with partialsum required only 25 training epochs.
Contrary to the previous section in which NN parameters were fixed and the partial-sum quantizer was optimized, the partial-sum quantizer is fixed, and the NN parameters are retrained in this
section. NN parameters can be categorized into synaptic weights used for feature extraction and
batch-normalization parameters used for balancing neuron values, and training them has two major effects on recovering the NN accuracy loss. First, fine-tuning the synaptic weights reconstructs
the NNs to extract proper information. The partial-sum quantization changes the NN computation results, so the features that are extracted from the NNs are also changed. However, if we
fine-tune the synaptic weights that are the filters for the feature extraction, then NNs can adapt
to the computational change and extract the desired information again. Second, retraining the
batch-normalization parameters can reduce the quantization error by fine-tuning the reconstruction level of quantization. The reconstruction level is the magnitude of quantization output (y-axis
of the Figure 3). The batch-normalization parameters define the scale-and-shift operation of MAC
results [7], so the quantization output is scaled with batch-norm parameters and the effective reconstruction level can be fine-tuned by retraining.
The retraining uses the back-propagation similarly to the conventional NN training [11]. The
learning rate of the fine-tuning is set to be smaller than the learning rate used for the baseline NN
training without partial-sum quantization. The other training conditions are the same as those for
training the baseline NNs. Simulation results show that the retraining recovers the NN accuracy
loss from the partial-sum quantization significantly (Figure 11). While the partial-sum quantization with data-driven optimization required five- to eight-level ADCs to preserve NN accuracy,
the partial-sum quantization with retraining requires three-level ADCs to achieve reliable NN
accuracy for CIFAR-10 dataset. Therefore, though it requires additional computational burden, retraining has noticeable advantage in reducing ADC resolution.

6 CONCLUSION
In this article, we proposed an aggressive partial-sum quantization methodology to reduce ADC
overhead in CIM-based NN accelerators. The proposed method was developed on the bit-scalable
CIM hardware to support generic multi-bit NN computations. We first introduced the zero-tread
quantizer to reduce the quantization error for near 0 values, and the partial-sum quantizer is optimized with a data-driven approach. The partial sum was optimized in networkwise manner or
layerwise manner, and the layerwise optimization showed better NN accuracy. Last, we retrained
the NNs with partial-sum quantization, which recovered substantial loss of the NN accuracy caused
by partial-sum quantizations, so that extreme quantization of partial sums could be achieved. Evaluation results show that NN accuracy on the CIFAR-10 dataset is preserved with three-level ADC,
and NN accuracy on the ImageNet dataset is preserved with nine-level ADC. As a result, CIM accelerators that are co-designed with proposed quantization algorithm achieve 50–80% reduction
in total area and 74–90% reduction in the energy consumption compared to previous designs.
