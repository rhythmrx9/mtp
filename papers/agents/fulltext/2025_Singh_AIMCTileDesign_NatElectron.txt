Analogue in-memory computing (AIMC) is an emerging computational
approach that executes operations directly within memory arrays,
reducing the need for data transfer between memory and processing units.
AIMC-based accelerators are, in particular, being explored for deep neural
network (DNN) inference, with the key element of such accelerators being
the AIMC tile, which can be implemented using various conventional volatile
charge-based and emerging non-volatile resistive memory (memristive)
technologies. Here we examine the design of non-volatile memristive
AIMC tiles for DNN accelerators. We explore the different components
of a memristive AIMC tile and the range of mapping techniques for
encoding signed multibit weights and inputs. We provide an analysis of the
efficiency and accuracy of output encoding schemes, including various
analogue-to-digital converter approaches. We also provide a comparative
analysis of the different memory technologies being explored and
projections for how technology scaling may impact key design components.

Deep neural networks (DNNs) have transformed the development
of artificial intelligence (AI). Unfortunately, such approaches have
substantial computational expenses, during both training and their
subsequent use for inference. Indeed, the need for such extensive
computational resources hindered progress in the field for nearly
three decades1. The introduction and gradual adaptation of specialized hardware such as graphics processing units has helped trigger
recent AI advances, while the computational demands of training and
inference with advanced AI models keep rising. For instance, training
Meta’s LLaMA, a massive language model with 65 billion parameters,
required 2,048 NVIDIA A100 graphics processing units over 5 months,
totalling approximately 2,638 MWh and emitting 1,015 tons of carbon2.
Deploying many instances of these same enormous models for inference, which represents over 90% of the AI hardware market, creates a
considerable challenge. Furthermore, some of these models must function within systems with limited power budgets3. Widespread adoption
of AI will thus remain elusive unless there is a marked improvement in
the overall power consumption of AI compute substrates.
We are currently in an era of domain-specific digital accelerators for deep learning (Fig. 1a). These accelerators achieve high efficiency and computational throughput and low latency by parallelizing

operations across a large array of processing elements (PEs), using
complex instructions, exploiting sparsity, adopting reduced precision and maximizing data reuse. However, even the best accelerators
struggle to achieve optimal energy efficiency, often not exceeding
100 fJ op−1 at high peak performance4,5. The primary bottleneck is
the incessant motion of synaptic weights between memory and processing units, particularly between off-chip dynamic random-access
memory (DRAM) and on-chip static random-access memory (SRAM)6.
This traffic limits energy efficiency to well below the efficiency of the
actual compute operations, especially in advanced complementary
metal–oxide–semiconductor (CMOS) nodes.
Several approaches are being explored to address the fundamental challenge of synaptic weight movement, including near-memory
computing7,8 (Fig. 1b). In near-memory computing, the idea is to equip
the memory units with adjacent PEs that can perform the matrix–vector multiply (MVM) operations central to DNNs, reducing the distance
over which weight elements must travel. A more exploratory but potentially higher-impact approach is in-memory computing (IMC), which
performs MVM operations directly within memory arrays, avoiding
weight transfers. IMC inference chips consist of multiple IMC tiles9
(Fig. 1c). The goal of an IMC tile is to perform MVM operations on

While AIMC synaptic weights can be realized using SRAM cells
and capacitive elements, such memory devices can only encode one
bit of weight data per memory element. Hence, some combination of
multiple bit-lines (BLs), multiple word-lines (WLs), digital addition
and/or time-multiplexing is required to implement the multiplication–
accumulation between multibit activations and multibit weights. As a
result, SRAM-based arrays are invariably not dense enough to hold all
the weights of most DNNs on a single die under reasonable chip area
constraints4. In this case, models with large weight capacities must be
mapped across multiple SRAM-based dies, chips, nodes and racks,
where high-speed interconnects become the primary challenge to
ensure efficient communication between these components14. Embedded DRAM (eDRAM) can improve the on-chip weight density compared
with SRAM but comes with additional challenges related to destructive
DRAM read, cell leakage during inference and the associated costs of
initializing and refreshing weight arrays15–17.
One way to address these issues is to transition to non-volatile
memory (NVM), such as resistive random-access memory (RRAM),
phase-change memory (PCM) or flash memory—each of which offers
higher memory density than does SRAM. Resistive memory devices
such as RRAM and PCM are also referred to as memristive devices. In
one implementation, by encoding multibit weights into a continuous range of analogue conductance, Ohm’s law can be used to compactly and rapidly perform the multiplication operation, followed by
accumulation using Kirchhoff’s current law18. This can support both
high-density and high-speed MVM operations. Moreover, NVM-based
AIMC can enable power gating of an accelerator chip without needing
to reload the weights.
However, these devices have limited write endurance, and programming them is costly in terms of energy and time, limiting their
suitability to applications that need only occasional programming
with repeated reads, such as AI inference. Consequently, NVM-based
AIMC accelerators are predominantly investigated within the context
of weight-stationary models that could meet the requirements of edge
devices19 and large language model inference20. Similar to the need to
maintain DNN model accuracy despite the use of reduced precision in
conventional accelerators, an important aspect of all IMC approaches

is the analysis of DNN model accuracy in the presence of analogue
noise, quantization effects and other imperfections present during
the MVM operations21.
In this Perspective, we examine the design of NVM AIMC tiles,
considering the various choices and combinations available within
the design space of the circuit components. We first examine the different components of a memristive AIMC tile and the range of mapping techniques for encoding signed multibit weights and inputs.
We then consider the challenges and opportunities associated with
designing these components. We also provide an analysis of the efficiency and accuracy of output encoding schemes, including various
analogue-to-digital converter (ADC) approaches. Finally, we present
a comparative analysis of the different memory technologies being
explored and projections for how technology scaling may impact tile
efficiency and weight capacity. (Previous review articles focusing on
particular aspects of this technology are also available22–28, including those with a primary focus on unit-cell configurations22–24 and
benchmarking methodology27,28).

AIMC tiles
An AIMC tile implements MVM operations where an input vector is
multiplied by a weight matrix stored in a synaptic unit-cell crossbar
array within the tile. For example, a column vector of inputs would
produce an entire row vector of MVM outputs. Inputs, weights and
outputs can all be signed, multibit or analogue quantities, and proper
encoding and design are needed to accommodate all cases. Although
some works on SRAM-based IMC have explored floating-point precision of inputs and weights, by handling the exponent and mantissa
representations separately using a combination of time, digital and
voltage (analogue) domain computing blocks29–31, this requires considerably higher memory capacity and computation energy than the
more conventional designs using integer precision31. Therefore, we
focus on integer precision to illustrate the different encoding schemes.

Weight encoding
The encoding schemes for mapping signed multibit weights with BW-bit
precision onto a memory array can be generally divided into two categories: (1) analogue differential storage and (2) digital bit-slicing storage32.
In both schemes, several elementary sub-unit cells are required to map
each signed weight, so collectively we can group these into a single
weight unit cell, where these cells are arranged in a crossbar array configuration (Fig. 3a). This crossbar configuration can potentially utilize
either selector-based or selectorless sub-unit-cell implementations.
Selectorless cells offer highly dense cross-point memory, but greatly

In the analogue differential storage approach, each weight unit
cell includes two sets of sub-unit cells: one for positive weights and a
second for negative weights. The conductance of the device(s) within
the sub-unit cell that corresponds to the polarity of the weight being
encoded is programmed to be proportional to the weight magnitude,
while the device(s) in the opposing set are programmed into the
minimum-possible-conductance state. To reduce programming variation, multiple devices can be used per sign, with their conductances
adding up. For zero weights, all devices are preferably programmed to
a minimum conductance. If multiple devices are available to encode
the weight magnitude, additional options for distributing the weight
magnitude across these devices become available as well, allowing
optimization for placement accuracy, long-term conductance stability
and other considerations34,35. In the digital bit-slicing weight-storage
approach, weights are typically mapped using a two’s complement
format36. Each sub-unit cell, assumed to be a single-level capacity (SLC)
device, represents one bit, with logic 0 and 1 programmed to low RESET

(GRESET) and high SET (GSET) conductance states, respectively. For an
8-bit signed weight, eight sub-unit cells are needed, each with varying
bit significance, which can be scaled using analogue or digital factors
in the peripheral circuitry.

Input encoding
Inputs can be encoded in the voltage or time domain. Voltage encoding
schemes offer fast sensing but are difficult to implement due to inefficient digital-to-analogue converters (DACs), device nonlinearity and
IR drop considerations37. We focus on the more prevalent time-based
encoding schemes in this section.
Encoding schemes for mapping multibit inputs with BX-bit precision can be grouped into three approaches on the basis of bit parallelism: (1) bit serial (BS), where each bit is represented as a separate
activation signal, (2) bit parallel (BP), where all bits are combined into
a single signal, and (3) bit hybrid (BH)13,38, where groups of bits are
represented by separate signals. The BS and BP approaches are shown
in Fig. 3a. In the BS approach, logic 1 or 0 is represented by high- or
low-voltage pulses. In the BP approach, a single pulse represents all
bits, with its duration proportional to the input value. The pulse length
is defined with respect to the smallest time-step TSTEP, corresponding
to the least significant bit (LSB), and increases by multiples of TSTEP for
higher bits, ranging from 0 to 2BX −1 × TSTEP. The BH approach breaks
the BX-bit input into two (or more) parts, leading to multiple durations,

Output encoding
The output encoding block carries out the analogue-to-digital
(A/D) conversion of the MVM output, incorporating both analogue
pre-conversion and digital post-conversion steps for voltage-to-current
conversion and to manage weighted partial MVM results. Optionally,
it may be followed by digital post-processing units for tasks such as
scaling and offset correction, applying activation functions and buffering data for the subsequent AIMC tile42,43. Recent AIMC-based DNN
(and numerous reduced-precision digital accelerator) implementations show that the output encoding does not need to retain the full
precision; mid-resolution A/D conversion is often sufficient to achieve
near-software accuracy for advanced AI applications21.
A/D conversion can use either voltage or current ADCs. Voltage
ADCs need integration capacitors for signal storage, enabling ADC sharing and easing pitch-matching13. Current ADCs allow immediate conversion without large capacitors but require dedicated pitch-matched
units per column43. As an example of handling weighted partial MVM
outputs, an AIMC tile using bit-sliced weights and BS/BH input encoding distributes bits across columns and input cycles. Bit significance
can be managed via (1) analogue pre-processing, where scaled column
currents or capacitors feed a mid-resolution ADC44,45, or (2) digital
post-processing, where low-resolution ADC outputs are combined
using shift-and-add logic13 (Fig. 3b). Input bit significance is handled
similarly; voltage sensing enables weighted accumulation across
phases, while current sensing requires immediate conversion and
risks amplifying quantization errors46.

Design challenges for AIMC tiles
Device and circuit non-idealities

The primary factors contributing to accuracy degradation in AIMC
tiles stem from the non-ideal behaviour of both memory devices and
peripheral circuitry involved in the analogue computation of MVM
operations. Imperfections in the input activation signals, as well as variability in the conductance or capacitive properties of memory devices,
directly affect the correctness of the analogue outputs generated by the
crossbar array. Additionally, inaccuracies introduced during the digitization of these analogue signals further compound the degradation
of the final MVM result. Non-idealities in charge-based memories such
as SRAMs have been extensively discussed in previous review articles,
with particular emphasis on various unit-cell configurations and their
accuracy and efficiency23. Recent studies employing capacitor-based
unit cells have demonstrated that such architectures exhibit strong
robustness against process and device-level variations23,47. On the other
hand, non-idealities in memristive devices include programming errors,
finite conductance ratios due to non-zero minimum conductance value
(GMIN), and temporal conductance drift48. Programming errors occur
when the written conductance deviates from the intended value, affecting readout accuracy and reducing the sensing margin. Techniques such
as using multiple devices with equal bit significance or binary true and
complementary data storage can mitigate these errors, but at the cost
of reduced weight density. Iterative program-and-verify methods are
often necessary to achieve target conductance levels35,49.
Limited conductance ratios reduce sensing margins. While
six-transistor (6T) SRAM offers a read current ratio near 1,000, those
of PCM and RRAM range from 10 to 100 (refs. 50,51), and that of
spin-transfer torque MRAM often falls below 2 (ref. 52). A non-zero
GMIN causes input-dependent offsets in MVM, requiring costly digital
correction that impacts speed, energy and area. Additionally, conductance drift in PCM and RRAM introduces errors. Global drift can be
Nature Electronics | Volume 8 | December 2025 | 1156–1169

corrected via column sampling53, but device-level variability remains
difficult to compensate32. The use of enhanced device structures, such
as projected PCM devices, can mitigate the temporal conductance drift,
but at the expense of conductance ratio54.
Circuit non-idealities include wire parasitic mismatches, CMOS
process variations and design variations. Wire parasitic mismatches
result from the finite resistance and capacitance of interconnect wires
in the memory array, affecting propagation delays and signal amplitude. Capacitance influences the transient behaviour of analogue
signals, while resistance impacts both the transient response and signal
magnitude due to IR drop, leading to inconsistent activation times of
select transistors and variable degradation of analogue signals across
different memory locations. This effect is input dependent, with the
impact of IR drop depending on the input vector conditions. Certain
circuit-level mitigation techniques, such as matching the wire parasitics
at the array level and reducing supply noise, can be utilized to boost
signal-to-noise ratio in the presence of input-dependent variations52,55.
CMOS process variations can also affect the analogue readout and
sensing circuits. While analogue components such as amplifiers and
reference blocks can be calibrated, additional variations from the
memory array may reduce sensing margins. Temperature and voltage
fluctuations can alter CMOS drive strengths and wire resistance, leading to design variations. Additionally, the ADC conversion process itself
is subject to error, and the digital shift-and-add process, as described
in the previous section, can amplify initially small errors.
Overall, these non-idealities contribute to diminished sensing
margins and introduce nonlinearity in the analogue MVM output13,56.
Hardware-aware training or fine-tuning of DNNs, which encapsulates
some of these effects in the forward pass, can be used to make models
resilient to deterministic (for example, IR drop) or random (for example, read noise) sources of noise and help preserve model accuracy21,57,58.

Energy, speed and area efficiency
Energy efficiency depends on operational voltage, tile size, data conversions and wire parasitics. Higher read voltage improves dynamic
range and sensing margins, while higher periphery voltage boosts
transistor headroom and noise margins. However, dynamic energy
rises quadratically in digital (CV2) and linearly in analogue circuits. ADCs
are major energy consumers. Larger tiles help amortize ADC costs but
increase IR drop and dynamic power, and may reduce efficiency when
mapping smaller DNN layers. D/A conversion consumes energy for data
pre-processing and input encoding, driven by power-hungry components such as large WL/SL drivers and high-frequency clocks. Higher
D/A resolution increases DAC energy use and prolongs static current
duration in elements such as crossbar arrays and operational transconductance amplifiers (OTAs) in ADCs. In advanced nodes, DAC latency
also prolongs leakage consumption in digital circuits such as oscillators
and counters. Additional energy is spent on long wire signalling, line
stabilization and capacitive output driving during MVM operations.
Latency is mainly determined by the input encoding scheme and
influenced by (1) the internal clock period, (2) worst-case input transition time and (3) OTA response time on the BL. Low bit parallelism or
multiphase MVM schemes require extra settling time per cycle. A/D
resolution also impacts latency (time-based ADCs see exponential
delays with higher resolution), but this is often masked by the input
encoding latency, as conversion occurs in parallel.
Finally, the AIMC tile area is determined by the memory array and
the input and output encoding blocks. Enlarging the memory array
within an AIMC tile improves area efficiency and reduces inter-tile
communication but introduces wire parasitic challenges. Finite RC
effects can cause IR drops, delay mismatches and signal integrity issues,
especially for analogue signals. Larger arrays also increase input settling times and may require buffering to preserve pulse width. Programming memristive devices typically requires high voltages (~2 V for
PCM, 3–4 V for RRAM) and 100–500-μA currents, necessitating thick

weight unit-cell pitch to avoid the latency increases associated with

Analogue-to-digital converters
The output encoding block can be divided into three stages:
pre-conversion, conversion and post-conversion (Fig. 4a). The
pre-conversion stage performs a direct voltage or current sensing
and optionally translates to another analogue domain (current to voltage) and combines weighted analogue quantities using an analogue
weight combiner. The conversion stage involves A/D conversion using
ADCs. The post-conversion stage performs final data formatting and
optionally shift-and-add operations using digital logic. In voltage
sensing, an analogue voltage VMVM proportional to the MVM result is
directly obtained from the array, and usually stored on a capacitor56.
A current sense approach holds the access line at a fixed read voltage
(typically using an OTA) while acquiring a current. This current can
then be directly digitized using a current-based ADC43, or integrated
into a capacitor and then subsequently digitized using a voltage-based
ADC41. Below, we describe different ADC architectures in these classes
and present their advantages and disadvantages.

Voltage-based ADCs
The flash ADC, successive-approximation register ADC (SAR ADC),
voltage-sense-amplifier-based ADC (VSA ADC) and voltage-to-time
converter ADC (VTC ADC) are all examples of voltage-based ADCs
(Fig. 4b). The flash ADC compares the analogue voltage with all possible reference levels in a single cycle using multiple comparators45,60,61.
For an n-bit ADC, 2n − 1 comparators are required, generating a binary
vector that is decoded by a thermometer decoder to produce the final
output. While the latency remains constant regardless of resolution, the
area and energy grow exponentially with the number of comparators,
flip-flops, interconnects, thermometer logic and size of the resistive
ladder generating reference levels. Additionally, post-processing is
needed to decode the thermometer code into the final ADC output.
Area and energy requirements imply that mid-range resolution (~8-bit)
flash ADCs are not a good fit for AIMC, but low-resolution flash ADCs
have been used with additional conversion steps for more bits25,62.
The SAR ADC performs successive comparisons of analogue values
using a binary search and an adaptive reference set based on previous
decisions41,63–67. As bit resolution increases, latency grows linearly with
the number of comparison cycles, but area and energy consumption
grow semi-exponentially due to the increasing size of the capacitive
DAC, along with a linear increase in comparator, calibration and SAR
logic size. Moderate resolution, low-to-medium energy consumption
and low latency make SAR a good fit for AIMC. Optimizations such as
bridge capacitors68 or shifted voltage references69 offer area saving
with increasing bit-widths. The VSA ADC converts multiphase analogue outputs of varying bit significance through successive voltage
comparisons with tunable resolution38,62,70,71. It processes inputs in
multiple cycles, generating partial analogue outputs, often combined
with a BS input encoding scheme where the number of cycles is equal
to BX, or a BH scheme, reducing the number of cycles. Bit significance
scaling can occur pre-conversion in the analogue domain (via weighted
current mirrors or capacitors) or post-conversion in the digital domain
(using shift-and-add logic). The number of conversions and bit resolution are tunable, thus balancing accuracy and efficiency. VTC ADCs
have an additional pre-conversion stage that converts the acquired

https://doi.org/10.1038/s41928-025-01537-5

voltage into a time signal, which can then be quantized using a clock, a
free-running oscillator or a look-up table. In some scenarios involving
simple activation functions such as ReLU, the generated time pulse
can be clipped and consumed downstream as the pulse-width input
for the next NN layer, avoiding the intermediate analogue-to-digital
and digital-to-time steps entirely33,72. Time-based ADCs suffer from
exponential time increases with bit resolution, but this can be alleviated
using fast counting mechanisms at moderate resolution of interest,
thanks to technology scaling.
An inherent advantage of all voltage-based architectures over
current-based ones is that storing the output as a voltage allows some
amount of ADC sharing across different access lines if needed, mitigating tight pitch-matching constraints. For instance, the reference
capacitor bank, comparator and SAR logic block of the SAR ADC can
be shared across multiple columns, which are converted sequentially.
Latency increases, but can be carefully managed through pipelining if individual A/D conversions can be considerably faster than the
overall MVM integration time. Sharing approaches for the flash ADC
are similarly straightforward. For the VSA ADC, this approach is possible, but depends on the exact scheme being used and the internals
of the architecture.

Current-based ADCs
Current-based ADCs directly convert an output current IMVM on the
access lines proportional to the MVM result into a digital value. The
integrate-and-fire circuit ADC (IFC ADC), current-controlled oscillator
ADC (CCO ADC) and current-sense-amplifier-based ADC (CSA ADC)
follow this approach (Fig. 4c).
The IFC ADC integrates current over a capacitor to generate a voltage, firing a pulse when this voltage crosses a threshold and resetting
the capacitor73–75. The number of pulses is proportional to the current
magnitude. Higher bit resolution is achieved by increasing pulse counts,
resulting in exponential growth in latency and energy consumption,
while the area remains nearly constant. While the IFC leaks continuously
and is subject to the threshold voltage variation of the discharge transistor, a variant known as the charge-injection cell uses a comparator
and feedback mechanism to discharge the capacitor only when a pulse
is fired76. The CCO ADC generates oscillations in an oscillator with a
frequency proportional to the input current43. The number of oscillations produced in a fixed time determines the digital output. The use
of a dedicated oscillator and the high frequency at which it operates
allow the CCO to achieve a higher digital resolution over similar timescales compared with IFC approaches, but it does require nonlinear
compensation at high input currents. The CCO design is also more
compact and energy efficient than the SAR or flash ADCs since it does
not require either capacitance banks or multiple comparators, with
most of the power consumed by the OTA and digital counter. As bit resolution increases, the area remains nearly constant (except for a larger
counter), but latency and energy grow exponentially due to the rise in
maximum oscillation counts. Like the VSA ADC, the CSA ADC converts
multiphase analogue outputs of varying bit significance through successive current comparisons with tunable resolution44,77,78. The column
current is either mirrored or directly fed into the CSA, while a reference
block provides tunable reference signals. The CSA size and number of
reference blocks grow exponentially with increasing resolution. Most
energy is consumed by the OTA and CSA, often exceeding that of the VSA
ADC13. Bit significance scaling can occur pre-conversion in the analogue
domain via weighted current mirrors (though this poses additional mismatch and calibration challenges)44 or post-conversion digitally (using
shift-and-add logic)13,37, balancing accuracy and efficiency.
Direct current-to-digital conversion precludes circuit sharing and
potentially multiplies positional input significance errors in BS and
BH schemes due to residual quantization error effects. In most cases,
handling the sign of the MVM requires separate components (except for
the case of the charge-injection cell that handles differential currents)

IMC tile efficiency and weight capacity
We begin by analysing how the memory array, input and output encoding blocks affect efficiency and accuracy. We then review recent trends
in efficiency, weight capacity and unit-cell size in IMC-based DNN accelerators, and conclude with projections for how technology scaling may
impact key design components.

Relative contribution of IMC tile sub-blocks
The computational efficiency and accuracy of IMC tiles are strongly
influenced by the encoding schemes for inputs and weights, which
are also tied to the output processing methods. To explore these
relationships, three different tile designs are analysed (Fig. 5a). AIMC
Tile1 uses an analogue-leaning approach where multibit inputs and
weights are represented as analogue quantities. AIMC Tile2 employs a
digital-leaning approach with multibit inputs and weights represented
as discrete quantities. Both AIMC tiles are based on memristive devices.
DIMC Tile is a fully digital SRAM-based IMC tile performing bitwise
multiplication of inputs and weights. The ADC design, topology and
architecture in AIMC tiles depend on the encoding schemes. Each tile
assumes signed 8-bit precision for inputs and weights, with a 256 × 256
weight matrix. The designs draw from recent literature26,43,44,81,82 and we
analyse component-wise contributions to the overall area and energy
(see Supplementary Note 1 for details).

area efficient than AIMC tiles due to low MVM latency and sub-threshold
operation. Recent work reports up to tenfold gains in efficiency, though
this comes with limited on-chip weight capacity (<100 KB), requiring
frequent weight reloading for larger models (>1 MB). The overhead
of weight transfers, despite excellent macrolevel FoMs, may become
a critical bottleneck and warrants thorough architectural evaluation.
There are also promising improvements for the on-chip weight
capacity, particularly for AIMC. Figure 6b shows recently proposed IMC
accelerators that can support more than one million on-chip weight bits
(none of the DIMC works could be included due to their lower weight
capacity) and Fig. 6c shows the weight density in terms of unit-cell
area per bit. On the basis of the same CMOS technology, a more than
tenfold improvement is observed between memristive units and SRAM.
This is because a single transistor-based memristive unit cell can store
one or even more bits, whereas a multitransistor SRAM cell can store
only one bit. In fact, a multilevel capacity (MLC)-based RRAM cell on
22-nm CMOS technology13 is nearly the same size (estimated from the
unit cell occupying 53F2 area) as a 6T SRAM cell based on 3-nm CMOS
technology90, implying nearly twice the weight density.
Projections based on reported IMC tile efficiency and memristive
device resistance suggest distinct trends for DIMC- and AIMC-based
accelerators with technology scaling (see Supplementary Note 4 for
more details). For DIMC, further improvements are anticipated due to
advancements in voltage scaling, CMOS logic and SRAM scaling beyond
3-nm technology (Fig. 6d,e). These improvements are estimated to provide
up to 1.4-fold gains in density and 1.2-fold gains in energy efficiency with
each CMOS technology node91,92. However, estimating the full potential
of DIMC-based accelerators is challenging due to limited data on fully
integrated systems, particularly regarding on-chip data communication.
Technology scaling in AIMC can improve device resistance and offer area
and power gains of up to 1.2-fold and 1.1-fold per CMOS node beyond
22 nm. AIMC also benefits from a nearly ten times smaller weight encoding
area than DIMC, thanks to memristive devices, an advantage expected to
grow with multibit encoding. However, scaling introduces challenges such
as increased wire RC, longer OTA response times, higher static energy, IR
drop and reduced noise margins, all of which can degrade MVM accuracy.
Finally, to support large DNN models, on-chip weight capacity
can be increased using three-dimensional (3D) memory-based tiles.
Recent IMC demonstrations have explored 3D NAND flash93,94 and
NOR flash95, which support both SLC and MLC and are compatible
with digital bit-slicing and analogue differential weight encoding in
AIMC tiles. NAND-flash-based 3D structures are particularly promising, with an expected annual areal density increase of 1.48-fold
due to further scaling and stacking94. While 3D memristive devices
are under active development96,97, 3D SRAM remains limited by
manufacturing challenges98.

Outlook
Recent silicon-verified demonstrations of AIMC tiles have exhibited
exponential advancements in precision, energy efficiency, area efficiency, on-chip weight capacity and weight density, driven by circuit
innovations and advancements in CMOS technology scaling. While
the wire parasitics increase substantially with technology scaling,
high-resistance devices are becoming more favourable for weight
encoding. The resulting lower column currents help mitigate accuracy degradation due to IR drops and concurrently reduce overall
energy consumption.
For future implementations, digital-intensive data conversion
topologies, particularly in ADC designs, are highly desirable when
implemented with advanced CMOS technology nodes and can further leverage the advantages of technology scaling. Notably, compact time-based ADCs such as ramp-based VTCs and controlled
oscillator-based designs offer inherent advantages over traditional
voltage or current comparator-based ADCs. This can be attributed to
Nature Electronics | Volume 8 | December 2025 | 1156–1169

https://doi.org/10.1038/s41928-025-01537-5

the exponential improvement in time resolution enabled by technology
scaling, while operating voltage and threshold levels remain relatively
stagnant, resulting in limited scaling benefits for reference and comparator circuits. Design techniques such as component sharing, replacing
resource-intensive digital on-chip calibration with compact analogue
calibration schemes99 and reducing the number of A/D conversions
through the use of analogue combiner circuits are becoming increasingly preferred to realize compact and energy-efficient ADC designs.
In the near term, AIMC based on switched-capacitor SRAM is likely
to remain a focus of research due to its resilience to device-level variations, robustness against IR drop-induced accuracy degradation, and
mature manufacturing infrastructure. Hybrid designs combining
SRAM and memristive devices may also offer a compelling trade-off
between robustness, density and energy efficiency, paving the way
for practical and scalable AIMC solutions31,100.
However, we have shown that, even with relatively mature CMOS
nodes, highly dense memristive and 3D flash memory technologies
offer an order-of-magnitude improvement in weight density and
on-chip storage capacity compared with SRAM implemented using
the most advanced CMOS technology. This, along with the support of
novel circuit- and algorithm-level signal-to-noise ratio enhancement
techniques, suggests that long-term future advancements in memristive and flash memories using advanced CMOS technology nodes could
enable the support of large models fully on-chip, paving the way for
more efficient DNN accelerators20.
