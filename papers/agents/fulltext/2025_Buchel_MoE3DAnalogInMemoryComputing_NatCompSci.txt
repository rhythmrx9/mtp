Large language models (LLMs), with their remarkable generative capacities,
have greatly impacted a range of fields, but they face scalability challenges
due to their large parameter counts, which result in high costs for training
and inference. The trend of increasing model sizes is exacerbating these
challenges, particularly in terms of memory footprint, latency and
energy consumption. Here we explore the deployment of ‘mixture of
experts’ (MoEs) networks—networks that use conditional computing to
keep computational demands low despite having many parameters—
on three-dimensional (3D) non-volatile memory (NVM)-based analog
in-memory computing (AIMC) hardware. When combined with the MoE
architecture, this hardware, utilizing stacked NVM devices arranged in
a crossbar array, offers a solution to the parameter-fetching bottleneck
typical in traditional models deployed on conventional von-Neumann-based
architectures. By simulating the deployment of MoEs on an abstract 3D
AIMC system, we demonstrate that, due to their conditional compute
mechanism, MoEs are inherently better suited to this hardware than
conventional, dense model architectures. Our findings suggest that MoEs, in
conjunction with emerging 3D NVM-based AIMC, can substantially reduce
the inference costs of state-of-the-art LLMs, making them more accessible
and energy-efficient.

Transformer-based large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated
state-of-the-art capabilities across a spectrum of tasks1–4, and their
remarkable generative capacity has led to a transformative impact
on the world. These advances are mostly a product of scaling laws5: a
smooth increase in the performance of LLMs is observed by increasing
the size of the model, assuming that the model is trained on sufficiently
large amounts of data6. However, this has brought about a reality where
state-of-the-art models easily surpass the billion-parameter threshold,
some of them even exceeding hundreds of billions7.

A crucial step towards enabling the widespread adoption of this
technology across various services and products involves reducing the
hardware cost and the inference cost in terms of time and energy. The
ongoing trend of ever-increasing model sizes, however, contradicts
that objective, because the inflated parameter count incurs an increase
in memory footprint, memory transactions and compute latency.
Moreover, the primary hardware elements used for serving such models, notably graphics processing units (GPUs), are power-intensive and
not designed for LLM inference. Given this, a key concern is reducing

A promising approach8,9 to alleviate the inference cost associated with LLMs involves embracing conditional computing, which is
a concept where the trajectory of an input through a neural network
is dynamically determined, and only a fraction of the parameters is
utilized to process the input. These disjoint sets of parameters are
referred to as ‘experts’, and models based on this concept are often
called a ‘mixture of experts’ (MoE). The first promising implementation building on this idea, the switch transformer, was proposed in
refs. 10,11. This architecture replaces every other feedforward network
(FFN) in the transformer architecture with E expert FFNs. During the
forward pass, a router network dynamically selects the top-k expert
for processing the incoming activations at each modified FFN layer.
The outputs from the selected experts are subsequently aggregated
with a weighted sum (Fig. 1a).
Although earlier works have indicated that MoEs can surpass conventional dense models in performance, with lower inference cost12,13
and with more resource-efficient training13, MoEs typically required
up to an order of magnitude more parameters just to match the performance of their dense, floating-point operations (FLOPs)-equivalent
counterparts14,15. For this and other reasons such as training instability, MoEs were often dismissed as an interesting approach to scaling
models, though impractical for deployment due to their size, which
introduced the need for model parallelism at the expert level, incurring
additional communication overhead11.
The assumption that MoEs require more parameters was refuted
with the publication of the Mixtral network1, a switch-like MoE that
demonstrates the best cost/performance ratio among contemporary
LLMs. It surpasses LLaMA 22 and GPT3.54 on numerous benchmarks,
exhibiting faster inference times—six times faster than LLaMA 2—and
fewer parameters (47 B compared to LLaMA’s 70 B). Additionally, a
study by Csordás, Irie and Schmidhuber16 revealed that with further
optimizations, MoEs are able to consistently outperform their dense
counterparts in parameter-equivalent scenarios while achieving speedups of up to 8×. To summarize, although MoEs were once deemed
impractical, recent advancements demonstrate that MoEs can not only
outperform dense models, but they also offer substantially cheaper
inference, with faster processing times and fewer parameters, making
them more efficient overall.
Although the conditional computing aspect of MoEs presents a
clear pathway to mitigate the computational scaling that accompanies the enlargement of models, it is essential to acknowledge that
deploying MoEs on conventional artificial intelligence (AI) hardware
encounters similar challenges as found with their dense counterparts.
The scaling of MoEs, whether by increasing the number of experts or
the embedding size, will inevitably lead to increased data movement
and thus to additional energy and time penalties. This issue stems from
the hardware adhering to the von-Neumann architecture. GPUs, for
instance, require continuous shuffling between the various levels of
memory hierarchy and the computational units (this is also referred to
as the von-Neumann bottleneck). This imposes a ceiling on the achievable computational efficiency and a substantial floor to the required
power budget17.
A promising alternative to the von-Neumann architecture that has
risen in popularity is in-memory computing using non-volatile memory
(NVM) devices18–21—commonly referred to as analog in-memory computing (AIMC). AIMC accelerators leverage device characteristics and
circuit laws to perform matrix–vector multiplication (MVM) operations
within the memory arrays storing the weights, thus eliminating the
necessity for weight shuffling, and requiring only the movement and
buffering of activations. Moreover, the operation is performed in the
analog domain, resulting in energy-efficient computation with 𝒪𝒪𝒪1)
time complexity. Recent publications of large-scale integrated AIMC

(ReRAM)24–26 and Flash27, show the viability of the technology. However,
for AIMC to be considered for LLM inference, such chips must be capable of storing the entirety of the model’s weights in the NVM devices.
Although dense integration of NVM devices has been demonstrated
in the literature28, current AIMC chips are orders of magnitude away
from the parameter count of LLMs. To that end, the advancement from
two-dimensional (2D) planar NVM to three dimensions offers a native
solution to the aforementioned problem. By scaling to the third dimension, the demonstrated density of such memories29,30 provides a viable
path towards storing the parameters of an LLM model. The potential
of this technology has attracted the attention of researchers, leading
to proposals for 3D AIMC architectures for deep-learning inference
and vector similarity search31–35.
In the following, we combine the conditional computing mechanism of MoEs with the characteristics of 3D AIMC. We demonstrate
how the concept of conditional computing of MoEs is an ideal fit for
3D AIMC and how MoEs scale favorably compared to dense networks.
We simulate the execution of dense and MoE-based LLMs in an abstract
3D AIMC system and compare their scaling behavior. It should be
noted that providing an exact accelerator architecture is beyond the
scope of this work. Instead, we investigate the impact of the inherent
limitations of 3D AIMC hardware on different network architectures,
gaining insights into the design principles of such an accelerator.
Furthermore, we study the impact of noise introduced by the analog
hardware on a language modeling task and demonstrate floating-point
arithmetic-equivalent accuracy (iso-performance) at noise levels comparable to those observed in previously published AIMC hardware.

Results

The simulation framework
There are several reasons motivating the implementation of MoEs on
3D AIMC hardware. In Fig. 1b we present the number of non-stationary
(for example, activations) and stationary (for example, weights) parameters of several MoEs for different sequence lengths. The memory
associated with storing weights outweighs the memory required for
buffering activations by up to three orders of magnitude. Moreover, the
demonstrated densities of 3D NVM have reached more than 10 Gb mm−2
(Fig. 1c). If 3D AIMC hardware would approach the 3D NVM densities,
all the parameters of a 100-billion-sized LLM model could be stored
in a single AIMC chip.
To perform an MVM y = xWT, one programs the elements of the
weight matrix, W, onto the devices that correspond to a 2D slice of the
memory stack, herein referred to as a tier. During inference, the target
tier is selected and the input vector x is fed into the memory array. The
output vector read back from the tile will be the result of the computation y. The computation is typically performed in the analog domain
by exploiting the physical attributes of the memory devices and their
array-level organization (Fig. 1d). When deploying MoEs, the weight
matrices in each expert are chunked and mapped to tiers in separate
tiles. This way, the weights of the FFN in each expert are mapped to the
same set of tiles, and the experts are stacked vertically (Fig. 1e). Note
that, although the use of 3D memory alleviates capacity constraints, it
does not necessarily increase the computational density of the accelerator. This is because performing MVM in parallel across multiple tiers
on one tile is impractical. In some cases, this limitation—here referred
to as the one-tier-at-a-time (OTT) constraint—is introduced by the
physical arrangement of the memory devices31, whereas in other cases
it implicitly arises as the amount of scratchpad memory and peripheral
circuits required for supporting parallel MVM and would prohibitively
increase the areal footprint. Therefore, these tiles need to be operated
in a mutually exclusive way, which can have a detrimental impact on
inference performance if not carefully considered.

We focus on batched autoregressive generation of tokens given
an input sequence. We focus on this use-case because it is currently the
most popular way to interact with an LLM. However, other use-cases
exist, such as prompt or multimodal processing37. These set-ups typically utilize slightly different architectures38–40, which often include an
encoder and a decoder. Although we focus on decoder-only architectures in this Article, we do not expect our main results to change for
encoder-only or encoder–decoder architectures, as the underlying
layer structure is—with the exception of the attention mechanism—
the same.
Autoregressive generation of tokens can be greatly accelerated
by using KV-Caching41. This is done by only requiring the last token
to be fed into the decoder, rather than the entire sequence each time.
This introduces a compute–memory trade-off, the impact of which
is substantial in performance and memory requirements, especially
considering that the size of the KV-Cache scales linearly with batch
size, sequence length, model dimension and number of layers41. Using
KV-Caching necessitates the addition of a non-negligible amount of
dynamic random access memory (DRAM). Because of the high capacity
and weight stationarity of 3D AIMC, DRAM technologies, which have
less capacity, are cheaper and are more efficient, can be considered.
When DRAM is added to a 3D AIMC system, various trade-offs between
re-computation and caching, and the subsequent effect on latency,
throughput, external memory requirements and efficiency can be
studied. It is worth noting that in its basic application, KV-Caching
can be employed in AIMC systems in the same manner as in digital
accelerators. Although the main results presented in this Article have
been obtained without KV-Caching, we have quantified the effect
of KV-Caching on throughput and energy efficiency in separate
experiments.
When batched inference is considered, we construct the execution
graph for a single sequence and replicate it for the required number of
sequences in the batch (Fig. 2c). Furthermore, the simulator natively
supports inter-sequence pipelining, meaning that operations associated with one sequence can be interleaved with operations of another.
We observe that although a batch size greater than 1 generally raises
the peak scratchpad memory requirements, inter-sequence pipelining
notably reduces the total inference time (Supplementary Note 1 provides a more thorough study). Finally, after the execution graph is generated, the simulator schedules the individual operations, providing
the total execution time required for a workload, along with the energy
consumption and peak scratchpad memory requirements (Methods
section ‘Simulation of LLM execution on the 3D AIMC accelerator’).

Comparing the scaling behavior of dense and MoE-based
models
In the experiment depicted in Fig. 3a, we study the scaling behavior of
dense and MoE-based models deployed on the 3D AIMC accelerator by
varying the model dimension dmodel and the number of experts. For each
dmodel, the number of tiles is chosen as the required number of tiles for
hosting the MoE-based model with the most number of experts and
the respective dmodel. The alternative to this choice is that the number
of tiles is chosen as the minimum number of required tiles per model,
which makes the results harder to interpret, as the effective number
of OTT conflicts depends on the number of tiles available for mapping
the model (Supplementary Note 2 provides a more detailed investigation). By increasing the model dimension dmodel, the inference time
Nature Computational Science | Volume 5 | January 2025 | 13–26

https://doi.org/10.1038/s43588-024-00753-x

increases substantially, as a larger dmodel directly increases the number
of FLOPs and contributes to more OTT conflicts in the pipeline. This is
because the layers increasingly conflict with other layers mapped to
the same tiles. The inference time increases sharply when moving from
dmodel = 512 to 768. This jump in inference time can be attributed to a
sudden increase in the total number of OTT conflicts. We generally
observe that our inference time is dominated by the overall sum of OTT
conflicts across all tiles. Although the number of OTT conflicts per tile
even decreases when moving from dmodel = 512 to 768, the total number
of tiles necessary to fit the largest MoE model increases drastically,
as one 768 × 768 layer now occupies four tiers, compared to one tier
for the 512 × 512-layer size. From this, we also conclude that the MVM
operations performed in the analog tiles contribute a non-negligible
fraction of the overall latency. Supplementary Note 3 further investigates how much each operation contributes to the overall latency and
energy, and how these proportions change on varying parameters such
as sequence length and model size.
Unfortunately, scaling dense models to the billion-parameter
regime inevitably means increasing dmodel to exorbitantly high values7.
MoEs, on the other hand, can be scaled by increasing the number of
experts, keeping the FLOPs constant. Figure 3a shows that we can easily
scale to 51 billion parameters at the same inference time as the dense
model with the same model dimension. By scaling the model using
more experts, we make ideal use of the high capacity of 3D NVM, while
avoiding additional OTT conflicts.
On the other hand, von-Neumann architectures, such as GPUs,
do not exhibit the constant scaling of 3D AIMC. With an increase in
model dimension dmodel and the number of experts, the volume of
data associated with the weights of the experts that need to be fetched
from memory—often DRAM—increases. Figure 3b shows the amount
of expert weight data that needs to be fetched to generate eight new
tokens from a context of length 64 as a function of the number of
experts for four distinct model dimensions dmodel, assuming the use of
5-bit weights. Methods section ‘Calculation of weight movement for
MoEs’ provides more details about how the amount of moved memory
is computed.
In 3D AIMC there is no need to fetch weights from memory, so the
overall peak memory consumption barely exceeds 1 MB (Fig. 3c). The
peak memory originates from the staged data at the MHA layer, which
is roughly four times the sequence length times the model dimension
(Q, K, V and 1 for the residual connection). Here we consider a batch
size of 4, and hence the aforementioned activations must be staged at
most four times in parallel. For a model dimension of 1,024 and a maximum sequence length of 72, this results in ~1.18 MB. We assume int8
activations and do not use KV-Caching. Although the inference time
comparison was done for top-1 routing, meaning that only one expert
is picked for each token, recent studies13,16 have shown that increasing
the number of utilized experts k improves model accuracy. This comes
at the cost of an increased number of FLOPs, which is directly reflected
in the inference time, as Fig. 3d illustrates. Noticeably, as the FLOPs in
the expert FFN start to dominate the total number of FLOPs, the factor by which the inference time increases approaches k. From this, we
conclude that it is generally better to reduce the model dimension of
the experts while choosing a higher k, which has been found to increase
model accuracy16.

Discussion
The primary focus of this work is to introduce readers to the combination of 3D AIMC and the concept of conditional computing. With the
findings of this work, we aim to stimulate architectural exploration
towards the development of 3D AIMC accelerators capable of competing with conventional AI accelerators. We have identified several key
areas for this exploration. First, it is crucial to identify a viable communication fabric that can efficiently move activation data without
limiting the throughput of the compute elements. Various approaches
have been proposed for 2D AIMC systems, and it is imperative for the
community to assess their viability and scalability in the context of
3D AIMC. Next, an essential aspect of any generative transformer
accelerator is the organization of its memory hierarchy. Although 3D
AIMC has a clear advantage over von-Neumann systems due to weight
stationarity, there is a study to be conducted on how to localize the
scratchpad memory within the accelerator and whether or how to
utilize high-bandwidth external memory for scaling to large sequences
and for memory-compute trade-offs like KV-Caching. Furthermore, the
precision, amount and complexity of the digital auxiliary compute is
crucial to ensure minimal impact on the bandwidth and efficiency provided by the tiles. Finally, in this work we assume that embeddings are
stored and retrieved from the tiles through one-hot MVMs. It remains
to be determined whether the tiles can support such sparse reads or if
embeddings should be fetched from an external memory.

Methods

The 3D AIMC accelerator
The accelerator consists of a parameterizable number of tiles, each
composed of a configurable number of tiers. Two types of digital
compute are included, with the first type handling lightweight digital
computation, such as accumulations, activation and normalization
functions, and the second type handling heavier digital computations
associated with MHA (Extended Data Table 3 presents the operations
we consider and where they are executed). Data transmission between
tiles and digital compute units is facilitated by an interconnect with a
constant time and energy cost per bit. These simulations do not consider interconnect contention, as it has a strong dependence on the
selection of the interconnect technology and the physical layout of
the accelerator. Data exchange between digital units occurs through
memory, meaning that the transferred data have to be written into a
buffer before another digital unit then reads and processes said data.
All digital units are considered to have access to a scratchpad memory,
from which they read inputs and write outputs. The read/write latency
and energy associated with these static random access memory (SRAM)
interactions are included in each digital operation. We refrain from
localizing SRAM to specific units and assigning a capacity for each
block, as more detailed studies are required to determine the optimal

Simulation of LLM execution on the 3D AIMC accelerator
To assess the performance and energy efficiency of LLM inference in the
3D AIMC accelerator, we developed a high-level, architecture-agnostic
inference simulator tailored to the pipelined execution flow of AIMC
accelerators. The simulator has been functionally verified (that is, it
produces the same logits as pretrained networks) and validated against
a previously published low-level simulator of a 2D heterogeneous
AIMC architecture (details are provided in Supplementary Note 6).
Implemented in Python, the simulator leverages existing libraries
such as PyTorch46 and torch.fx47, which ensures high flexibility and
ease of extension. Under the hood, it operates on the simple torch.fx
intermediate representation (IR) in lieu of more complex and less scalable IRs, such as torch.jit.script. The process of simulating a network
in our simulator involves four key steps. First, we define the network
architecture. Second, we map the layers of the network to 3D AIMC tiles.
We then trace the network to generate the operation graph in the torch.
fx IR. Finally, we simulate its execution by scheduling the operations of
the graph. The outputs of the simulator include the inference time for a
given number of sequences and sequence length, the energy expended
in performing all operations, and the peak memory required to buffer
activations during inference.
To define the networks, we developed modified versions of
PyTorch’s elementary transformer blocks, namely the encoder and
the decoder. Unfortunately, using the standard blocks and model
definitions was deemed suboptimal due to the way torch.fx traces such
models. When tracing conventional definitions, each torch module
appears once at a per-tensor basis, meaning once, independent of
the selected number of sequences and sequence length. Although
this poses no problem when simulating conventional accelerators,
the weight stationarity of AIMC necessitates a dataflow where each
token (vector) is independent and passes through each operation at a
different time. For instance, when tracing an encoder block, the conventional model’s graph would force all tokens to pass through a first
linear layer before proceeding to the next. In AIMC systems, however,
21

Article
the execution flow would entail the first token passing through the
first linear layer and then beginning on the second linear layer as the
second token undergoes processing by the first linear layer and so on.
The pipelined nature of the AIMC dataflow required us to modify the
definition of the transformer modules to ensure that tracing yields a
token-wise operation graph. Once these modules were updated, the
definition of large models followed the standard approach of defining a PyTorch model with custom modules. To ensure correctness, we
functionally verified the new modules against standard implementations of encoder–decoder and decoder-only transformers48. Once a
model is defined, the next step involves mapping the linear layers of
the network onto the tiles of the accelerator based on a greedy in-order
approach: for mapping a single weight matrix, we chunk the matrix
into tile-shaped pieces (here 512 × 512) and greedily pick the set of
tiles on the accelerator that have the lowest tile utilization. In this case,
the utilization of a tile is defined as the sum of the utilization of each
chunk belonging to some layer that has already been mapped to one
of the tiers of the tile. It should be noted that the tile utilization does
not simply correspond to the number of used tiers per tile, because
the utilization of chunks belonging to expert layers is 1/E, where E is
the number of experts (assuming top-1 routing and uniform routing
probabilities), while the utilization of non-expert layers is 1. Interestingly, this mapping strategy naturally leads to scenarios where expert
layers are mapped on top of each other, as the summed utilization of
all the experts equals the utilization of just one dense layer. We find
that the utilization-based approach generally minimizes the number of conflicts arising from the OTT constraint (referred to as OTT
conflicts). The network is traversed in the order of execution, and the
layers are mapped in the order in which they are called. As we show in
Supplementary Note 2, this mapping strategy leads to an almost ideal
distribution of the computational workload across all tiles. It should
be noted that—in favor of keeping our mapping and scheduling algorithms simple—tiles can only be occupied by one layer or parts of one
layer. We note that co-occupation of tiles with different layers makes
sense when the model dimension is smaller than the tile size, or when
the model dimension is not dividable by the tile size.
For the embedding, we assume a vocabulary size of 50,000, where
the embedding of one token is retrieved by performing an MVM with a
one-hot encoded vector. Note, however, that through simple modulo
arithmetic, one can identify the tiers required for retrieving the correct
embeddings. This avoids performing an MVM with a 50,000-element
vector. More details are provided in Supplementary Note 7. The third
step involves tracing the model to acquire a token-wise operation graph
in the torch.fx IR. The upper part of Fig. 2b illustrates this step. Initially,
we utilize torch.fx to generate the initial operation graph. The resulting
graph then undergoes processing and formatting to make it suitable
for scheduling. This process entails removing trivial nodes that are
not explicitly simulated (for instance, concat and view operations),
while also incorporating nodes specific to our simulation (for example, communication nodes). Functional dependencies are preserved
throughout this process, by ensuring causality is respected when connecting floating edges following the removal of a node. Finally, each
node representing a linear layer that needs to be chunked and mapped
across multiple tiers is replaced by a subgraph containing the tier-level
mapped MVM operations and the requisite operations for combining
the partial results. Finally, the operations in the processed graph are
scheduled in a pipelined execution flow. To that end, we adopt a conventional instruction scheduling approach49,50, during which we iteratively
identify the source nodes in the operation graph, assess which nodes
can be executed, based on the resource constraints of the accelerator,
and designate them as processing. A global clock is then advanced by
the time step required for any operation to be completed. Nodes that
finish executing within this time step are removed from the graph, and
the remaining time of all processing nodes is adjusted to account for
the elapsed time. This process continues until all nodes are scheduled,

https://doi.org/10.1038/s43588-024-00753-x

signifying the end of the inference. During this iterative process, we
additionally calculate the energy required for each operation and
determine the number of vectors that need to be stored in activation
memory at each time step. An illustrative example of this process is
depicted in the lower part of Fig. 2b.
The scheduling algorithm is configurable in two levels: (1) in terms of
what it considers as resource constraints and (2) in the manner it selects
an operation if multiple operations request the same resource simultaneously. Regarding the former, we take into account the OTT constraint,
which occurs when multiple tokens request tiers belonging to the same
tile, as well as constraints on the number of available DPUs and MHA
units in the accelerator. It is worth noting that we elected to not enforce
resource constraints on data transmission, as doing so would tether
the simulations to a specific underlying communication channel and
accelerator layout. The same principle applies to the activation buffer
memory, where we choose to record the amount needed without applying a constraint on its placement or size. Regarding operation selection,
we employ a heuristic that prioritizes operations from earlier sequences
(lower sequence ID) and earlier tokens within each sequence (lower token
ID). Through empirical observation, we find that this heuristic provides
a solid baseline for achieving fast and resource-efficient inference.
In an initial version of the simulator, we traced over the autoregressive for-loop of generative transformers, which scaled poorly. We
therefore resorted to another approach where we, in parallel, traced
and pruned the graph for the generation of one token, given a context
length of X, X + 1 and so on. Finally, we simply connected the graphs’
endpoints to preserve the functional dependence of the autoregressive
loop. Similarly, we replicated the graph horizontally to account for the

partition of the dataset. The base model used for training is an adapted
version of the LLaMA model with rotary position embeddings that
supports a variable amount of MoE layers. The MoE layer is programmed in triton53 and adapted from ref. 16. The model supports all
the base functionality from Hugging Face and is openly available (see
Code availability section). The hyperparameters used for training the
models are provided in Extended Data Table 6. For hardware-aware
finetuning, we leveraged AIHWKIT54, an open-source framework used
for hardware-aware training of neural networks. Because AIHWKIT
only supports standard PyTorch modules such as the convolutional or
linear layer, we implement a custom analog MoE layer written in triton
that supports most of the base hardware-aware training functionalities,
including weight noise injection, output noise injection, input range
learning and arbitrary input quantization. The correct forward and
backward passes of the analog MoE layer are tested against a naïve,
linear layer-based implementation of the MoE layer, which can be easily
handled by AIHWKIT. The hardware-aware triton-based MoE layer is
also openly available (Code availability section) and is compatible with
AIHWKIT54 and AIHWKIT-Lightning55. During training, we clip the
weights to 2.0 standard deviations after every batch update and inject
Gaussian noise ζ ∼ ηtrain max 𝒪|W|) 𝒩𝒩𝒩0, 1), where ηtrain = 0.067, into every
weight matrix W in the forward pass. Although previous works56 have
demonstrated that sampling the Gaussian noise per sample in the batch
improves downstream robustness, we sample the noise once per batch
because it is much faster. We also use input range learning, where we
initialize the input ranges using a moving average over the maximum
of the inputs to every layer for the first 1,000 batches. We additionally
quantize the inputs to 8 bits and use full precision in the backward pass.
Embeddings and digital operations such as applying activation functions or performing MHA are computed in FP-32.
