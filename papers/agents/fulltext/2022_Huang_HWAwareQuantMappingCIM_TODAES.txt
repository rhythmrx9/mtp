Hardware-aware Quantization/Mapping Strategies for
Compute-in-Memory Accelerators
SHANSHI HUANG, HONGWU JIANG, and SHIMENG YU, Georgia Institute of Technology
The emerging non-volatile memory (eNVM) based mixed-signal Compute-in-Memory (CIM) accelerators are of great interest in today’s AI accelerators design due to their high energy efficiency. Various CIM
architectures and circuit-level designs have been proposed, showing superior hardware performance for deep
neural network (DNN) acceleration. However, hardware-aware quantization strategies for CIM-based accelerators are not systematically explored. Since there are a variety of design options for neural network
mapping on CIM systems while improper strategies may narrow the circuit-level design space and further
limit the hardware performance, it is important to make a comprehensive early-stage design space exploration
and find appropriate quantization/mapping strategies to achieve better hardware performance. In this paper,
we provide a joint algorithm-hardware analysis and compare the system-level hardware performance for various design options, including quantization algorithms, data representation methods and analog-to-digital
converter (ADC) configurations. This work aims to propose guidelines for choosing more hardware-friendly
design options for chip architects. According to our evaluation results for CIFAR-10/100 and ImageNet classification, the properly chosen quantization approach and optimal mapping strategy (dynamic fixed-point quantization + 2’s complement representation/shifted unsigned INT representation + optimized precision ADC)
could achieve ∼2× energy efficiency and 1.2∼1.6× throughput with 5%∼25% reduced area overhead, compared
to naïve strategy (fixed-point quantization + differential pair number representation + full precision ADC).

2 PRELIMINARY
2.1 CIM Basics
DNN processing is filled with massive convolutions, which can be mapped to 2D VMMs between
the input vector and the flattened weight matrix. After mapping 2D weight matrices into memories properly, the CIM crossbar array structure can naturally support VMMs with perpendicular
input rows and output columns. Various types of memory devices have been investigated for CIM
designs. Among them, eNVMs are preferred for their non-volatile characteristic, multi-level capability, and dense cell structure. CIM designs generally leverage adjustments in peripheral circuits
of the memory array to support in-memory computing. As shown in Figure 1, the input vector is
loaded to the rows in parallel and multiplied by weights conductance to generate products in the
form of current. By applying Kirchhoff’s law, the summed current along columns represents the final MAC outputs. As this MAC operation is done in the analog domain, array-level interfaces such
as Analog-to-Digital Converters (ADCs) and Digital-to-Analog Converters (DACs) are required between the memory array and other peripheries, offering inter-array or upper-level digital
processing. This mixed-signal computing scheme offers the scalability towards hierarchical system
designs via interconnect buses or network-on-chip (NOC) while introducing additional power
dissipation and area overhead for the necessary data conversion.
For further reference in this paper, The term ‘digit’ will refer to inputs and weights used in the
single MAC operation (as shown in Figure 1(a)) to differentiate them from the binary bits. In other
words, one digit could represent a k-bit data. This digit-wise MAC itself could support multi-bit
operation with high precision input digits encoded as analog signals by DACs and multi-bit weight
digits stored in multi-level cells (MLC). Multi-digit precision MACs could be further supported
in CIM by encoding the high-precision inputs as pulses in sequence and splitting weights into
multiple cells. Then the digitized MAC outputs by ADC will go through the shift-and-add process
to obtain the final outputs. Since DNN models necessitate quantization before being mapped to
CIM hardware, the quantized data precision will also affect ADC (& DAC) resolution accompanied
with different hardware overheads. Besides, different data representation methods would cause
different data distribution, leading to different memory capacity and ADC requirements.
In this work, as shown in Figure 1(b), we map the weights at different spatial locations of each
kernel into different sub-matrices. For example, all the weights at the left-top channel of each
kernel are restructured as one group (i.e., one sub-matrix). Hence, K × K sub-matrices are needed
for mapping all the kernels. Considering the hardware constraints of memory array, a sub-matrix’s
size may still be too large. In this case, each sub-matrix can be further partitioned into a group of
sub-arrays, and the input data can be assigned to corresponding sub-arrays. During the parallel
computation, the segments of partial sums are first collected from sub-arrays, and then the partial
sums from sub-matrices need to be accumulated to obtain the final output.

larger to improve software performance. This trend makes them unfriendly for edge devices, which
have limited area and power budgets. The research efforts to solve this problem could be classified
into two types [7]. The first one is to design compact network structures such as MobileNet [8]
and ShuffleNet [9]. The second one is to quantize the large networks to reduce the computation
and memory cost. The compact networks are not suitable for CIM-based inference engines for two

Since X has lower precision than Y , it generally only represents a sub-range of Y . From the software’s point of view to quantization, choosing this range is very important for good performance.
It is usually harmless to directly quantize the weights into the [−1,1] region. However, the inputs
need to be taken care of during the quantization since they could vary substantially according
to the network structure. Some methods use a fixed range across all the layers, which could be
viewed as a fixed-point quantization. For example, WAGE [12] directly clips the input range to
[−1,1] with fixed constants for normalization between layers. This fixed range is achievable by
controlling the statistics of the weights from initialization and learning rate (LR) to avoid exploding and vanishing. WAGE is hardware friendly since it only considers fixed range clips while
no normalization is needed, which is usually in higher precision and costly. However, the scalability of WAGE is proved poor as the network goes deeper or the network structure becomes more
complicated since it is hard to maintain the network’s statistics only through initialization and LR.
The upgraded WAGE [15] introduces batch-normalization (BN) (i.e., WAGEBN) into networks
with a fixed range input quantization. BN could help stabilize the layer output statistics and make
applying the fixed range quantization safer. Still, the BN output range is not necessarily limited,
and thus may suffer from the overflow loss. Some other works utilize nonlinear functions like Tanh
or Sigmoid to reform the output for fixed range quantization, requiring high-precision operation,
and thus introducing larger hardware overhead. Another method is to find the quantization range
from the input statistics dynamically. For example, [10] proposes a dynamic fixed-point (DF)
number scheme to quantize the network for each layer, which could be viewed as dynamically
finding the input (Y ) range and mapping it to a fixed-point number set X by using layer-wise C.
This work will compare the hardware performance of these two quantization methods, namely
WAGE and DF, as an illustration. It will be seen that even though the same precision is adopted,
the quantization method will affect the data distribution and further change the hardware performance. Figure 2 shows the accuracy results of the quantization method on the VGG-8 network
for CIFAR-10 classification, and the ResNet-18 network for CIFAR-100/ImageNet (subset) classification. WAGE performs well for VGG-8 but introduces a big accuracy drop when it moves to
ResNet-18, thus BN is necessary for deep networks. Both methods could achieve the floating-point

quantizing the weight to 2-bit is almost negligible, which is desired to hardware design. While
these two algorithms are not state-of-the-art (SOTA) quantization algorithms that could achieve

3 NEURAL NETWORK MAPPING
As aforementioned, most of the CIM architectures for DNN adopt a mixed-signal scheme that
first conducts MAC operations in the analog domain. Then, the analog results are converted back
to the digital domain for further processing, such as BN, ReLU, and pooling function. Besides,
considering the limited size of the CIM array, it is usually hard to fit the whole weight matrix of
one DNN layer into a single array. The weight matrixes are generally cut into several subarrays
to generate partial sums. The partial sums are converted back to the digital domain for further
accumulation. Thus, there are three key problems to solve when mapping the CNN to the CIM
system: (1) How to map the MAC operations to the analog domain; (2) How to convert the
analog MAC results back to the digital domain; and (3) How to perform the following digital
processing. The design space of these three steps is large and a number of techniques have been

3.1

MAC Mapping in CIM

3.1.1 Number System in CIM. When mapping MACs to the CIM array, we need to make three
decisions: the number system, the number representation, and the analog mapping method. Unlike digital computer systems, which are typically binary number systems, the CIM architecture
employs analog calculation, thus could support high-precision digits in the MAC operation. As
a result, different digit precision of input/weight may lead to different hardware requirements or
overhead. For instance, increasing the input digit precision could reduce the compute cycles while
expensive input-encoding circuits such as DACs need to be employed, causing a large area overhead. Generally, reducing the hardware cost is more critical than speeding up the computation for
the resource-constrained edge devices. Thus, binary input digits are widely used in CIM designs.
In contrast, increasing the weight digit precision could reduce the needed memory capacity by
storing multi-bit value into one cell (e.g., 2-bit or 4-bit per cell) with negligible overhead as long as
memory characteristics support multi-level states. Besides, as the digit precision of inputs or/and
weights increases, the full precision of the analog MAC outputs will also increase, leading to higher
required ADC resolution and overhead. Theoretically, increasing the digit precision for weights
(cells) or inputs has the same impact on deciding the full precision of MAC outputs. The difference
is mainly the hardware cost. Considering all the above, the number system we employed for CIM
architecture discussed in this work will be binary inputs with flexible precision weights.
3.1.2 Number Representation in CIM. As the quantization ranges are symmetric around zero
for these two demonstrated quantization methods (i.e., WAGE and DF), choosing N-bit integer
(INT) as the X for the quantized fixed-point number is natural to keep consistency across different quantization methods. The quantized inputs and weights of the convolution/fully-connected

3.2 Analog-to-Digital Conversion
In most of the CIM designs, the conversion from the analog to the digital domain is the bottleneck
of hardware performance. In general, high parallelism is preferred to improve the efficiency of the
analog MAC, which will increase the full precision of the partial sum. For lossless conversion, a
high-resolution ADC is essential. However, the high-resolution ADC is usually area-consuming,
introducing difficulties to match the column pitch of the array. In this case, several columns need to
share one ADC through a multiplexer, which reduces the column-wise parallelism. In addition, the
limited dynamic range also causes challenges in high-precision ADC design. Besides, as illustrated
in previous work [30], the ADC contributes a large portion of total energy consumption and latency in CIM design, becoming more severe with increasing ADC precision. There are mainly two

strategies used in analog output digitization. The first is to limit the number of rows open in parallel [31], which only needs low-precision ADC for no quantization loss. This method will relieve
the dynamic range problem and fit more ADCs to the column pitch. However, this will not improve
the throughput since it increases the column-wise parallelism by sacrificing row-wise parallelism.
Another drawback is that energy efficiency will be limited as more cycles of low precision quantization are conducted. The advantage of this method is that the full precision conversion could
guarantee the software’s performance. The second method is to introduce ADC quantization loss
during the conversion. Designing the ADC according to the partial sum distribution is important to
minimize the performance loss with the highest precision loss. For example, Reference [6] shows
that instead of cutting LSB for the linear quantization in ADC as the general quantization method,
it will be more efficient to cut MSBs of the partial sums as they are around “0”s in most cases. Thus,
partial sums’ MSBs and/or LSBs are cut according to their distribution for the linear ADC quantization. To further fit the ADC quantization to the distribution. Reference [32] uses the Lloyd-Max
algorithm to find nonlinear references for quantization. Reference [29] uses the Cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the partial sum to decide the nonlinear references corresponding to
linear quantized cumulative distribution. No matter how these nonlinear references are decided,
the ADC levels must be mapped to these references through a look-up table (LUT) for further
processing. Also, compared to the linear quantization, the nonlinear quantization is more statistics dependent. This work chooses the former one as the case study for the nonlinear quantization.
Introducing quantization loss could release the dynamic range and maintain parallelism with reduced energy consumption and latency from ADC. However, the quantization method combined
with the MAC mapping method could change the partial sum distribution even for the same task.
As the ADC precision reduction heavily depends on partial sum distribution, we need to explore
the best point for each task on a different design.
3.3 Post-ADC Digital Processing
As mentioned before, the weight matrixes are partitioned into different subarrays, and the MACs
are digit-wise. Thus, the MACs from different precision need to be shift-added across different significant digits, and the partial sums from different subarrays are added together using pure digit
circuits. These digit circuits are assumed to be fixed-point whose precisions are decided by the
ADC output. The ADC output precision will be the same as ADC precision for the linear quantization. However, for the nonlinear quantization, the LUT will give a higher-precision ADC output to
represent the nonlinear levels, thus introducing higher hardware overhead for the digital circuit.
The final outputs of the Conv/FC layers will be high-precision fixed-point values. According to
the network structure or quantization method, some intermediate functions, such as BN and/or
scaling, need to be applied between two layers of Conv/FC. Some works have proposed using low
precision operations for these functions with some modifications in the flow [11, 12]. Since this
work mainly focuses on the CIM structure, we use a floating-point unit (FPU) to support these
operations. We also assume that if there are back-to-back BN and Scaling, they will be merged into
one FPU operation. This assumption will not affect the system-level performance as the computations of Conv/FC layers dominates the hardware performance.

CONCLUSION

In this work, we explored the design space of the CIM in terms of algorithm-to-hardware mapping.
We demonstrated the basic design flow of neural network mapping and explored two software
quantization methods (i.e., DF and WAGE) combined with different MAC mapping approaches
(cell precision, number representation methods). By analyzing the hardware performance, we find
that the quantization method with high input sparsity will help to improve the energy efficiency
of the CIM accelerators, which is the DF according to our assessment. Compared to the input
distribution, the weight distribution is less affected by different quantization methods utilized in
this work, thus leading to similar energy consumption. However, increasing the weight sparsity
could also improve the energy efficiency, thus other techniques such as pruning could further
be introduced. In the extreme case of weight precision vs. cell precision, the mapping strategy
may also affect the digits’ sparsity and thus the energy consumption. In conclusion, sparsity could
improve the energy efficiency of the CIM architecture without any special techniques such as zero
skipping. When choosing the networks mapped to the CIM accelerator, improving the sparsity of
both input and weight digits is desired for better hardware performance.
We also evaluated the hardware performance with and without ADC quantization loss across
different design options. Low precision ADC could improve the hardware performance. Thus, it
is desired to have as much ADC precision loss as possible while maintaining the software performance. When mapping the same network to different architectures, its ADC requirements could
differ across the mapping schemes utilized. The differential pair scheme could tolerate the most
ADC quantization loss across all cell precisions while 2’s complement and shifted unsigned INT
require high precision for high precision cells and low precision cells separately. After further analysis, we conclude that a mapping scheme with identical distribution among weight/input digits
and components will be a better choice considering ADC quantization. We also found that while
the nonlinear ADC quantization could achieve 1-bit more ADC quantization loss for some tasks,
this trend is not guaranteed for all the tasks because of the initial accuracy loss. The nonlinear
ADC quantization is not recommended for the CIM accelerators considering the hardware cost
and difficulties in implementing nonlinear references.
For CIM designs with high-precision weights mapped to low-precision cells, 2’s complement
number representation is the best choice, regardless of whether ADC quantization loss is introduced. Considering the CIM designs equipped with memory cells that have comparable precisions
with weight precisions, the shifted unsigned INT is the best choice with full precision ADC, while
the differential pair should be selected if ADC loss is introduced. Under the extreme case that
weight precision equals or is even lower than the cell precisions, the differential pair and shifted
unsign INT could be comparable because of the peripheral digital circuits. For the analysis purpose, when designing the architecture for CIM, the main components that will affect the hardware
performance are cells needed per weight synapse (or subarray needed per weight array, the input/weight digital sparsity, ADC precision, and the interconnects. The higher the input/weight
digit sparsity and the cells need per weight synapse, and the lower the ADC precision, the better
the result will be. As the CIM-array becomes more and more energy-efficient, the interconnect will
show up and become the accelerator’s bottleneck. The mapping strategy decides the cells needed
per weight synapse under certain cell precision. The digit sparsity/distribution could be affected by
the network parameter statistics and the also the mapping strategy. The input/weight digit distribution mainly decides the ADC. Finally, the interconnect is mainly decided by the chip size considering the routing length, which could be highly decided by the subarray needed per weight array.
