Abstract—Analog Non-Volatile Memory-based accelerators offer high-throughput and energy-efﬁcient Multiply-Accumulate
operations for the large Fully-Connected layers that dominate
Transformer-based Large Language Models. We describe architectural, wafer-scale testing, chip-demo, and hardware-aware
training efforts towards such accelerators, and quantify the
unique raw-throughput and latency beneﬁts of Fully- (rather than
Partially-) Weight-Stationary systems.
Index Terms—In-memory computing, Non-volatile memory,
large language models, analog multiply-accumulate for DNN
inference, analog AI, deep learning accelerator, system modeling

I. I NTRODUCTION
Scaling the size of Deep Neural Network (DNN) models has been a
key driver of progress in artiﬁcial intelligence, ﬁrst in image recognition [1] and more recently in Large Language Models (LLMs) [2]. This
trend has greatly increased the amount of compute and energy needed
to train these very large networks with vast datasets [3, 2], as well as to
perform rapid inference on the resulting highly capable models [2]. The
Graphics Processing Units (GPUs) used for these compute tasks have
scaled greatly in capability (and quantity), but only modestly in energyefﬁciency. Reductions in computational precision since 2015 [4] have
now mostly saturated, and the beneﬁts of sparsity [5] are much less
applicable to the large Fully-Connected (FC) layers found in LLMs –
leaving technology node as the primary path to still higher efﬁciency.
Recently, numerous researchers are exploring Compute-In-Memory
(CIM) approaches [6, 7, 8] to increase energy-efﬁciency by performing
Multiply-Accumuate (MAC) operations within ON-chip memory
“Tiles,” thus markedly reducing the motion of model-weights and
partial sums. By exploiting such weight stationarity over short
timespans with Volatile Memories (VMs) – SRAM or e-DRAM [6,
8] – or over longer timespans with slower, ﬁnite-endurance NonVolatile Memories (NVMs) – Flash [9], Resistive-RAM [10], or phasechange memory (PCM) [11, 12] – such CIM approaches can offer high
throughput, low latency, and high energy-efﬁciency [6, 7, 8].

II. T RANSFORMER - BASED NETWORKS
While these beneﬁts can be seen across all DNN models, they
are particularly pronounced for workloads that exhibit large FC
layers, such as Transformer-based models [14, 13, 2]. Such models,

including BERT-base [13], BERT-large [13], and GPT-3 [2], use a
stack of 12, 24, or 96 identically-structured layers, each comprising
4 large FC-layers (In-projection, Out-projection, FC1, FC2) and a
self-attention block (Fig. 1). FC-compute operations (OPs) typically
dominate the workload, but scale linearly with sequence-length S ;
attention-compute OPs scale as S 2 .
Encoder-based models perform Natural Language Processing
(NLP) classiﬁcation-tasks (sentiment analysis, etc.) by pushing an
entire sequence of S “tokens” through the layer-stack, using all S 2
elements of each attention-matrix (within each of the attention-heads,
within each layer, Table 1). Decoder-based models generate one or
more new tokens with a similar pass, except the attention-block uses
masking and sparsity to reduce the amount of attention compute [2,
15] to support long sequences. In an encoder-stack, tokens need to
be staged before each attention-compute block, requiring signiﬁcant
local scratchpad and disrupting pipelining. In contrast, the masked selfattention in a decoder-stack can proceed in a more pipelined manner,
needing only a few incoming tokens before outgoing tokens can be
injected into the subsequent Out-Projection block. Note that encoderbased models are trained in two steps – extensive pre-training that
typically only needs to be performed once, followed by ﬁne-tuning
for the particular classiﬁcation task at hand. Ultra-large LLMs are
so capable after pre-training that sometimes only simple “prompt
engineering” is required.
The sheer size of these Transformer-based LLMs calls for more
efﬁcient AI compute. While the macro-level energy-efﬁciency of a
CIM-Tile is demonstrably high [6, 8], it is non-trivial to deliver high
system energy-efﬁciency — but careful design can implement the
necessary Auxiliary digital compute, and can communicate excitationvectors between Tiles and Compute-cores with reasonable energy and
time [16]. VM-based CIM systems also need to occasionally move
weight-vectors, in order to reuse the same small set of Tiles for many
different FC-layers comprising different weight matrices.

III. A NALOG -FABRIC A RCHITECTURE
We recently introduced a highly programmable Analog-AI and
Compute-In-Memory accelerator architecture [16], which utilizes a
highly specialized set of Compute-cores and Tiles arranged within a
common building block known as an Analog Fabric (AF) (Fig. 2),
together with SRAM scratchpads, IO blocks, and a 2D Mesh on top of
the building blocks. Border-Guard circuits (BGs) are placed between
components to control dataﬂow over the 2D mesh for high throughput
and energy-efﬁciency across a wide variety of workloads, supporting
multi-casting of data-vectors traveling from Compute-cores to Tiles.
This on-chip interconnect helps enable system-level utilization and
efﬁciency well beyond current AI accelerator systems. Multiple AFs
can share the same chiplet, which can be tiled for system scale-up.

The Tiles are the primary MAC-compute and weight storage units
for the DNN inference workload. In NVM-based accelerators, the
conductance G of the analog resistive device implements synaptic
weight, while voltage level or duration implements input activations.
Activations arrive as parallel bit-streams via the 2D Mesh and are
converted into analog pulse durations, enabling parallel excitation of
the analog memory array. Multiply-accumulation is performed by the
combination of Ohm’s and Kirchhoff’s Current Laws (Fig. 2c) [17,
7]. The resulting raw summations are converted from analog current
and integrated charge back into digital vectors, which are then
transmitted using the 2D Mesh. Conversion is performed in parallel
using dedicated Current-Controlled Oscillator (CCO)-based Analogto-Digital Converters (ADCs) [12].
Given the ﬁnite endurance, and the slow and power-hungry programming of NVM-devices, such Analog-AI systems must be FullyWeight-Stationary (FWS) – every weight must be pre-programmed
before execution of the inference workload begins. While this requires
unique NVM-devices for each and every weight in the model, there are
numerous beneﬁts. No time or energy is spent moving weights during
the workload, allowing the maximum degree of pipelining supported
by the model. Adverse tradeoffs between efﬁciency and latency are
avoided, so that the system inherently reaches maximum throughput
and energy-efﬁciency at batch-sizes as low as b=2. Excess padding of
short sequences is also not required.
By optimizing a spatial distribution of Tiles and Compute-cores to
meet the needs of diverse networks (including LSTMs, Transformers,
and CNNs), together with a well-optimized 2D mesh-interconnect, we

showed that this Analog-AI CIM-based architecture can be 40×-140×
more energy-efﬁcient than the NVIDIA A100 (compared at b=8) across
networks of interest [16]. The NVM-Tile assumed here is based on
PCM, with characteristics (100 TOPS/W at 40 nsec integration-time)
taken from 14nm-node circuit-simulations [16].
We now turn to various challenges involving devices (accurate
programming of NVM devices within high-yield arrays), array- and
chip-circuitry (reducing nonlinearities, orchestrating vectorized communication, and efﬁciently performing specialized digital-compute),
and algorithms (preparing model weights, maximizing DNN accuracy).

IV. WAFER - SCALE TESTING
The vast number of NVM CIM-Tiles needed (Table 1) must be
implemented at high-yield. While identifying completely dead chips
with Wafer-Scale Testing (WST) is straightforward, ﬂagging PCMbased Analog-AI chips that “work, but not very well” is more difﬁcult.
Unlike a digital memory characterized by bit-error-rate and fast writelatency, an Analog-AI array requires precise conductance-tuning across
broad resistance range. While array readout must be fast (nanosecond
regime), models are installed infrequently — so slow and complex
iterative-write-and-verify schemes that can maximize DNN accuracy
by reducing write-programming errors are preferred. Yet such iterative
schemes are far too slow for screening entire wafers. So we need to
gauge the suitability of the PCM arrays for Analog-AI using a very
small number of conductance-reads per device.
Since device-to-device variability primarily manifests as broadened
RESET (lowest-conductance) and SET (highest-conductance) distributions, we can perform efﬁcient WST for multicore PCM-based
Analog AI by examining the sharpness of the RESET and SET
Cumulative Distribution Functions (CDFs). Devices in an ideal array
should exhibit nearly-identical RESET and SET resistances with large
contrast (> 5×) [20]. Fig. 3 shows how this maximum achievable
SET conductance varies across Tiles, chips, and a full wafer. Chips
identiﬁed by WST as having either poor contrast or excessively high
SET distributions tend to exhibit poor weight-programming accuracy.

VI. H ARDWARE -AWARE T RAINING OF DNN S
We can further increase the robustness of DNN models by performing hardware-aware (HWA) training, preparing the network for the

various imperfections in the Matrix-Vector-Multiplications (MVMs)
performed on NVM-based CIM-Tiles. These arise from a variety of
noise sources, nonlinearities and quantization effects. HWA training
requires a model that is accurate enough to be predictive while still
being amenable to the rapid computation needed to perform DNN
training in reasonable time. HWA is analogous to quantization-aware
training (QAT) methods now commonly used when deploying DNNs
onto reduced-precision digital hardware [22].
For instance, the image captioning network shown in Fig. 5 was
trained with HWA techniques using a noise-model carefully matched
to measured characteristics of the NVM-CIM tiles on the 64-Tile chip.
In fact, starting with a model trained to high-accuracy with FP32,
HWA training can greatly enhance the robustness of a variety of deep
neural networks (DNNs) including recurrent neural networks (RNNs),
and transformers (Table 2) as well as convolutional neural networks
(CNNs) (not shown here, see Ref [18, 21]). In general, recurrent
networks (such as LSTMs and RNNT) are the most robust to NVMbased CIM-Tile imperfections. Transformer models are reasonably
robust, although models with fewer degrees-of-freedom tend to be less
robust. For instance, ALBERT has the same number of operations as
BERT-base but reuses the the same weights for all layers. While this
may decrease the model footprint, there is a cost in both raw testerror performance and in robustness. In contrast, using HWA training
techniques to perform the ﬁne-tuning for each GLUE classiﬁcationtask can provide fully software-equivalent accuracy on BERT-base, at
least until the accumulated effects of one year of PCM conductancedrift after programming [18]. HWA training of RoBERTa – an
optimized version of BERT trained on a much larger dataset – can
provide even lower absolute test-errors (Table 2).
As a result, we should be able to prepare many high-yield chiplets
organized into an NVM-based Analog Fabric, accurately program
model weights made highly robust with HWA-based ﬁne-tuning, and
have an expectation to obtain the same accuracies as any digital system
using ∼4-bit weights and 8-bit excitations, with high energy-efﬁciency.

VII. B ENEFITS OF F ULL W EIGHT S TATIONARITY
But one might ask: “Why make the effort to provide all the NVM
CIM-Tiles needed for a Fully-Weight-Stationarity (FWS) system?
Since a Partially-Weight-Stationary (PWS) system can greatly reduce
the number of Tiles, perhaps weight-reloading can be infrequent
enough that it is not troublesome?” To answer these questions, we
employ the same performance simulator used in Ref [16].
The AF used in Ref [16] was optimized for performance across
a wide variety of workloads. Here we introduce two new AFs
Fig. 2(a,b), designed speciﬁcally for BERT-base and BERT-large,
containing exactly enough tiles, MC-cores, CCL and CCH cores for
one layer of BERT-large (a few Compute-cores and Tiles are powergated for BERT-base). Die-to-Die (D2D) I/O cores are added to
provide sufﬁcient interconnect between FWS chiplets. Both systems
use exactly the same Tile characteristics (100 TOPS/W, 40nsec
integration). This Tile performance (10.9 TOPs/sq.mm) matches or
exceeds existing SRAM-based CIM-Tiles [23].
Our AF architecture was adapted for VM-based CIM tiles, to implement a highly-efﬁcient PWS system by adding additional scratchpad
(MC) cores (Fig. 2(b)), both for staging of excitations (b × S tokens)
each time the workload passes through the currently-programmed
VM-CIM tiles, and for weight-storage across all the layers (up to
12 MB, 10 sq.mm per MC). (Alternatively, weights could be loaded
from DRAM, albeit at a signiﬁcant energy cost.) To reduce data-trafﬁc
and reduce weight rewriting, odd layers progress from south to north,
even layers from north to south. These choices, and the fraction of
the network implemented between reloading, will drive interesting

Analog non-volatile memory (NVM)-based accelerators can offer
high-throughput and energy-efﬁcient multiply-accumulate (MAC)
operations for the large Fully-Connected (FC) layers that dominate
Transformer-based Large Language Models (LLMs). We describe
a highly heterogeneous and programmable accelerator architecture
for DNN inference that combines Compute-In-Memory (CIM) Tiles
for weight-stationary, energy-efﬁcient MAC operations, together with
heterogeneous special-function Compute-cores for auxiliary digital
computation. Massively parallel vectors of neuron-activation data are
exchanged over short distances using a dense and efﬁcient circuitswitched 2D mesh, with analog compute implemented using Ohm’s
law and Kirchhoff’s current law on arrays of resistive memory devices.
If weights are accurately programmed onto NVM devices and MAC
operations are sufﬁciently linear, competitive DNN accuracies can be
obtained, aided by Hardware-Aware training techniques. Even though
a Fully-Weight-Stationary system does occupy more area, it offers
so much throughput that it effectively pays for itself in terms of
TOPS/sq.mm. We show that an FWS system can offer a 6× higher
raw-throughput and 7× faster latency.
