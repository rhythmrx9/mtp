Artificial intelligence (AI) edge devices1–12 demand high-precision energy-efficient
computations, large on-chip model storage, rapid wakeup-to-response time and
cost-effective foundry-ready solutions. Floating point (FP) computation provides
precision exceeding that of integer (INT) formats at the cost of higher power and
storage overhead. Multi-level-cell (MLC) memristor compute-in-memory (CIM)13–15
provides compact non-volatile storage and energy-efficient computation but is prone
to accuracy loss owing to process variation. Digital static random-access memory
(SRAM)-CIM16–22 enables lossless computation; however, storage is low as a result of
large bit-cell area and model loading is required during inference. Thus, conventional
approaches using homogeneous CIM architectures and computation formats impose
a trade-off between efficiency, storage, wakeup latency and inference accuracy. Here
we present a mixed-precision heterogeneous CIM AI edge processor, which supports
the layer-granular/kernel-granular partitioning of network layers among on-chip
CIM architectures (that is, memristor-CIM, SRAM-CIM and tiny-digital units) and
computation number formats (INT and FP) based on sensitivity to error. This layergranular/kernel-granular flexibility allows simultaneous optimization within the
two-dimensional design space at the hardware level. The proposed hardware
achieved high energy efficiency (40.91 TFLOPS W−1 for ResNet-20 with CIFAR-100
and 28.63 TFLOPS W−1 for MobileNet-v2 with ImageNet), low accuracy degradation
(<0.45% for ResNet-20 with CIFAR-100 and for MobilNet-v2 with ImageNet) and
rapid wakeup-to-response time (373.52 μs).

Artificial intelligence (AI) edge devices for real-world applications
are expected to provide high-precision computation (for reliability),
high energy efficiency (for limited battery life) and rapid wakeupto-response time (for mostly off scenarios). Existing AI edge processors
with high-precision dot-product engines based on the von Neumann
architecture provide robust accuracy and high flexibility across various applications; however, the energy efficiency is low and wakeupto-response speed is slow, owing to the ‘memory wall’ (that is, the data
transfer bottleneck between compute and storage units)1–3, which
increases energy consumption and latency during wakeup and inference operations.
Recently, various AI edge processors4–12 have used an integrated
compute-in-memory (CIM) macro to enable dot-product computation
within the memory macro and thereby reduce the energy consumption
and latency associated with the transfer of weight and intermediate
data. Among various types of CIM implementation, non-volatile CIM
(nvCIM)9–15,23–47 provides the high-density on-chip non-volatile memory

(NVM) required to store the neural network (NN) model to eliminate
data transfer after power-up; however, it lacks robust accuracy owing
to process variation, particularly in MLC operations. Previous works
using self-designed analogue13 or MLC devices exceeding 2 bits per
cell14,15 have achieved good results with relatively simple NN models
and datasets; however, further assessments based on foundry-ready
memristors will be required. Digital static random-access memory
(SRAM)-CIM16–22 provides lossless computation with limited memory
capacity (owing to large bit-cell area), which necessitates frequent
data transfer from non-volatile storage during inference operations.
The computation number format adds another dimension to the
design space. Floating point (FP) computation is commonly used for
applications that require complex NN models and high accuracy; however, it requires higher memory capacity and computation energy,
owing to the use of high-precision input (IN) and weight (W) data. Integer (INT) computation lacks the precision of FP computation; however,
it provides high energy efficiency for applications of low to medium

handle a range of arithmetic functions, including ReLU, pooling, batch
normalization and exponent processing. These modules are essential
for an end-to-end demonstration of inference without the need for
a field-programmable gate array (FPGA) host. The main control and
input/output modules process commands, image inputs and the output of inference results. The ability to configure the CIM architecture
and computation number format makes it possible to optimize several conflicting metrics, which would otherwise impose performance
trade-offs.

Figure 2b,c respectively illustrate the assignment of layer-granular
INT–FP format for ResNet-20 layers when applied to simple and complex datasets. The pure-FP mode is ideally suited to the first and last
few layers, which tend to be highly sensitive to computing errors48–50.
Hidden layers tend to be more resilient to noise; however, the degree
of error tolerance varies with the datasets. For simple datasets such as
MNIST51 and CIFAR-10 (ref. 52), the low noise sensitivity in hidden layers
makes the pure-INT mode optimal for maximizing energy efficiency
without sacrificing accuracy. Conversely, for more complex datasets
such as CIFAR-100 (ref. 52) and ImageNet53, the high noise sensitivity
makes modes such as INT-input-FP-weight or FP-input-INT-weight the
best choices.
Figure 2d presents a flow chart and mathematical example of a
dot-product operation for a single output pixel in pure-FP mode. The
weights are pre-aligned offline and their maximum exponent (known as
the weight shared exponent) is extracted before the dot-product operation. The weights and their shared exponents are then programmed
into the memristor array in accordance with the specifics of the NN
model. During runtime, the inputs undergo on-chip pre-alignment
and the maximum exponent of the inputs (referred to as the input
shared exponent) is extracted. The mix-CIM engine then computes
dot products using the pre-aligned inputs and weights. The resulting
partial dot-product values (pDPVs) are combined with the shared input
and weight exponents, thereby completing the dot-product operation
and outputting the correct final dot-product value (DPV). Note that
aligning the inputs and weights separately is mathematically equivalent
to aligning the dot products.

Layer-granular INT–FP computation format
As shown in Fig. 2a, the layer-based INT–FP hybrid-mode controller
offers a unique layer-granular INT or FP configuration separately
for both inputs and weights in the following four modes: pure-INT,
INT-input-FP-weight, FP-input-INT-weight and pure-FP modes. Pure-INT
mode maximizes energy efficiency at the expense of computing accuracy, owing to its limited data range and low bit precision. Pure-FP
mode enables the highest computing accuracy at the expense of energy
efficiency. The INT-input-FP-weight and FP-input-INT-weight modes
provide alternatives for situations requiring a balance of the two. Note
that the FP-input-INT-weight mode occupies less non-volatile storage,
whereas the INT-input-FP-weight mode achieves slightly higher energy
efficiency and accuracy by eliminating the need for on-chip alignment.

Figure 3a illustrates the offline kernel assignment procedure. First,
the pretrained model is sent for kernel feature extraction. For each
kernel, the kernel feature values for FP and INT modes are respectively
defined as the shared exponent (shared-exp) and mean value. Note that
kernels with high kernel feature values have a greater impact on the
inference accuracy. Second, the mix-CIM assignment maps each kernel
to the most suitable CIM architecture, based on the extracted kernel feature value. Insensitive kernels, such as those with small shared-exp in FP
mode or small mean value in INT mode, are mapped to memristor-CIM
to enable highly energy-efficient dot-product operations. Sensitive
kernels, such as those with large shared-exp in FP mode or large mean
value in INT mode, are computed in SRAM-CIM to enable dot-product
operations of high accuracy. Specialized kernels with low weight-data
reuse or low compute parallelism are assigned to the tiny-digital unit,
which provides flexibility in dot-product operations. Finally, the kernels
and their mix-CIM assignment flags are programmed to the processor.

Measurement platform set-up and timing-extraction circuit
with measurement methodology for wakeup-to-response
latency
Extended Data Fig. 1a presents the set-up of the experiment used to
characterize the performance of the hybrid-mode mix-CIM processor.
The set-up included an oscilloscope, data generator, logic analyser
and power supply. The proposed hybrid-mode mix-CIM processor was
designed for end-to-end inference operations, thereby eliminating the
need for an external FPGA host. The presence of a large on-chip NVM
array also eliminated the need for external off-chip memory (DRAM or
flash). The data generator was responsible for transmitting instructions
and input images to the hybrid-mode mix-CIM processor. The oscilloscope captured waveforms used to characterize chip performance
(for example, wakeup-to-response latency) under a given computing
task. The logic analyser captured the intermediate outputs and final
inference results for design validation. The power supply provided
two VDDs to the memristor-SRAM CIM-fusion processor, including a
standard VDD voltage of 0.8 V and a high VDD voltage of 2.5 V.
Extended Data Fig. 1b,c illustrates the test-mode circuit and experiment methodology used to measure wakeup-to-response latency. A
timing-extraction circuit using a D-type flip-flop (DFF)-based timingextraction scheme was included on-chip to ensure precise measurements34–36. The initiation phase began with the activation of the
processor through an inference-start-flag (FlagSTART) signal, whereas
the inference-end-flag (FlagEND) signal was set to a low state. Once
the processor completed the inference task, it output the inference
result and toggled the FlagEND signal from low to high. The timing difference between the onsets of FlagSTART and the DFF clock (DFF_CLK) was
increased incrementally until the rising edge of DFF_CLK was aligned
with the transition of the FlagEND signal to high. Wakeup-to-response
latency was determined by measuring the time difference between
FlagSTART and DFF_CLK. The timing-extraction circuit test-mode circuit
matches the delays of DFF_CLK and FlagEND to obtain accurate measurements of chip wakeup-to-response latency, while excluding the delay
caused by parasitic capacitance and resistance associated with the
PCB and on-chip wiring.
Experiment results of the INT–FP hybrid-mode controller
Extended Data Fig. 2 presents experiment results indicating the performance of the INT–FP hybrid-mode controller, which uses both INT
and FP formats for the inputs and weights used in NN computation.
This approach provides the flexibility required to achieve a balance
between energy efficiency and inference accuracy. Extended Data
Fig. 2a,b present the measured energy efficiency and inference accuracy
of the hybrid-mode mix-CIM processor when using a ResNet-20 model
trained for CIFAR-100 under various input and weight formats. Energy
efficiency ranged from 25.35 TOPS W−1 to 75.38 TOPS W−1, whereas inference accuracy ranged from 68.78% to 69.59%.
The pure-INT mode demonstrated remarkable energy efficiency at
the expense of inference accuracy degradation owing to reduced bit
precision, whereas the pure-FP mode demonstrated robust inference
accuracy at the expense of energy efficiency. The INT-input-FP-weight
and FP-input-INT-weight modes provided a balance of energy efficiency
and inference accuracy. The INT–FP hybrid mode improved accuracy
degradation when transitioning from FP mode to INT mode to enhance
energy efficiency. Extended Data Fig. 2c compares the figure of merit
(FoM) obtained under hybrid mode with those of the constituent modes.
Hybrid mode improved the FoM (defined as the ratio of energy efficiency
to inference accuracy degradation) by between 1.79 and 3.09 times.

Implementation of MLC-based memristor-CIM and SRAM-CIM
Extended Data Fig. 4a illustrates the simplified structure of the
MLC-based memristor-CIM macro, which includes a memristor cell
array to store weight data, bitline (BL) voltage (VBL) regulators to control
VBL during dot-product operations, 5-bit-resolution analogue-to-digital
converters (ADCs)9 to convert analogue domain partial dot products on
BLs into the digital domain and an accumulator to combine partial dot
products (5-bit) as DPVs. The weight data are stored in two’s complement format on the same row (WL) across different columns. Each MLC
cell encodes ‘11’, ‘10’, ‘01’ and ‘00’ in its cell resistance as RLRS, 3/2xRLRS,
3xRLRS and RHRS, respectively. When a clamping voltage is applied to the
BL during a dot-product operation, the memory cell current remains
proportional to the dot-product result as ILRS, 2/3ILRS, 1/3xILRS and IHRS.
During a dot-product operation, the inputs are sent to the MLC-based
memristor-CIM for dot-product computation through the wordline
(WL). Each memristor cell multiplies a given input by a weight stored
in the memory cell. A BL then accumulates the multiplication results
from activated memory cells in the same column to generate analogue
BL current (IBL). Each IBL is read out by a 5-bit-resolution ADC, which
has full precision to the analogue signal levels, to generate the partial
dot product. An accumulator combines the partial dot products with
their respective place values from the MSB readout channel to the LSB
readout channel to generate the DPV.
Extended Data Fig. 4b details the circuit implementation of the
mux-based SRAM-CIM structure designed specifically to ensure the
accuracy of dot-product operations for high-impact kernels. The
mux-based SRAM-CIM layout comprises 64 subarrays (organized
into 8 rows and 37 columns), which store the weight data required for
computation. Each subarray is accompanied by a dedicated mux-based
compute unit (MCU), which performs dot-product operations involving two bitwise inputs and two word-wise weights. The mux-based
SRAM-CIM structure also includes a digital adder tree, which accumulates the outputs of 64 pairs of dot products, resulting in the equivalent
of 128 accumulations.
Each memory row contains a pair of 12-bit weights, denoted as
WN[11:0] and WN+1[11:0], and their sum (WN[11:0] + WN+1[11:0]). These
are programmed into the subarray using the weight-combined write
circuit. A maximum of 64 subarrays are activated during dot-product
operations and each subarray loads data from a single row into its
corresponding MCU. The MCU performs the dot-product operation
between two bitwise inputs and two word-wise weights by selecting one
of four possibilities (0, WN[11:0], WN+1[11:0] and WN[11:0] + WN+1[11:0]).
The selection is based on the value of INN[x] and INN+1[x], with specific
combinations given as follows: 0 for both inputs as 0, WN[7:0] for
INN[x] as 1 and INN+1[x] as 0, WN+1[11:0] for INN[x] as 0 and INN+1[x] as
1 and WN[11:0] + WN+1[1:0] for both inputs as 1. The digital adder then
accumulates 64 pDPVs throughout all input bits, resulting in a final DPV.

Proposed 2D-PVA-DA in memristor-CIM
Extended Data Fig. 5 compares the conventional approach to accumulation versus the proposed two-dimensional place-value-aware
dynamic accumulation (2D-PVA-DA) scheme using memristor-CIM.
Extended Data Fig. 5a presents the conventional accumulation scheme
that uses a fixed number of accumulations for all input and weight
place values, whereas the 2D-PVA-DA scheme adjusts the number of
accumulations according to the place values of inputs and weights.
This is meant to increase the energy efficiency and throughput of
multiply–accumulate (MAC) operations performed in CIM without
compromising accuracy.
The 2D-PVA-DA divides the weights into two groups (MSBs and
LSBs), based on their place values. MSBs have a greater impact on inference accuracy, therefore we limit the number of accumulations used
to compute them to preserve the accuracy of dot-product operations.
LSBs are subjected to a larger number of accumulations to improve
the throughput and energy efficiency of dot-product operations. The
same approach is taken to deal with inputs. The two-dimensional bar
plot in Extended Data Fig. 5b presents the number of accumulations
resulting from the proposed 2D-PVA-DA as a function of input and
weight.
Extended Data Fig. 5c presents the simulated operational cycle
counts of conventional accumulation and 2D-PVA-DA, in which
the operational cycle count is directly proportional to energy efficiency and latency. The 2D-PVA-DA reduced the operational cycle
count by 42%, thereby highlighting its effectiveness in improving the energy efficiency and throughput of MAC operations in
memristor-CIM.
Layer-wise configurations and experiment results for keyword
spotting and visual wake word detection application
Extended Data Fig. 6a illustrates the layer-wise configuration of the
ResNet-20 model trained for CIFAR-100 using hybrid mode and mix-CIM.
The sensitivity of the initial and final layers of the NN to computational
error48–50 necessitates the use of the pure-FP mode for these layers.
Note that hidden layers are more resilient to noise; however, they
are still prone to accuracy degradation when dealing with complex
datasets. To mitigate this issue, the hidden layers are assigned the
INT-input-FP-weight format. The memristor-CIM and SRAM-CIM units
are activated concurrently to handle convolution layers with insensitive and sensitive kernels, respectively. The SRAM-CIM units and the
tiny-digital unit are simultaneously activated to process fully connected
layers. This configuration using mix-CIM and hybrid mode allows for
efficient and accurate processing of the ResNet-20 model trained for
CIFAR-100.
Extended Data Fig. 6b presents the layer-wise configuration of the
MobileNet-v2 model trained for ImageNet using hybrid mode and
mix-CIM. The layer-wise configuration of this model is similar to that
of ResNet-20, in which the first and last few layers use pure-FP mode,
whereas the hidden layers use INT-input-FP-weight. In pointwise and
standard convolution layers, memristor-CIM units are activated to
manage insensitive kernels at the same time that SRAM-CIM units
are activated to manage sensitive kernels. SRAM-CIM units and the
tiny-digital unit are used simultaneously to process fully connected
and depthwise convolution layers.

common AI tasks, including keyword spotting and visual wake word
detection. Keyword spotting was performed using the Google Speech
Commands dataset56 with DS-CNN. Visual wake word detection was
performed using the Visual Wake Words Dataset57 with MobileNet-v2.
This figure also presents a breakdown of computing modes as well as
corresponding energy efficiency and accuracy degradation under
each scenario.

Flow chart illustrating our systematic approach to layergranular and kernel-granular assignment
Extended Data Fig. 7a illustrates the process of quantizing the pretrained model using the proposed two-dimensional quantization
and assignment (2DQA) method. Optimization in the first dimension
involves the layer-level assignment of a computation format (INT, FP
or INT–FP hybrid), whereas optimization in the second dimension
involves the kernel-level allocation of an engine according to its precision capability. Optimizing both the precision and engine allocation
makes it possible to achieve the optimal balance between accuracy
and computational overhead, thereby enhancing energy efficiency
without sacrificing inference accuracy.
Extended Data Fig. 7b illustrates our systematic approach to layergranular and kernel-granular assignment. Error injection provides concise guidelines by which to determine the sensitivity of each layer, which
can then be used to streamline the process of deriving a usable configuration without having to assess all possible combinations iteratively.
This study opted for post-training quantization58 as an alternative
to quantization-aware training, which is impractical for edge devices,
owing to the resource-intensive process of retraining. In the first stage,
quantization from FP to INT format59 is performed sequentially from
the first layer to the last. Note that, after each layer is quantized (with
the other layers frozen), inference operations are performed to assess
the resulting accuracy. This makes it possible to rank the layers according to sensitivity to quantization error. A computing format is then
assigned to each layer in accordance with these rankings with the aim
of optimizing performance.
In the second stage, we extract either the shared exponent of each
kernel (in FP format) or the absolute mean value of each kernel (in INT
format). This information is then used to guide the assignment of kernel
granularity, indicating the dominance of each kernel. This two-stage
assignment process loops iteratively until the target inference accuracy
is achieved, after which the resulting configurations are implemented
in our proposed architecture.
As shown in Extended Data Fig. 7c, the proposed scheme outperformed existing mixed-precision architectures11,47,60,61 in terms of
inference accuracy, particularly when applied to the complex ImageNet database. Note that, when applied to these datasets, previous
methods that rely on a single engine with fixed precision or dynamic
mixed-precision format are unable to maintain precision without a
substantial increase in computational overhead. The proposed 2DQA
scheme overcomes this limitation by enabling layer-level precision
assignment and kernel-level engine allocation to optimize the trade-off
between accuracy and hardware efficiency. It also minimizes quantization errors and maximizes inference performance to improve accuracy.
Illustration of the pre-alignment process for FP inputs and
weights and comparison of alignment methods for FP operations
Extended Data Fig. 8a illustrates the standard FP format and fixed-point
(INT-like) format after pre-alignment. The standard FP format comprises three parts: sign, exponent and mantissa. The FP value is represented as follows: (−1)sign × 2Exp−Bias × 1.Mantissa2, in which ‘Bias’ refers to
the offset of the exponent from the FP standard. After pre-aligning a
group of FP numbers, they are transformed into the fixed-point format,
which includes a signed pre-aligned mantissa for each number and a
shared exponent for the entire group. The fixed-point value is represented as 2Exp_S−Bias × Signed PA_Mantissa2. The fixed-point (INT-like)
expression is used in our mix-CIM engine for subsequent computation.
Extended Data Fig. 8b illustrates common alignment methods and
their comparisons. The product alignment scheme, in which—before
accumulation—Product (PDM = INM × WM) alignment-based FP-MACs align
the product’s mantissa (PDM) based on the product’s exponent (PDE-MAX).
This method has been widely used in digital circuits62 and near-memory
compute macros63; however, it has been proved impractical for

=== page 10 ===
Article
in-memory-compute macros, owing to variability in PDE-MAX hindering
accumulation in various physical row/column configurations.
The input pre-alignment scheme, as outlined in ref. 25. Before the
dot-product operation, the input mantissa is aligned according to the
product’s maximum exponent, whereas the weight remains unaligned.
This CIM-friendly approach streamlines accumulation within the same
column; however, it struggles when dealing with variable weights across
channels, each of which has its own shared exponent. This problem
could be alleviated by reducing the array size (that is, allocating one
array to each weight channel); however, this would negate the benefits
of high-density memristor cells.
The input-wise and layer-wise weight separate pre-alignment scheme.
Before the dot-product operation, the input mantissa is aligned according to its input maximum exponent, whereas the weights are aligned
according to the weight maximum exponent within each layer. Note,
however, that this approach results in marked data truncation, as all
layer weights are aligned to the same reference.
The proposed input and kernel-wise weight separate pre-alignment
scheme. Before the DP operation, the input mantissa is aligned according to its input maximum exponent, whereas the weight in each kernel is
aligned according to the weight maximum exponent of the corresponding kernel. This fine-grained alignment helps to reduce data truncation.
This architectural design facilitates the simultaneous computation of
different weight channels within the same array to enhance computational efficiency and parallel processing capability. Moreover, the
ability to perform post-training based on predetermined weights allows
for offline processing before programming within the memristor array.
Extended Data Fig. 8c,d respectively illustrate simulated energy
consumption under various alignment methods and the corresponding inference accuracy. The product alignment method yielded the
highest inference accuracy; however, it also resulted in the highest
energy consumption, owing to the fact that its structure prohibits
several accumulations in the same column. The energy consumption of
the input pre-alignment and input and weight separate pre-alignment
methods was far lower. Overall, the input-wise and kernel-wise weight
separate pre-alignment scheme achieved the lowest energy consumption for a given pre-alignment bit width, owing to its ability to execute
dot-product operations within different channels of the same array.
This method also achieved inference accuracy higher than that of the
input-wise and layer-wise weight separate pre-alignment scheme by
retaining more information from the weight mantissa.

Electrical behaviour, functional characteristics, transistor behaviour

model provided by a large semiconductor manufacturing foundry
(TSMC). Simulation results and waveforms were obtained using SPICE
Explorer and Nwave.

Hybrid-mode mix-CIM processor design visualization platform
The design of the building blocks used in the proposed hybrid-mode
CIM-fusion processor was established using Cadence Virtuoso and
NC-Verilog with a GUI capable of presenting circuits in the form of a
schematic to be transferred into a netlist file for electrical behaviour
simulation. Layout verification was facilitated using Calibre for design
rule checking (DRC), layout versus schematic (LVS), parasitic electrical
characteristic extraction (PEX) and antenna rule checking. The specifications of DRC, LVS and PEX were provided by TSMC.
Experimental platform
A test chip of the proposed hybrid-mode mix-CIM processor was fabricated using the TSMC 22-nm CMOS logic process with 1T1R memristive devices and compact layout rule 6-T SRAM bit cells. All peripheral
circuits were integrated on a single chip with the memristor cell array.
The bonding and packaging of test chips was performed by Integrated
Service Technology Inc. in Hsinchu, Taiwan.
Electrical measurements for captured waveforms
Voltages were supplied using an Agilent E3468A. Input control signals
and input data were generated using a Tektronix AFG3252 arbitrary
waveform generator and an Acute DG3096B digital signal generator.
A Tektronix MSO9104A oscilloscope and an Acute LA3136B+ logic analyser were used to capture the DOUT signal of the test chip by means
of a connector on the PCB.
Electrical measurements for shmoo plot
An Advantest CX-1000D-R was used to measure the supply voltage
versus computing latency. The resolution of VDD and measured computing latency were set at 0.05 V and 0.2 ns, respectively.
Network training environment
An NVIDIA A6000, four NVIDIA 3090, 13 NVIDIA 2080ti and four NVIDIA
Titan GPU cards were used to train the NN. The programming framework was based on the Python computing package PyTorch. Simulation
accuracy refers to the maximum accuracy on the test set after training
300 epochs.
