ABSTRACT
Memristor-based deep learning accelerators provide a promising solution to improve the energy eﬃciency of neuromorphic
computing systems. However, the electrical properties and
crossbar structure of memristors make these accelerators
error-prone. To enable reliable memristor-based accelerators,
a simulation platform is needed to precisely analyze the
impact of non-ideal circuit and device properties on the inference accuracy. In this paper, we propose a ﬂexible simulation
framework, DL-RSIM, to tackle this challenge. DL-RSIM simulates the error rates of every sum-of-products computation
in the memristor-based accelerator and injects the errors in
the targeted TensorFlow-based neural network model. A rich
set of reliability impact factors are explored by DL-RSIM,
and it can be incorporated with any deep learning neural network implemented by TensorFlow. Using three representative
convolutional neural networks as case studies, we show that
DL-RSIM can guide chip designers to choose a reliabilityfriendly design option and develop reliability optimization
techniques.

INTRODUCTION

Deep learning and neuromorphic algorithms have attracted
a lot of interest in recent years as they can provide optimal solutions for many classiﬁcation and regression tasks,
including image/speech recognition, data mining, and natural
language processing. These neuromorphic algorithms often
cost much more memory and computing resources than traditional methods, incurring tremendous data movements [4]. In
conventional von Neumann architecture where computation
and data storage are separated, such data movements become
critical performance and energy bottleneck [8].

The innovation of memristor-based computing-in-memory
accelerator provides a promising solution to reduce data
movements and boost the energy eﬃciency of neuromorphic
computation. Recent work demonstrated that metal-oxide
resistive random access memory (ReRAM) [11] can be used to
build a crossbar structure to eﬃciently perform matrix-vector
multiplications in convolution and fully-connected layers of
deep neural networks [7]. With the capability of performing
arithmetic operations beyond data storage, ReRAM-based
computing-in-memory accelerators can provide signiﬁcant
energy savings comparing to CPU or GPU solutions [3, 9].
While the electrical properties and crossbar structure of
ReRAM enable energy-eﬃcient neuromorphic computing,
these properties also make the ReRAM-based accelerators
error-prone. When performing matrix-vector multiplication
in a ReRAM crossbar array, multiple wordlines are activated
concurrently and the currents ﬂowed through diﬀerent cells in
the same bitline are accumulated to get the sum-of-products
result. Even if none of cells in a bitline is in error, the sumof-products result read out by the sense ampliﬁer may be
incorrect [5] mainly due to (1) the accumulation of read noise
from each activated cell in the bitline and (2) the imperfect
sense ampliﬁer with limited resolution. Since many factors
can aﬀect the reliability of ReRAM-based accelerators, a
simulation platform is needed to estimate the impact of current sensing errors and hardware constraints on the inference
accuracy of the targeted neural networks.
Prior work has studied the performance and energy eﬃciency of ReRAM-based deep learning accelerators [3, 9] and
proposed simulation tools to facilitate design space exploration [1, 12], but none of the existing simulation platforms
completely supports ﬂexible and precise error rate analysis.
The non-ideal circuit and device properties that cause the
current sensing errors are usually ignored in prior systemlevel work [3, 9]. Some circuit-level [1] and behavior-level [12]
simulation platforms take the impact of hardware errors into
consideration. However, the circuit-level tool [1] is hard to be
integrated with various neural networks while the behaviorlevel tool [12] provides only imprecise analysis on inference
accuracy. As a result, a ﬂexible and accurate simulation platform that fully explore the impact of hardware errors on
various neural network models is highly demanded.
In this paper, we propose a ﬂexible simulation framework,
DL-RSIM, for ReRAM-based deep learning accelerators to
analyze the impact of non-ideal circuit and device properties
on the inference accuracy of various neuromorphic computing

RERAM-BASED DEEP LEARNING
ACCELERATOR ARCHITECTURE

We ﬁrst present the architecture of typical ReRAM-based
deep learning accelerators. As shown in Figure 3, the ReRAMbased deep learning accelerator is composed of multiple computation units (CUs) connected with on-chip interconnects.
Each CU has multiple crossbar arrays, which are responsible for the acceleration of matrix-vector multiplications in
convolution and fully-connected layers. A wordline driver
(WLD), such as a digital-to-analog converter (DAC) or an
inverter [9], is connected to each wordline of the ReRAM
crossbar array, in order to convert the input feature map
data to input voltage. The accumulated currents on bitlines
(sum-of-products results) are read out by multi-level sense
ampliﬁers (SAs). As a multi-level SA consumes a signiﬁcant
amount of power and chip area [1, 9], each SA is shared by
multiple bitlines. In addition to the crossbar arrays, there is
one on-chip buﬀer in each CU for temporarily storing input
and output feature maps. A non-linear function (ReLU) unit
and a pooling unit are also included in the CU to support
the implementation of non-linear function and pooling layer
in the neural network.
When performing a matrix-vector multiplication (dotproduct computation), the input vector is encoded using
multiple clock cycles, as an area- and energy-eﬃcient WLD
usually has limited resolution [9]. Since a practical ReRAM
cell can store only a limited number of bits [7, 9], each data
in the original ﬁlter weight is also partitioned and mapped
onto diﬀerent bitlines. Ideally, all the wordlines in the crossbar array can be activated concurrently in one single cycle.

ReRAM Cell

The statistical randomness of ReRAM cell resistance is a
critical issue when aiming to design a reliable ReRAM-based
deep learning accelerator. Figure 4(a) shows the ReRAM cell
structure, with a metal-oxide material sandwiched between
two metal layers of electrodes. When a voltage is applied
across a ReRAM cell, a conductive ﬁlament made out of
oxygen vacancies is either formed (LRS, low resistance state)
or ruptured (HRS, high resistance state) depending on the
voltage polarity. Due to the randomness of the oxygen vacancy
generation, the shape of the conductive ﬁlament varies from
cycle to cycle even under the same programming condition.
This resistance variability of ReRAM greatly impacts the
error rate of matrix-vector multiplication. Recent work shows
that the resistance histograms for both LRS and HRS follow
the lognormal distribution [6]. As shown in Figure 4(b), the
resistance distribution is mainly determined by the mean
resistance of on-state (Ron ), the resistance ratio between
on and oﬀ state (R-ratio = Rof f /Ron ), and the resistance
deviation (σ) of each state. When the resistance distributions
of LRS and HRS are overlapped, it is hard to distinguish
between these two states at the overlapped region. As a result,
read error is more likely to occur on a cell with smaller Rratio and/or larger σ due to the larger overlapped resistance
region.

3.2

Operation Unit

In addition to the statistical randomness of ReRAM cell resistance, the size of the operation unit (SOU ) also aﬀects the
error rate of sum-of-products computation. When multiple
wordlines are activated simultaneously, the output current
(IBL ) of a bitline is equal to the summation of all activated
cell currents. Since the resistance of ReRAM cells follows a
lognormal distribution, the accumulated current on a bitline
also follows a statistical distribution. Higher number of activated wordlines implies the accumulation of more cell resistance variance, leading to larger overlapped regions between
neighbor states (sum-of-products values) in the accumulated
current distribution and higher error rates. Thus, practical
ReRAM crossbar designs [2, 10] restrict the maximum number of wordlines that can be activated concurrently (SOU )
to avoid serious accuracy degradation. Within an OU, the
actual number of activated wordlines depends on the number
of non-zeros inputs, as a wordline is turned oﬀ if the input is
zero. If there are many non-zero values in the feature maps
of the targeted neural network (i.e., many wordlines need to
be activated), reducing SOU degrades performance but can
help to reduce bitline sensing error rates.

However, only a limited number of wordlines can be activated
concurrently in a practical design [2, 10], as activating a massive number of wordlines consumes too much power and it is
hard to accurately read out the current accumulated from
too many cells at the SA. Thus, each original dot-product
computation is split into the dot-product computation of
multiple operation units (OUs), as shown in Figure 3, with
at most SOU wordlines activated at one single cycle. The
dot-product result of each OU are added together and temporarily stored at the register connected to the SA. After
summing up the dot-product result of diﬀerent OUs that
share the same set of bitlines, a shift-and-add circuit is used
to assemble the ﬁnal result based on the bit position of input
and synaptic weight.
Among these components in the CU, the inference accuracy
of the neural network is mainly aﬀected by the non-ideal
properties and design constraints of ReRAM cell, operation
unit, and sense ampliﬁer. In the following subsections, we
discuss the impact of these three components on sum-ofproducts error rates per bitline, and use an example to show
how these errors and hardware constraints aﬀect convolution
computation in neural networks.

3.3

Sense Ampliﬁer

The design of sense ampliﬁer aﬀects the read sensing error rate
of the sum-of-products result at each bitlines. Recent work
showed that dual-reference sensing method can provide larger
sensing margin and lower error rate than conventional sensing
scheme [2]. As shown in Figure 5, when dual-reference sensing
scheme is applied, the distance between the accumulated
current on the bitline (IBL ) and the reference current of the
higher state (IR [n+1]) is compared with the distance between
IBL and the reference current of the lower state (IR [n])
to determine the sum-of-products result. Considering the
impact of SA safety guard band delta voltage (dVSA ), which
represents the minimum voltage requirement to distinguish
diﬀerent states and is related to process variation, IBL is
sensed as the lower state if

Dot-Product Computation under Hardware
Constraints

To sum up the hardware constraints and non-ideal circuit/device properties mentioned above, we use an example to
show how these limitations aﬀect the process of dot-product
computation in neural networks. Figure 6(a) represents the
original dot-product computation. Due to the limited WLD
resolution, bits-per-cell, and OU size, the original feature
maps and ﬁlters are ﬁrst partitioned into smaller OU-based
bit-level computation units. We call this process Decomposition. As shown in Figure 6(b), the feature map is divided
based on the WLD resolution (1 bit in this example). For example, the ﬁrst data, 2, in the feature map is partitioned into
1 (MSB) and 0 (LSB). The original ﬁlter is also partitioned
into MSB and LSB columns based on the bits-per-cell (1 bit
in this example). Since only a limited number of wordlines
can be activated concurrently, the computation should be
split into several OU-based portions. Suppose that SOU =2,
the MSB and LSB of the feature map and ﬁlter are further
split into two portions.
After decomposition, OU-based dot-product computation
is performed, as shown in Figure 6(c). For example, at the
ﬁrst cycle, the dot-product of the LSBs of the upper-half
feature map (0,1) and the weights stored in OU1 is computed,
generating the ﬁrst output feature map (1,1). During the dotproduct computation, the unstable cell resistance and nonideal SA may generate error results, as shown in Figure 6(d).
In this example, the fourth output feature map is wrongly
sensed as (1,1) instead of original (1,0).
Finally, the OU-based bit-level results need to be resembled
to get the ﬁnal result of the dot-product computation and
pass to the next neural network layer. We call this process
Composition, as shown in Figure 6(e). During Composition,
the dot-product outputs of the same bit-position in the input
feature map but diﬀerent OUs are ﬁrst added together. For
example, the ﬁrst two outputs (1,1) and (0,0) are summed up
to get the new output (1,1). Then, shift-and-add of the new
outputs generated by the ﬁrst step is calculated based on the
bit-position of the input feature map to get the new result
(3,5) in the second step. At the last step, shift-and-add is
performed based on the bit-position of the ﬁlter, in order to
get the ﬁnal error-included dot-product result (3∗21 +5∗20 =
11). In this example, the original output, 9 (Figure 6(a)),
is changed to 11, due to the errors induced by unstable
ReRAM cells, accumulated currents at bitlines within an OU,
and non-ideal SA. To analyze the impact of these hardwareinduced errors on the inference accuracy of neural networks,

DL-RSIM: SIMULATION FRAMEWORK

DL-RSIM is a simulation framework that enables the analysis
on the impact of non-ideal circuit and device properties on
the inference accuracy for memristor-based deep learning
accelerators. Figure 7 shows the simulation framework of our
DL-RSIM, which is composed of two modules. The NVM Error Analytical Module takes a set of hardware conﬁgurations
(e.g., ReRAM cell properties, SA-related parameters, etc)
as input parameters, and estimates the error rates of each
sum-of-products computation per bitline within an OU. The
TensorFlow-based Inference Accuracy Simulation Module is
based on a typical TensorFlow inference ﬂow and incorporates the underlying ReRAM-based hardware conﬁguration
and estimated error rates of an OU from the NVM Error
Analytical Module to generate inference accuracy for the target input feature map and neural network model. All the
input hardware conﬁgurations are listed in Table 1 and the
detailed designs of each module are described in the following
subsections.

models the impact of accumulated current sensing errors

and integrate with the current sensing error rates per bitline

In this section, we ﬁrst validate the correctness of our DLRSIM by comparing the modeled current distribution with
SPICE. We then use three representative CNNs as case studies to show that DL-RSIM can help chip designers to select
reliability-friendly design options and develop reliability optimization techniques to reduce error rates.

5.1

Validation

We use the simulation results from SPICE to validate the correctness of DL-RSIM. With the same LRS and HRS resistance
distribution [6], we compare the accumulated current distribution derived from SPICE and DL-RSIM when SOU = 9.
When simulating the accumulated current on a bitline, SPICE
models the interference between cells, which is not modeled
in DL-RSIM. Figure 13 shows that the accumulated current
distribution generated by our analytical module is similar
to SPICE. The root mean square error between these two
accumulated current distributions is less than 0.5%.

the inference accuracy by varying the R-ratio from 10 to

R-ratio reduces the overlapped region between LRS and
HRS resistance distribution. For example, the error rate of 0
decreases from 25% to 0% when the R-ratio increases from
10 to 50. The decrease in error rates per bitline leads to the
improvement of inference accuracy, as shown in Figure 14(b).

propagation at deeper networks exacerbates the accuracy
degradation problem. These results imply that chip designers
can choose an ReRAM cell with higher R-ratio to improve
inference accuracy and deeper neural networks require larger
R-ratio to avoid serious accuracy loss.

CONCLUSION

We propose DL-RSIM, a ﬂexible simulation framework, to precisely explore the impact of non-ideal circuit and device properties on the inference accuracy of various neural networks.
DL-RSIM can help chip designers to choose reliability-friendly
options and develop reliability optimization techniques based
on DL-RSIM’s analysis. In the future, we plan to add performance and power estimation modules in DL-RSIM to enable
more comprehensive design space exploration.

RELATED WORK

The two most well-known simulation platforms for ReRAMbased neural network accelerators are MNSIM [12] and NeuroSim [1]. MNSIM is a behavior-level simulation platform
that provides ﬂexible interface for users through a hierarchical module structure, and it can be utilized to estimate the
area, power, latency, and inference accuracy. Nevertheless,
they use only worst and average case for simulation, resulting
in less precise estimation on inference accuracy. NeuroSim is
a circuit-level simulation tool that takes the impact of nonideal device and circuit properties into consideration when
estimating the area, power, and performance of ReRAMbased accelerators. However, NeuroSim’s architecture is less
ﬂexible, since it is ﬁxed on a 2-layer multi-level perceptron
(MLP) neural network. Comparing to these prior studies, our
DL-RSIM can provide detailed analysis on inference accuracy
under diﬀerent hardware conﬁgurations and support ﬂexible
simulation for any TensorFlow-based neural network models.

5.2.5 Workload-dependent sensing scheme. We propose
and evaluate a workload-dependent sensing scheme to show
that chip designers can use DL-RSIM to develop reliability
optimization techniques for ReRAM-based deep learning
accelerators. Figure 18 shows that small values (<5) occur
more often than large values (≥5) when performing OUbased dot-product computation in all these three datasets.
In particular, more than 40% of sum-of-products results
per bitline are zero due to the sparsity of feature maps
and synaptic weights in these datasets, especially MNIST.
Thus, we change the oﬀset of the reference current when
