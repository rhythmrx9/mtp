Traditional Deep Learning Accelerators (DLAs) rely on off-chip memory to store large weight tensors, leading
to high bandwidth demands and energy consumption. Compute-in-Memory (CIM) accelerators mitigate this
by integrating high-density, non-volatile memory arrays, enabling a fully weight-stationary (FWS) dataflow.
Multi-core CIM systems further enhance efficiency with cross-layer inference, where intermediate tensors
stay on-chip, and cores operate in a pipeline. Despite diverse architecture proposals, no existing tool models
the dataflow, memory access patterns and timing behaviour of multi-core CIM accelerators. We introduce
CIMFlow, a modelling framework for cross-layer CIM architectures. Our flexible Hardware Architecture
Model includes CIM and digital cores and leverages the buffets storage idiom for distributed token-based flow
control. An Array-OL-based Workload Model captures CNNs’ multidimensional dependencies and applies
hardware-aware transformations. These models are transformed into a timed cyclo-static dataflow graph
for simulation. CIMFlow delivers latency, energy and traces for core and buffer utilisation. Our case studies
on state-of-the-art CNNs show that cross-layer inference reduces latency by up to 52×. We also reveal that
neglecting memory access delays results in throughput overestimations of up to 308%. To our knowledge,

Introduction

Deep Learning (DL) applications have driven important advancements across different domains,
such as computer vision and natural language processing. Modern Deep Neural Networks (DNNs)
contain several hidden layers and have grown exponentially in scale and complexity over the years.
They incorporate thousands of parameters and perform millions of operations during inference. As
a result, DNNs demand extensive resources for both arithmetic operations and data movement.
To address this computational challenge, specialised Deep Learning Accelerators (DLAs) have
been developed in recent years [10, 16]. Conventional digital DLA architectures utilise synaptic
arrays arranged in a single core and process one layer at a time [27]. These systems rely on off-chip
memory to store all filter weights, inputs, outputs, and intermediate feature maps, thereby limiting
their throughput to the available memory bandwidth. This limitation is known as the Von-Neumman
bottleneck or memory wall [26].
Compute-in-Memory (CIM)-based accelerators are a promising solution to overcome the
memory wall. On-chip CIM-cores, equipped with non-volatile memory arrays, are used as storage
and computation units [11]. Demonstrator chips achieving low power massively parallel matrixvector multiplication (MVM) operations have been built using arrays of Resistive Random
Access Memory (RRAM) [34] and Phase Change Memory (PCM) [20]. These devices can
provide enough memory density to store all the weights of a small to medium DNN model onchip [26]. This way, a fully-weight-stationary (FWS) dataflow is possible, drastically reducing
the bandwidth and storage requirements for off-chip memories. Following these principles, several
architectures have been proposed, where multiple CIM-cores, on-chip buffers and interconnect are
integrated in a single chip [4, 15, 20, 31].
CIM DLAs with FWS dataflows can also take advantage of cross-layer inference, a scheduling
technique where the results from one layer are forwarded to the next before finishing its computation. Instead of processing the workload layer-by-layer, cross-layer inference schedules the
operations in a way that allows multiple layers to be processed concurrently [28]. This approach
creates a pipeline where different parts of the DNN are executed concurrently, significantly increasing the temporal utilisation of the processing elements and the throughput of the accelerator [28].
This concept is illustrated in Figure 1.
With cross-layer inference, feature maps are kept in on-chip buffers shared by producer and
consumer cores, further reducing the burden on the off-chip memory. However, cross-layer inference
also increases the complexity of the data orchestration between the different stages of this pipeline,
which is still an open problem in the literature.

Previous CIM-based accelerator proposals have recognised the importance of cross-layer inference [4, 15, 31]. However, a comprehensive model capable of representing and quantifying the
various data orchestration aspects of cross-layer inference for CIM DLAs has not been introduced.
Most design-space exploration (DSE) tools for DLAs were initially developed for digital accelerators and lack support for cross-layer inference [25, 27] or provide only limited functionality in
this area [32]. CLSA-CIM [28] is a scheduling tool designed explicitly for cross-layer scheduling
on FWS CIM architectures, but it does not consider the costs of data movement or orchestration
strategies.
This work aims to fill this gap by introducing CIMFlow, a framework for modelling and simulating
the dataflow in CIM-based DLAs. Our main contributions are:
— An Array-OL-based Workload Model for DNNs that expresses data dependencies and inherent
parallelism. This multidimensional representation is useful for performing hardware-aware
transformations such as splitting kernel weight tensors to fit within the CIM-Core dimensions
and creating tensor slices for cross-layer execution.
— A powerful Execution Model for cross-layer inference on multi-core CIM accelerators. This
extendable model uses a Timed Cyclo-Static Dataflow (TCSDF) graph to describe the
dataflow between compute and storage units in the target hardware platform.
— Through case studies with the CIMFlow framework on state-of-the-art (SOTA) DNN
workloads, we demonstrate that neglecting data transfer costs can lead to throughput overestimations of up to 308%.
The remainder of this article is structured as follows. Section 2 provides a summary of related
works. We present the foundational concepts for our work in Section 3. An overview of CIMFlow in
Section 4 is followed by a detailed description of our Hardware Architecture, Workload and Execution models in Sections 5, 6, and 7, respectively. In Section 8, we present case studies demonstrating
the capabilities of CIMFlow. Concluding remarks are summarised in Section 9.

CIM-based DLAs

The potential benefits of CIM for the acceleration of DNN workloads has resulted in numerous
architecture proposals [2, 4, 31] and demonstrator chips [15, 20, 34].
ISAAC [31] presents multi-core CIM architectures and demonstrates the importance of cross-layer
inference, which the authors refer to as pipelining. They employ weight duplication as a strategy to
balance the pipeline and increase the temporal utilisation of the CIM processing elements (PEs).
ISAAC employs a hierarchical interconnect where several tiles are connected using an on-chip
concentrated mesh. In each tile, a group of CIM cores, digital cores and buffers are connected with a

2.2 Cost Models and DSE Tools for CIM DLAs
Several cost models and DSE tools for DLAs have been presented in recent years [3, 25, 27, 30, 32].
Timeloop [27] is a well-established DSE exploration tool that integrates an analytical execution
model and a mapper that explores the loop orders, tiling and parallelisation options for a given
DNN layer. It targets digital single-core layer-by-layer digital accelerators. The Timeloop execution
model works based on loop-nest analysis and generates action counts for each component in the
hardware architecture. The technology library automation tool Accelergy [35] produces energy
statistics based on the action counts. Accelergy is basically a wrapper, providing a single interface
to established energy estimation tools, including CACTI [23] and NeuroSim [30].
The latest version of the Timeloop/Accelergy framework, called CiMLoop [3], extends the capabilities of the original framework to support CIM accelerators. More specifically, it allows modelling
data-value-dependent energy consumption, a key feature for CIM accelerators. Its architecture
description provides enough flexibility to model the different kinds of CIM accelerators, both at
circuit and architecture levels.
However, the major limitation of the Timeloop/Accelergy framework is that it does not support
cross-layer inference. This is a major drawback when considering the FWS mapping commonly
found in CIM accelerators, as the temporal utilisation of the PEs will be substantially lower when
inference happens in a layer-by-layer fashion.
Another DSE tool that has been used in the context of DLA is ZigZag [25]. ZigZag follows the
same principles as Timeloop. It uses a custom representation of the architecture, composed of
an array of multiply-accumulate (MAC) units and a memory hierarchy. The workload is also
represented as a set of independent loop nests, which are then mapped one-by-one to the hardware
architecture.
Stream [32] uses ZigZag as a cost model to schedule whole workload graphs instead of analyzing
each layer individually, allowing it to explore cross-layer scheduling possibilities. To do so requires
the user to specify the granularity to use when scheduling each layer. The scheduler in Stream
uses a genetic algorithm to explore the design space, which is particularly useful for multicore
digital accelerators, where the number of possible mappings is large. However, CIM DLAs typically employ large high-density 2D arrays for computation, and so the mapping space is more
reduced [26].
CLSA-CIM [28] is a scheduler specifically designed for CIM architectures based on RRAM. It
introduces a novel cross-layer scheduling approach compatible with FWS systems. In their case
studies, the combined benefits of cross-layer execution and weight duplication are quantified. This

Background

This section introduces key concepts that lay the foundation for the models in CIMFlow. We start
by describing the major operations in DNNs in Section 3.1. Two models for describing parallel
applications, Array-OL, and TCSDF, follow in Sections 3.2 and 3.3, respectively. The buffet storage
idiom, which enables efficient data sharing in multi-core systems, is described in Section 3.4.
3.1

Operations in DNNs

DNNs are composed of multiple layers arranged in a directed network [21]. In serial architectures,
layers form a single sequential chain, whereas in residual networks, fork and join nodes create and
merge multiple computational paths. The input and output of each layer are multidimensionalal
arrays known as tensors. Beyond 2D convolutions, which are discussed in the next section, SOTA
DNNs commonly incorporate layers such as dense (fully connected), addition, concatenation,
max/average pooling, and reshape.

Array-OL

Array-OL is a specification language for multidimensional intensive Digital Signal Processing
(DSP) applications [14]. The applications are represented as a graph of tasks that operate on
multidimensional arrays. The language presents semantics to express data access patterns and
inter-task dependencies.
In Array-OL, data-parallel computations are specified using repetition tasks, which define how a
basic operation is repeatedly applied across an input-output space. In each repetition, a sub-array of
the input is processed to produce a sub-array of the output. These sub-arrays, called patterns, are regions with a fixed shape, regular spacing, and consistent placement, which are specified using tilers.

3.3

Timed Cyclo-Static Dataflow

Synchronous Dataflow (SDF) [22] is a model of computation where a set of nodes, called actors,
exchange data tokens with constant consumption and production rates. Each actor consumes and
produces a constant number of tokens per firing. This predictability allows for static analysis and
optimisation, making SDF suitable for modelling real-time signal processing applications [9]. The
tokens represent the atomic pieces of data transmitted between actors. Heterogeneous data token
types can coexist in a single dataflow graph, with each communication channel generally associated
with a single type of data token [9].
This work employs the cyclo-static extension that replaces the constant production and consumption rates with finite ordered lists [7]. CSDF graphs retain SDF’s analysability while enabling
the modelling of periodic execution phases. Window-based algorithms, where the number of consumed/produced tokens varies cyclically depending on the position of the window, also benefit
from the added expressiveness of CSDF [17].
Additionally, we annotate each actor with a firing time to model the delays of hardware computational units [6]. The formal definition of the resulting TCSDF is given in Definition 3.1.

3.4

The Buffets Storage Idiom

The Buffets storage idiom [29] provides efficient and composable data channels suitable for domainspecific accelerators. This principle has been used in modern single core [10] and multi-core [12]
DLAs.
A buffet is effectively a circular buffer with head and tail pointers defining a current active
window. The available capacity of the buffet is managed using a credit system. Buffets can be used
to connect a pair of producer and consumer nodes with four well-defined interfaces:
— Read: used by the consumer to read values at specific indices within the current window,
— Update: used by the consumer to modify data in-place,
— Fill: used by the producer to push data into the buffet,
— Shrink: used by the consumer to remove values from the buffet to effectively shrink the
active window and release credits.
Fills and shrinks update the the tail and head pointers, respectively. For reads and updates,
indexes relative to the head pointer are used, meaning the element at the head is always at index
zero. Additionally, buffets provide read/write synchronisation. While reading an index, it can be
marked for update, which blocks any further reads to that index until it is updated. This stalling
also occurs when reading an index outside the active window. This prevents Read After Write
(RAW) data hazards, making buffets easier to manage than standard scratchpad buffers. The ability
to read and update values in place makes them a more flexible alternative than conventional FIFO
buffers [18].

CIMFlow’s Hardware Architecture Model

The Hardware Architecture Model is a collection of compute and storage elements organised
hierarchically. CIM Processing Elements (PEs) are the elemental engine and can execute only
MVM operations. Several PEs are arranged into a CIM-Core. Within each core, input and output
buffets are instantiated. The CIM-Core also includes post-processing logic to compute activation
functions. The Digital cores handle non-MVM operations, such as addition and pooling.
Several CIM-Cores, digital cores and on-chip memory are composed into a Tile. The digital core
supports Single Instruction Multiple Data (SIMD), with a configurable vector size. The shared

6.2

Hardware-Aware Workload Transformations

A series of transformations are performed on the Workload Model so that it fits the dimensions
and execution patterns of the target hardware.
6.2.1 Weight Duplication. CIMFlow uses the weight duplication algorithm from CLSA-CIM [28].
This is a greedy iterative algorithm in which the layer with the lowest throughput is selected
for duplication in each iteration. If the PE budget allows, the selected layer is duplicated and the
throughput is updated. The process is repeated until there are not enough PEs left to duplicate any
layer.
6.2.2 Matrix Splitting. MVM tasks that do not fit into a single CIM-Core are split into sub-tasks.
Concatenation and addition nodes are inserted as required.
6.2.3 Vectorisation of Digital Tasks. The vectorisation of digital tasks, such as add and pooling
layers, is done based on the vector size of the SIMD unit. In this step, the repetition vectors and
ports of all digital tasks are modified but no new tasks are created.

6.2.4 Tensor Slicing. We establish the granularity for each array, called a tensor slice, and an
execution order for the actors. The tensor slice allows the model to track the produced and consumed
data without the cost of tracking each pixel individually. Moreover, the tensor slices defined in this
step set the granularity of the data exchange between tasks for the cross-layer inference execution.
6.2.5 Mapping. The workload is mapped to the target Hardware platform by assigning each task
to a core. In the current implementation of CIMFlow, a straightforward mapping is implemented
where all tasks are mapped to a set of cores within a single tile.
6.3

Supported Operands

In CIMFlow, MVM-based NN layers, namely 2D convolutions (including grouped, depth-wise, and
pointwise) and dense, are mapped to are mapped to CIM cores. The im2col transformation [33] is
used to convert these to MVM operations. Activation and batch normalisation operations are fused
to the convolution layers.
Additionally, the tool supports arithmetic layers such addition, average and max pooling and
tensor transformation layers including reshape, resize, and flatten. This is a key distinctive feature
of CIMFlow, compared to most SOTA tools [3, 27, 28], which only consider MVMs to compute
latency and energy.

CIMFlow’s Execution Model

Although possible, a direct simulation of the Workload Model is impractical as it requires tracking
the status of each multidimensional array over time. Instead, we reduce the complexity of the
simulation by transforming the Array-OL Workload Model into a TCSDF graph. In the following,
we describe three versions of the Execution Model, increasing the level of detail progressively.
7.1

Model with Unbounded Buffets

The simplest model uses abstract unbounded buffers. Each repetition task from the Workload Model
is converted into an SDF actor producing and consuming 𝐾𝑝 and 𝐾𝑐 tokens, respectively. 𝐾𝑝 and
𝐾𝑐 are scalars derived from the 𝑠𝑝𝑎𝑡𝑡𝑒𝑟𝑛 attribute of the Workload Model. The firing time 𝑡, the delay
between the instant the tokens become available on their input edge and the instant they produce
new tokens, represents the computation time. This delay is different for each type of task and
is calculated based on the parameters of the CIM PEs and the SIMD and pooling units from the
Hardware Architecture Model.
The unbounded buffet between two communicating tasks is modelled with three SDF actors.
These actors encapsulate the logic of the entire data production and consumption patterns with
two lists of rates:
— [Kw ] specifies the number of iterations from the producer that need to be executed to
produce the next tensor slice completely.
— [Kr ] specifies the amount of iterations from the consumer that can be executed given the
number of completely produced slices.
These lists ensure that data is correctly managed and transferred between different stages of the
computation. Figure 8 shows an example of a model of two tasks connected through an unbounded
buffet.
The model with unbounded buffets conveys the data dependency in the arrays [Kw ] and [Kr ]
and can be used to simulate the best-case scenario activation pattern of the tasks. However, this
model does not impose any bounds on the storage capacity of the buffet elements.

8.3

Case Study I - Re-evaluating Weight Duplication for FWS CIM Systems

Previous work [2, 28, 31] proposed duplicating certain weight filters to improve system throughput.
CIMFlow uses the weight duplication algorithm from CLSA-CIM [28]. This algorithm is greedy:
while there are PEs left, the layer with the lowest throughput is duplicated.
In this experiment, we sweep the extra PE budget from 0 to 64 and quantify the throughput gain,
normalised against the baseline from Table 2. The results are plotted in Figure 12, where orange
and blue colours are used for serial and cross-layer results, respectively. It is important to note that
the additional PEs are given in absolute numbers and do not have a homogeneous impact on the
total available PEs. For instance, for the smaller networks TinyYOLOv3 and ResNet18, adding 64
extra PEs almost doubles the total available PEs. Three simulation variants are presented regarding
how the data movements are modelled:
— no_dm: all memory delays are set to zero, effectively turning off the effect of data movements,
— buff_min: memory delays are on, and inter-layer buffet allocation is set to the minimum,
— buff_max: memory delays are on, and inter-layer buffet allocation is set to the maximum.
When disregarding data movements (no_dm), a steep throughput gain is observed for most
workloads. This contrasts with the more moderate increase observed when data movements are
taken into account. Moreover, the throughput gain from data movements appears to be saturating.
This phenomenon is particularly notable for AlexNet, which shows almost no gain after adding
32 extra PEs. The reason for this is the limited bandwidth between PEs and the tile buffet. For the
evaluated workloads, neglecting data movements when estimating the maximum throughput gain
from weight duplication leads to overestimations of up to 308%.

buffer allocation.

8.5

Future Work

8.5.1 Non-FWS CIM Dataflows. In this article, we focused on FWS systems, which have two
major constraints. First, the MVMs in the workload must involve one constant operand so that costly
re-writing operations are avoided [26]. Second, the FWS platform must have enough PEs to fit all
weights in the workload. Small to medium CNN, as the ones used in our case studies, fit well within
this constraint. However, in other novel neural networks, particularly transformers, the MVMs
often involve two dynamic operands. We plan to extend our tool to accept the reprogramming of
PEs, enabling dynamic operands and time-multiplexed mappings.
8.5.2 Extended Mapping for Multi-tile Platforms. The case studies presented in this work assume
a single-tile platform. However, mapping large networks across multiple hierarchical levels, such
as tiles, cores, and PEs, is an open research challenge. CIMFlow provides modular and extensible
models that will be highly valuable for exploring novel partitioning algorithms and mapping
strategies for multi-tile platforms.
8.5.3 Detailed HW Models. One of the strengths of TCSDF is its modularity and extendability.
These properties allow subsequent transformations in order to capture relevant details of the HW
platform [6]. The authors in [6] present interesting modelling primitives, including communication
via shared memories and TDMA based routers. We plan to integrate some of these patterns in our
execution model, enabling the comparison of alternative interconnect topologies.
9

Conclusion

In this work, we introduced CIMFlow, a modelling and simulation framework tailored for
multi-core cross-layer CIM systems and MVM-centric workloads. Our novel models enable the
characterisation of dataflow in FWS CIM DLAs, providing insights into performance bottlenecks
and buffer utilisation.
In our cases studies we revisited weight duplication as a strategy to increase the thoughput of CIM
systems, and showed how this technique can provide throughput gains as high as 52×. Additionally,
we show that neglecting data transfer costs results in overly optimistic throughput estimates, with
overestimations reaching up to 308%. Using CIMFlow, we presented a simple strategy to optimise
buffer allocation so that structural unbalances in the workload graph are compensated.
CIMFlow serves as a powerful cost model for architecture exploration, allowing designers to
fine-tune memory hierarchies based on real application demands. By providing an extensible
simulation infrastructure, CIMFlow lays the groundwork for further research into optimising CIM
architectures for deep learning workloads.
