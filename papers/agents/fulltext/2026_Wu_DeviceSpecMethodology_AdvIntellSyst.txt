ABSTRACT
Analog in-memory computing (AIMC) based on non-volatile memories (NVMs) offers a transformative pathway toward ultra-energyefficient deep learning, enabling high-speed, low-power inference with scalable performance. Recent progress has demonstrated
floating-point-level accuracy through hardware-aware training, highlighting AIMC’s potential to rival conventional digital architectures.
As NVM technologies for AIMC continue to advance, a systematic framework for defining device specification targets is critical for
balancing inference accuracy and fabrication cost. Key device parameters, such as memory window, read and program noise, and
conductance drift, directly influence inference fidelity across different time scales while simultaneously affecting manufacturability.
Their strong interdependence complicates optimization, as tuning one parameter can relax or tighten constraints on others. To address
this challenge, we propose a comprehensive methodology for mapping the multidimensional device specification space required to
achieve floating-point-equivalent accuracy, using phase-change memory (PCM) as a representative platform. Cross-model evaluations
on convolutional neural networks, recurrent neural networks, and transformer architectures reveal that device characteristics must be
co-optimized within this coupled landscape. The resulting analysis identifies families of specification sets that deliver comparable accuracy, enabling flexible, cost-effective hardware design and paving the way for scalable, commercially viable AIMC deployment.

1 | Introduction
As AI development advances into the era of large-scale models, the
traditional von Neumann architecture is increasingly limited by
the memory wall, constraining both energy efficiency and latency
in AI computing. Analog in-memory computing (AIMC), which
performs computation directly within memory arrays, offers a
potential path to overcoming this bottleneck. By reducing data
movement, AIMC can deliver significant improvements in energy
efficiency and throughput, making it attractive for both resourceconstrained edge devices and high-performance data centers [1, 2].
Recently, as a result of the rapid development of research and
development in this area, there is a trend of accelerated growth
of the memory industry for computing applications [3]. Floating
point accuracies have been achieved using the hardware-aware

training methods [4–12] in both simulation [9–13] and chip
demonstrations [6, 7]. However, the analog memory device
non-idealities, such as noise, drift, limited memory window,
etc., have hindered wide deployment of AIMC. It is highly desirable to mitigate the challenges of managing analog device nonidealities by defining specification targets that are both achievable
and capable of ensuring high accuracy. We developed a method to
do that in this work, by finding all the combinations of relevant
device characteristics that are able to achieve the floating point
accuracies. This allows the manufacturer to pick the device specification targets that are easiest to achieve to reduce the barrier of
device development and the cost of manufacturing.

mostly focus on individual non-ideality [6, 10, 13], such as the

We use phase change memory (PCM) as an example to illustrate
the method of finding all valid device specifications. Among the
various devices suitable for AIMC, PCM has received much
attention because of its favorable attributes [3, 14–17], including
analog conductance programing, appropriate resistance levels, a
robust memory window, multilevel resistance states, high endurance, relatively mature integration technology compared to other
emerging memory devices, and the ability to perform large-scale
matrix vector multiplications (MVMs). These advantages position PCM as an excellent device candidate for AIMC, particularly
for inference applications, which account for more than 80% of
the computational resources used in AI workflows [18]. Similar
to any other analog NVMs [19–21], PCM has its intrinsic nonidealities, such as read noise [12, 22, 23], device-to-device

adversely affect DNN inference accuracy. These are very typical
for analog NVM devices.
Our study starts with training three representative DNNs—
ResNet [28] (a convolutional neural network (CNN)-based architecture), LSTM [29] (an recurrent neural network (RNN)-based
architecture), and BERT [30] (a transformer-based architecture)—
to iso-accuracy through noise injection and related techniques as
described in Ref. [12]. We then program these HWA DNN weights
into simulated PCM devices. The PCM device drift and noise are
modeled with the PCM noise model [31], implemented via the
IBM Analog Hardware Acceleration Kit (AIHWKIT) [32–34].
By systematically sweeping a multi-dimensional parameter space
encompassing memory window, drift, and noise across multiple
inference timescales, we generate comprehensive evaluations
and complete device specification ranges capable of sustained
iso-accuracy. These results can be used as a guide to the selection
of memory window, drift, and noise parameters for a given inference time and performance target. Rather than relying on trialand-error or overly simplified assumptions, our approach offers
a guideline for identifying a viable range of device configurations
to support the development of more robust or more cost-effective
designs in analog in-memory computing.

2 | General Approach
The general approach of this work is illustrated in Figure 1. First
of all, we begin with training floating point (FP) DNN models to
achieve state-of-the-art accuracy by either using a pre-trained
model or training the model from scratch, depending on the
availability. These FP models are then mapped to conductance
values of NVM devices in the AIMC crossbar arrays using the
AIHWKIT [32–34] simulation framework. To mitigate the impact
of NVM non-idealities and obtain robust weight values for AIMC,
we apply the hardware-aware (HWA) training procedure [5, 12].
The HWA method incorporates device imperfections, such as
noise injection detailed in the Methods section, during the training
process. Here we use the PCM noise model [6, 12, 31] as an example. This training method enables the development of models that
achieve or approximate iso-accuracy (within 1% of the floatingpoint reference) on AIMC systems. Such models can then serve

combined influence of multiple non-idealities, which are interdependent with one another. The method of investigating one
parameter at a time has intrinsic limitations. The collective
impact of various device characteristics is complex and not easily
predictable. In addition, the impact of these device characteristics
is usually time-dependent. What may appear optimal for short
periods after programing does not necessarily hold for later
times. For instance, mitigating drift or noise alone may fail to
preserve model accuracy over extended durations if the memory
window of this type of device is limited. Ensuring an optimal and
reasonable device design requires more holistic design strategies.
In this paper, we explore the collective impact of all relevant factors, including memory window, conductance drift, read noise,
and inference time, on the inference performance of Deep
Neural Networks (DNNs). We consider how these factors evolve
and interrelate over time under all possible configurations. It
should be noted that the device specification to maintain good
accuracy is not unique. It is difficult to assert that any single
device configuration is unequivocally better than another without considering the target application’s requirements and the
constraints on device characteristics. However, some configurations might be more preferred due to various reasons such as
design considerations or material and fabrication-related reasons. Our method presents all possible combinations of device
characteristics that achieve floating-point accuracy while allowing flexibility in selecting from different device manufacturers.

After obtaining the robust weight values in conductance for
AIMC to achieve iso accuracy, we conduct the inference study
using the AIHWKIT, which provides a state-of-the-art statistical
model for NVM arrays, including PCMs. This statistical model is
used when performing inference to simulate the various sources
of non-idealities that are present in a real hardware [31]. The
inference model is calibrated based on extensive measurements
performed on an array containing 1 million PCM devices fabricated at IBM [6]. The specifications of the PCM devices used for
calibration are summarized in Table S1. During the inference
study, the PCM noises and drift are incorporated using statistical
models across the NVM array. The DNN and datasets we used
include Image classification on CIFAR-10 [35] for ResNet-32,
language processing tasks using SQuAD v1.1 [36] and GLUE
(MRPC) [37] datasets for the BERT model, and word-level prediction using the Penn Treebank (PTB) [38] dataset for the LSTM.
The device characteristics used in the study are defined as follows. The memory window is defined as the linear programing
range, gmax−gmin, where gmin denotes the minimal conductance
(i.e., the value that the logical zero of the weights will be mapped
to) and gmax denotes the maximal conductance (i.e., the value
that the absolute max of the weights will be mapped to). The
noise scale and drift scale are defined as scaling factors compared
to the AIHWKIT default device model, which was obtained from
the statistical data from PCM devices [6, 12, 31]. The noise scale is
applied to both the programing noise and read noise, since the
programing noise is fundamentally limited by the read noise in
the case of iterative programing [6]. Details of the PCM statistical
device model are provided in the Methods section, and the corresponding base parameters are summarized in Table S2.

options of analog memory device specifications on representative

characteristics in a multidimensional device specification space,
which are capable of maintaining iso-accuracy, i.e., within 1% of
the floating point accuracy. These results provide a practical reference for setting target specifications for analog in-memory
computing devices and directions for device optimization.

7 | Method
7.1 | Hardware-Aware (HWA) Training
It has been shown that hardware-aware (HWA) training in software is crucial for improving accuracy for analog inference
[4–12]. In our standard HWA training method, we use the same
methods as described in [5, 11, 12]. In brief, non-idealities such as
input and output quantization, range clippings, and output noise
are used in the forward pass, but not in the backward pass, while
performing additional stochastic gradient descent (SGD) training
to make the model more robust against analog non-idealities. As
in [5, 11, 12], we also inject weight noise of PCM characteristics
during programing [6], which reversibly modifies the weight
matrix for each mini-batch during forward and backward. We
consider the learning rates and the strength-scale of the injected
weight noise hyper-parameters, and thus scanned those to
achieve good training performance with noise (learning rates
scanned: from 0.005 to 0.5, weight noise scale scanned: from
0.2 to 5). After hardware-aware-training, converged models are
subject to PCM programing noise and drift during inference as
described below. To avoid over-fitting in the hyper-parameter
search, we trained each parameter setting with 5 different initial
conditions and took the model after the same number of training
epochs for inference and averaged results. The training results are
averaged over 3 trials. For the inference simulations, results are
averaged over 25 trials. For LSTM and ResNet experiments, we
additionally average across multiple independently trained models, resulting in smoother and more consistent accuracy maps.
For ResNet and BERT, we apply HWA training directly on pretrained FP models. In contrast, the LSTM model is trained from
scratch, including an initial FP training phase. Training details
can be obtained from Table S3.

7.2 | Weight Programing
We utilize a previously established and well-characterized weight
programing drift model for PCM devices [6, 12, 31], as outlined in
the following sections. In this model, each weight is represented
by a pair of conductances in the crossbar, with the first conductance programed between reset (0) and a maximum conductance

gmax ) to handle positive weights, and the second conductance

process of computing matrix-vector multiplications (MVM) by
using signed conductances.
