Abstract— To enable energy-efficient computation for deep
neural networks (DNNs) at edge, computing-in-memory (CIM)
is proposed to reduce the energy costs during intense off-chip
memory access. However, CIM is prone to multiply-accumulate
(MAC) errors due to non-idealities of memory crossbars and
peripheral circuits, which severely degrade the accuracy of DNNs.
In this work, we propose a Data-Driven Non-ideality Aware
Training (D-NAT) framework to compensate for the accuracy
degradation. The proposed D-NAT framework has the following
contributions: 1) We measured a fabricated SRAM-based CIM
macro to obtain a data-driven MAC error model (D-MAC-EM).
Based on the derived D-MAC-EM, we analyze the impact of the
non-idealities on DNN’s accuracy. 2) To make DNNs robust to
the non-idealities of CIM macros, we incorporate the measured
D-MAC-EM into DNN’s training procedure. Moreover, we propose a statistical training mechanism to better estimate the
gradients of the discrete D-MAC-EM. 3) We investigate trade-offs
between quantization range and quantization errors. To mitigate
the quantization errors in activations, we introduce extended
PACT (E-PACT) that adaptively learns the upper and lower
bounds of input activations for each layer. Simulation results
show that our proposed D-NAT improves the accuracy of
ResNet20, VGG8, ResNet34, and VGG16 by 78.98%, 71.8%,
72.04%, and 57.85%, respectively, which reaches the ideal upper
bound of the quantized model. Lastly, the D-NAT framework is
validated on an FPGA platform with the fabricated SRAM-based
CIM macro chip. Based on the measurement results, D-NAT
successfully recovers the accuracy under non-idealities of a real
SRAM-based CIM macro.

I. I NTRODUCTION

D

EEP neural networks (DNNs) have demonstrated
overwhelming success in various machine learning applications, including image processing [2], language translation [3], and speech recognition [4]. However, intense data
movements between computational units and off-chip memories pose a significant challenge to conventional accelerators
based on the von-Neumann architecture. Recently, computingin-memory (CIM) [5], [6] mechanism has been proposed
as a promising solution to the von-Neumann bottleneck
through the integration of computational units and memories.
CIM-based accelerators can parallelly perform multiplyaccumulate (MAC) operations, the dominant operation in
DNNs, in the analog domain within a memory crossbar.
Therefore, the energy efficiency is significantly improved by
minimizing the costs of data movements.
However, CIM-based accelerators suffer from the reliability
issue of analog computation [7]–[12]. Since outputs of the
memory crossbar are sensed as a summation of currents
through memory cells, following Kirchhoff’s current law,
they are prone to MAC errors due to the non-ideal behavior
of the memory crossbar. Moreover, peripheral circuits such
as digital-to-analog converters (DACs) and analog-to-digital
converters (ADCs) also cause deviations in the computed
values and severely degrade the accuracy of DNN.
Many works have been proposed to address the reliability
issue of CIM. After our examination, they can be categorized
as 1) Hardware-based calibration methods, and 2) Analytical
non-ideality aware training (A-NAT) methods:
1) In the hardware-based calibration methods, dedicated
hardware is responsible for compensating for the nonidealities. In [13], the authors proposed a compact
row-by-row ADC with replica bitcells for mitigating the non-linearity and process variations. In [16],
a customized metal-oxide-metal (MOM) capacitor was
applied to minimize the variations of the stored data. The
body bias tuning technique for PMOS/NMOS mismatch

MRAM/ReRAM-based CIM. However, most variation

none of them apply their methods to SRAM-based CIM
macros.
To solve the above issues, this paper establishes a datadriven non-ideality aware training (D-NAT) framework for
non-ideal CIM macros. “Data-driven” indicates that D-NAT
utilizes the measured data that has been influenced by the
non-idealities of the CIM macro to train an error-aware model.
The overall D-NAT framework is depicted in Fig. 1. The main
contributions of this work are as follows:
1) Investigation of the major differences between
the pre-defined A-MAC-EM and the practical
D-MAC-EM: We set up an FPGA platform to measure a
fabricated SRAM-based CIM macro [1]. By comparing

theoretical outputs with measured outputs, we obtain
a data-driven MAC error model (D-MAC-EM) for
the following statistical training process. Furthermore,
we investigate the difference between the measured
D-MAC-EM and a conventional analytical MAC error
model (A-MAC-EM) with simulated variations. The
experimental results indicate that A-MAC-EM is insufficient to represent the non-idealities of a practical
SRAM-based CIM macro, highlighting the importance
of validation on a real fabricated chip.
2) Statistical training mechanism: Since D-MAC-EM
is a sequence of discrete points with a probabilistic
distribution, we propose a statistical training mechanism
to better estimate the gradient of each point. Specifically,
we first compute the expected value for each point
with the probabilistic distribution. The gradient of each
point is estimated as the slope of the piece-wise linear
curve between two adjacent points. Compared with
straight-through-estimator (STE) [24], which does not
consider the statistical characteristics of the measured
D-MAC-EM, the proposed statistical training mechanism improves the training speed and the accuracy of
ResNet20 and VGG8 by 0.55% and 1.3%, respectively.
3) Extended PACT (E-PACT): We first investigate the
trade-offs between quantization range and quantization
errors for a quantized model. Based on the observation,
we introduce E-PACT to automatedly determine a quantization range for each layer. E-PACT adaptively learns
the quantization range by parameterizing the upper
bounds and the lower bounds of input activations. Compared with clipped ReLU [3], E-PACT clips the input
activations within a more proper range by considering
the information of negative values. Experimental results
show that E-PACT improves the accuracy of ResNet20
and VGG8 by 1.1% ∼ 30.1% and 0.51% ∼ 12.06%,
respectively.
4) Validation of D-NAT trained model on a fabricated CIM Macro: We validate the proposed D-NAT
framework on the FPGA platform with the fabricated
SRAM-based CIM macro [1] for on-chip inference. The
measurement results show that the accuracy of VGG8
is enhanced from 14.17% to 87.17% without on-chip
calibration. Overall, D-NAT shows its superiority by

II. P RELIMINARIES
A. Fabricated SRAM-Based CIM Macro Used in This
Work [1]
In [1], the authors implemented an SRAM-based CIM
macro with a unified charge-processing network (UCPN),
and the proposed D-NAT will be derived based on our
observations and measurements of this fabricated CIM macro.
Fig. 2 shows the overall architecture of the CIM macro,
which includes 64 7-bit dynamic current-steering DACs,
16 computation banks, and a UCPN that offers the capability
of data conversion equivalent to 16 7-bit ADCs with a
maximum filter depth of 64. Each computation bank consists
of 64 local computing units (LCU) responsible for weight
storing and MAC operations.
During computation, DACs first convert 7-bit digital input
activations into corresponding analog voltages for pre-charging
a computation bank. The multiplication operations are performed between voltages on bit lines and weights in SRAM

In this part, we summarize the methodology of conventional A-NAT. For ReRAM-based CIM macros, A-NAT first
analytically models the non-idealities such as wire resistance
and imperfect write variations. The software training flow
consists of the following three steps: quantization, conversion,
and retraining. During the quantization phase, A-NAT quantizes weights and activations into fixed-point numbers, which
matches the precision of the ReRAM-based CIM macro. The
quantized model is mapped to the memory crossbars in the
conversion phase, which converts the activations to voltage
and weights to conductance. In the retraining phase, A-NAT
forward propagates input data with the analytical model to
obtain MAC errors generated from the non-idealities. After
that, backward propagation, gradient computation, and weight
updating are applied to the floating-point model to compensate
for the non-idealities.
While A-NAT achieves accuracy improvements under the
non-ideal ReRAM-based CIM, the following issues remain
open:
1) Modeling practical non-ideal effects with analytical
models: During the training process, A-NAT uses a
gaussian distribution to analytically model the devicelevel non-idealities. However, modeling all the variations
in the CIM macro is “impractical”. On the other hand,
the analytical model is not consistent with a practical
CIM circuit. Therefore, the non-ideality-aware trained
DNN may still suffer from accuracy degradation while
deploying on a real fabricated chip.
2) Estimating the gradients of the MAC error model
with an identity function: Quantization-aware training
(QAT) [21] is proposed to enhance accuracy by considering quantization errors during the training process.
To compute the gradients of the discrete quantized function, STE [24] is widely used to estimate the gradients
with an identity function. However, the quantization
function is not linear considering the non-ideal effects
of CIM. The mismatch of the gradients and the forward
function degrades DNN’s accuracy and reduces the
convergence speed of the training process.

A. Strategy for Mapping DNNs Onto SRAM-Based CIM
Macro
In this work, VGG8 and the CIFAR-10 dataset [27] are
used to demonstrate the functionality of the SRAM-based CIM

macro. The network structure of VGG8 includes 5 convolution
layers and 3 fully connected layer. Each convolution layer is
followed by a rectified linear unit (ReLU) layer and a batch
normalization layer. Specifically, only the middle 4 convolution layers are computed in the SRAM-based CIM macro.
The mapping example of the convolution layer 2 to the
SRAM-based CIM is shown in Fig. 4. The convolution layer 2
has 64 input channels and 128 output channels, and its
filter size is 3 × 3. In the SRAM-based CIM macro, each
bank has 16 columns to store the weights, each column has
64 bitcells. Hence, 8 banks are required for computing the
convolution layer 2 with 128 output channels. In this work,
four channels in the same kernel are allocated on the same
column, and the remained bitcells are stored with data “0”.
Therefore, 16 times of re-loading weights into the banks are
required to compute kernels with 64 channels. During the
computation, the SRAM-based CIM macro needs 16 cycles
to switch 16 weight columns in each bank to compute each
partial sum. The 8 banks in the SRAM-based CIM macro can
operate parallelly to generate the partial sums of the output
feature map.

Based on the observations, despite the fact that prior
A-MAC-EMs are effective in their works, they fail in the
practical situation. The main reason is that they only verify
their methods through software simulations with the testing
environment constructed similarly to the training flow. However, it is impossible to completely model all the factors in
the practical situation. Therefore, applying the A-MAC-EM to
the practical testing data (measured from the fabricated chip)
is not effective in this work. In conclusion, we have shown
that the traditional A-MAC-EM is not enough for correcting
the MAC computation errors generated from the SRAM-based
CIM macro. Instead, the D-MAC-EM is more coherent to the
non-idealities of the SRAM-based CIM macro and is required
for correct prediction.

A. Overview of NAT Mechanism
The computation flow of the proposed NAT is summarized
in Algorithm 1. Since each layer is processed sequentially,
we use the j t h layer as an example. The training flow of
the j t h layer is depicted in Fig. 8. First, the floating-point
input activations A j −1 are clipped within a range of [β j , α j ]
by E-PACT. The clipped activations A j −1cli p are transferred
into the forward function, which consists of the following
steps:
1. Split the activations and the weights into several tiles to
fit the size of the memory crossbars, denoted as cs.
2. Quantize the activations and the weights with the configurations of the CIM macro, where the scaling factors,
s fweight and s fact ivat ion , convert the weights and the
activations between floating-point and fixed-point.
3. Perform matrix-vector multiplications (MVMs) with the
quantized activations and weights to obtain partial sums,
which are further truncated to fit the resolution of ADCs.
4. The ideal partial sums A jideal are transformed to the
non-ideal measured values based on the D-MAC-EM.
5. The measured partial sums A jerror are aggregated and
converted back to the floating-point numbers for the next
layer.
After forward passing all the layers, the gradients of model
weights and clipping ranges are computed by the statistical
training mechanism and E-PACT, respectively. The details of
the statistical training mechanism and E-PACT are elaborated
in the following subsections.
B. Statistical Training Mechanism
1) Challenges of Training With D-MAC-EM: To mitigate
the non-ideal effects of CIM, we train DNNs with the D-MACEM obtained from the measurement results of a practical CIM.
One major challenge of training with a discrete function is that
derivatives of the function are zero everywhere. This causes
the gradients to be zero and prevents updating the DNN model.
The technique of straight-through-estimator (STE) [24] is
widely implemented in discrete optimization using stochastic
gradient descent (SGD) due to its effectiveness and simplicity.
STE estimates the gradients of a discrete function as an
identity function, which means STE directly bypasses the
incoming gradients to the preceding layer. However, when considering the non-ideal D-MAC-EM, the forward propagation
is a non-linear curve with different probability distributions.
Simply using STE to estimate the gradients during the back
propagation degrades the effectiveness of the training process.
Therefore, the gradients of training the model with such
D-MAC-EM need to be re-design.

VII. C ONCLUSION
In this paper, we present a data-driven non-ideality aware
training (D-NAT) framework to compensate for the non-ideal
effects of analog computing in CIM. We observe the deficiency
of the analytical MAC error model (A-MAC-EM) compared
with the data-driven MAC error model (D-MAC-EM). The
proposed statistical training mechanism shows a more stable
and faster convergence speed with 0.55% and 1.3% accuracy improvement on ResNet20 and VGG8 compared with
STE. The proposed E-PACT provides a 1.1% ∼ 30.1%
and 0.51% ∼ 12.06% accuracy improvement on ResNet20
and VGG8 compared with other quantization methods. The
overall simulation results show that our proposed D-NAT
improves the accuracy of ResNet20, VGG8, ResNet34, and
VGG16 by 78.98%, 71.8%, 72.04%, and 57.85%, respectively.
Finally, we validate the D-NAT trained model on the fabricated
SRAM-based CIM chip. The measurement results show that
our proposed D-NAT successfully recovers the accuracy of
VGG8 from 14.17% to 87.17% without additional hardware
circuits.
The proposed D-NAT mechanism can serve as the reference
design for other types of CIM macros based on emerging
memories, as long as they have enough measurement results
of the fabricated CIM macros.

Deputy General Director of the SoC Technology Center (STC), Industrial
Technology Research Institute (ITRI), Hsinchu, Taiwan. His research interests
include VLSI architectures for signal processing and communications and
adaptive/multi-rate signal processing. He has published more than 250 refereed
journals and conference papers in the above research areas, together with five
book chapters and 20 granted U.S. patents. He was elevated to IEEE Fellow
for his contributions to DSP algorithms and VLSI designs for communication
IC/SoC in 2015.
